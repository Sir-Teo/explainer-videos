## Part 1: Arrows that add

_Scene `s02_amplitudes.py::Amplitudes`_

Let's go back to the double slit, and do it more carefully, using the simulation from the start of the video. First, close the bottom slit. Electrons arriving at the screen pile up in one broad hump. Call that distribution P one.

Now close the top slit instead, and open the bottom one: another hump, P two. Common sense says that with both slits open, each electron goes through one slit or the other, so the two distributions should simply add.

That's not what happens. With both slits open, the distribution has stripes. And look at the dark places: there, with either slit alone, plenty of electrons arrive. Open both, and almost none do. Adding a second way to get somewhere can make it impossible to get there. Probabilities don't add.

Here's the rule that does work. Each way of getting from the source to a point on the screen contributes an arrow: a complex number called an amplitude. Add the arrows tip to tail, and the probability is the length of the total, squared.

Now change the angle between the two arrows. When they point the same way, the total is twice as long, and the probability is four times what one slit gives. When they point in opposite directions, they cancel, and the probability is zero. On average you get the sum of the two separate probabilities, but at any one point, you can get anything from zero to double that.

Let's see exactly why. The length squared of a complex number is the number times its complex conjugate. Multiply out: you get the two separate probabilities, plus two cross terms. The cross terms are conjugates of each other, so together they're twice the real part. Write each arrow as a length times e to the i phi, and the cross term becomes a cosine of the angle between them.

The first two terms are what you'd get if probabilities added. The last one is new. It's called the interference term, and because a cosine can be negative, it can take probability away. That's the whole difference: in quantum mechanics, amplitudes add, and you square at the end.

So where do the angles come from? Feynman's picture: each amplitude is like the hand of a tiny clock that the electron carries along its path. The hand turns one full revolution for every wavelength traveled. Two paths of different lengths arrive with their hands turned by different amounts.

In symbols: a path of length r turns the hand by two pi r over lambda. So the angle between the two arrows is two pi times the difference in path lengths, in wavelengths. When the screen is far away, that difference is d times y over L. The arrows line up, and the screen is bright, whenever the difference is a whole number of wavelengths: at heights m lambda L over d. And the wavelength itself comes from de Broglie: lambda equals h over the momentum. For electrons accelerated through fifty kilovolts, that's about five picometers.

Let's check that against the simulation. Its wavelength is 8.38 units, the slits are 60 apart, and the screen is 512 away. So the fringes should be 71.5 units apart. Measured from the simulated pattern: 71.0. And the dark fringes sit exactly where the two paths differ by a half-integer number of wavelengths.

Two slits, two paths, two arrows. But an electron isn't limited to two options. In general there's an amplitude for the electron to be at every point in space, and that's what we turn to next.

## A complex number at every point

_Scene `s03_wavefunction.py::Wavefunction`_

In the double slit, each path contributed one arrow, one complex number. Now let an electron be anywhere on a line. Quantum mechanics assigns it an arrow at every point x: a complex number whose length and direction can change from place to place. That assignment, x goes to a complex number, is the wavefunction, psi of x.

If you have seen a quantum state drawn as an arrow with a few components, this is the same idea, with one component for every point on the line. Two things vary along x: how long each arrow is, and which way it points.

To see every arrow at once, stand each one up in its own complex plane, perpendicular to the x axis, and connect their tips. The wavefunction becomes a curve in three dimensions: here, a helix that swells and fades.

Now look at it from the front. All you see is the real part: the wiggly curve you find in most textbooks. But that's only a shadow. Turn it a quarter turn and you see the imaginary part, the same wiggle shifted by a quarter of a cycle. Neither is more real than the other.

And looking straight down the x axis, every arrow lands in one complex plane: different lengths, pointing every which way. Both views matter. To keep both in a flat picture, we need one more idea.

Here's the idea: replace the direction of each arrow with a color. Pointing right is red, straight up is yellow-green, left is cyan, down is violet, and everything in between blends smoothly around the wheel. The angle of a complex number is called its phase, so color will mean phase.

So here is the same wavefunction, flattened. The height is the length of each arrow, and the color is its direction. The bands of color cycling along x are the helix's turns. One more change, and we'll keep this convention for the rest of the video: from now on, the height shows the length squared, for a reason we're about to see.

What does the wavefunction mean? Max Born's answer, from 1926: the probability of finding the electron between a and b is the integral of the length squared, the absolute value of psi, squared. For this wavefunction and this interval, that's 0.556. And since the electron is somewhere, the total area must be one.

So the wavefunction doesn't say where the electron is. It says where it might be found. If we prepared this same state over and over and looked, the places we'd find it would pile up where the height is large, and never where it's zero. These dots aren't a path the electron follows; each is just one possible outcome.

Notice what this rule throws away. Multiply the whole wavefunction by the same phase factor, e to the i alpha. Every arrow turns by the same angle, so every color shifts together, but every length stays the same, so every probability stays the same. No experiment can ever detect this overall phase. It's pure bookkeeping.

But the phase is far from meaningless when it changes from place to place. Here are three wavefunctions with exactly the same probability density. The only difference is how the colors cycle: one winds forward, one doesn't wind at all, one winds backward. Let's let them evolve, by the equation we're about to build.

They fly apart. The first moves right, the second stays put and spreads, the third moves left. The rate at which the phase winds, radians per unit length, is called the wave number, k, and de Broglie's rule says the momentum is h-bar times k. Momentum is written in the phase.

The purest case is a wave with a single k: a perfect helix, e to the i k x. Its density is the same everywhere, and its momentum is exactly h-bar k. But that means the electron could be anywhere at all. Sharp momentum, no position. Keep that tension in mind; it's coming back as the uncertainty principle. First, though: how do these arrows move?

## Part 2: Building the Schrödinger equation

_Scene `s04_schrodinger.py::SchrodingerEquation`_

So a quantum state is a complex number at every point. How does it change in time? Whatever the rule is, it has to do three things. It must be linear: if two wavefunctions are allowed, so is their sum, because that's where interference comes from. It should be first order in time, so the wavefunction now determines the wavefunction later. And it must keep the total probability equal to one, forever.

Here's the most direct route, the one most textbooks take. Start with the simplest wavefunction of all, a plane wave, e to the i k x minus omega t: a helix that spins as time goes on. We know two facts about it from experiment: its energy is h-bar omega, the Planck-Einstein relation, and its momentum is h-bar k, de Broglie's.

Now differentiate. A time derivative brings down a factor of minus i omega, so i h-bar times d psi d t is h-bar omega psi: the energy times psi. A space derivative brings down i k, so minus i h-bar d psi d x is the momentum times psi. Do it twice, and minus h-bar squared times the second derivative is the momentum squared times psi.

For a particle, energy is kinetic plus potential: p squared over two m, plus V of x. Replace each side by what it does to psi, and out comes the Schrödinger equation: i h-bar d psi d t equals minus h-bar squared over two m times the second derivative, plus V psi.

Notice what happened: momentum became an operation, minus i h-bar d by d x, and the energy became an operator called the Hamiltonian, H. The equation is just i h-bar d psi d t equals H psi. This isn't a proof. Plane waves made the guess; linearity extends it to every superposition of plane waves; and a century of experiments confirms it.

Why does i appear at all? Couldn't psi just be a real wave? Try it. Add a real wave moving right to one moving left. The sum is a standing wave, two sine k x cosine omega t, and a quarter of a period later it is zero everywhere, at the same instant. If psi squared were a probability, the particle would have vanished.

Now do the same with complex waves. The sum is two cosine k x times e to the minus i omega t. The overall factor only turns every arrow together, so the probability, four cosine squared k x, never changes and never vanishes. The colors cycle; the density stands still.

Here's another way to see what the i does. Strip the Schrödinger equation down to a free particle, in units where h-bar and m are one. Without the i, it would be the heat equation, the rule for how temperature smooths out. Start both from the same bump and let them run.

Both spread. But heat flattens and fades: the bump loses half its size, and you can never run it backward to recover the original. The Schrödinger version spreads too, but its size, the total probability, stays at exactly one hundred percent. Instead of fading, the shape is stored in the phases, the color pattern, and the evolution can always be undone. The i turns decay into rotation.

Rearranged, the equation says the rate of change of psi is minus i over h-bar times H psi. Read that geometrically. For a plane wave, H psi is just the energy times psi: an arrow pointing the same way. Multiplying by minus i turns it a quarter turn clockwise. So the velocity of the arrow is always perpendicular to the arrow itself, and an arrow whose velocity is always perpendicular to it just goes around in a circle, at angular speed E over h-bar, never changing length.

For a general wavefunction, H psi isn't parallel to psi, because the second-derivative term depends on how the wave curves. Each arrow is pushed sideways by its neighbors, and that's what makes waves travel, spread and interfere. But the quarter turn is always there, and it's what will keep the total probability fixed.

Let's prove that. How fast does the probability density change at one point? By the product rule, it's psi star times d psi d t, plus psi times d psi star d t. Use the Schrödinger equation for each time derivative. The potential terms cancel each other, as long as V is real, and what's left is a perfect derivative with respect to x.

That's a continuity equation, the same law that says fluid can't appear or disappear, only flow. The quantity flowing is the probability current, j, h-bar over m times the imaginary part of psi star psi prime. Probability only moves around; it is never created or destroyed.

Here's a moving packet with its current drawn underneath. The current pushes probability forward at the front, it drains out at the back, and the total stays at exactly one: here, to six decimal places, the whole time.

## Wave packets: why a free particle spreads

_Scene `s05_free_particle.py::FreeParticle`_

A single plane wave has a perfectly definite momentum, but it's spread over all of space. To describe a particle that's somewhere, add plane waves together. Take wave numbers clustered around one value, with weights phi of k. One wave alone is flat. Add a few neighbors, and they reinforce in the middle and cancel elsewhere.

With more and more components, the cancellation away from the center becomes complete, and we get a wave packet: a lump of probability with a hue that cycles at the central wave number. In the limit it's an integral, a Fourier transform. A narrow spread of k gives a wide packet; a wide spread gives a narrow one.

Now let each component move. Put a single plane wave into the Schrödinger equation with no potential, and it works only if h-bar omega equals h-bar squared k squared over two m. So the frequency grows like the square of the wave number. This is the free particle's dispersion relation, and it means different components travel at different speeds.

There are two speeds here. A single crest moves at omega over k: h-bar k over two m. But the lump, where the waves reinforce, moves at d omega d k: h-bar k over m, which is p over m, exactly the classical velocity. Watch two plane waves: the bright beats glide along at the group velocity, while the colors inside them crawl at half that speed.

Now the real thing: a Gaussian packet, evolved exactly. The dashed line marks its center, moving at the group velocity. The white dot rides on one crest, one particular color. It moves too, but at half the speed, so the colors slip backward through the packet. The particle goes where the group goes.

And the packet spreads. Its momentum content, on the right, never changes at all: a free particle keeps its momentum. The faster components simply run ahead and the slower ones fall behind. You can see it in the colors: the hue cycles faster at the front.

How fast? A packet of width sigma zero has a momentum spread of h-bar over two sigma zero, a fact we'll derive with the uncertainty principle. Divide by the mass to get a spread of velocities, and at late times the width grows like that velocity spread times t. The exact law, from completing the square in the Fourier integral, is sigma zero times the square root of one plus h-bar t over two m sigma zero squared, all squared. The simulation sits right on it, to one part in ten thousand.

The time scale for spreading is two m sigma zero squared over h-bar, so it depends enormously on mass. An electron squeezed into one angstrom, about the size of an atom, has spread to six angstroms after a single femtosecond. A one-microgram grain of dust, localized to a micron, would take about a million years just to double its width. That's one reason the everyday world looks so classical.

## A particle in a box: where quantization comes from

_Scene `s06_box.py::Box`_

Now let's put the equation to work. A standard trick: look for solutions that are a function of space times a function of time. Substitute, and divide by phi times T. The left side depends only on time, the right side only on position, so both must equal the same constant. Call it E.

The time part is just e to the minus i E t over h-bar: a clock, turning at a rate set by the energy. The space part must satisfy H phi equals E phi. That's an eigenvalue problem: find the functions that H merely rescales, and the factors E. For these special solutions the probability density never changes, because a phase factor has length one. They're called stationary states.

The simplest place to try it: a particle trapped in a box of length L, with walls it can never cross. Inside, there's no potential, so H phi equals E phi says phi double prime is minus two m E over h-bar squared times phi. At the walls the wavefunction must vanish. So start at the left wall at zero, pick a trial energy, and integrate across the box.

For most energies, the wave arrives at the right wall at some nonzero height, and the boundary condition fails. Now sweep the energy upward, and track where the wave ends. Only at special energies does it land exactly on zero: whole numbers of half wavelengths fitting in the box, like the notes of a guitar string. That's where quantization comes from: not from a rule imposed by hand, but from a wave that must fit.

The allowed solutions are sine waves with n half-wavelengths in the box, and their energies grow like n squared: one, four, nine, sixteen times the lowest. Here's the ladder of allowed energies, and the wave that belongs to each rung. The lowest energy isn't zero: a confined particle can never be perfectly at rest. For an electron in a box one nanometer wide, the lowest energy is 0.376 electron volts.

Here are the first three, evolving in time. The density of each never changes. Only the colors turn, the whole wave at once, at rates proportional to the energy: the second turns four times faster than the first, the third nine times faster.

Stationary doesn't mean the particle is sitting still. Measure the momentum of the second state and you'll find it's sharply peaked near plus or minus two pi h-bar over L: a standing wave is a sum of a wave moving right and one moving left. Their currents cancel exactly, so nothing flows, but the momentum is anything but zero.

Things get interesting when we add two stationary states. Each part keeps turning at its own rate, so the angle between the two clocks keeps changing, and the two parts interfere: constructively on the left, then on the right, then on the left again. The probability sloshes back and forth.

The algebra says the same. Square the sum, and alongside the two static densities there's a cross term oscillating at the difference of the two energies, divided by h-bar. So the average position swings back and forth with amplitude sixteen L over nine pi squared, about eighteen percent of the box. For an electron in a nanometer box, that's one swing every 3.67 femtoseconds.

Remember this: a superposition of two energies oscillates at the difference frequency, E two minus E one over h-bar. These are called Bohr frequencies, and when we reach the hydrogen atom they'll turn out to be the colors of the light it emits.

## Quantum carpets: the wave that reassembles itself

_Scene `s07_carpet.py::Carpet`_

Let's push that idea further. Put a narrow packet in the box, off center. Like any wavefunction in the box, it's a sum of the stationary states, with coefficients c n. Here are their sizes squared. Every term carries its own clock, and the clocks run at rates proportional to n squared: one, four, nine, sixteen, and so on.

Give it a push to the right and let it go. At first it behaves like a ball: it moves, spreads a little, bounces off the wall and comes back. But the clocks drift out of step, and within a couple of bounces the packet has smeared into a tangle of ripples filling the whole box. It looks like the information about where it started is gone for good.

To see the whole story at once, stack the snapshots: x across, time going down, brightness for the probability. We'll weave it one row at a time.

The early zigzag is the bouncing packet. Then it dissolves into a fine weave, crossed by straight dark channels... but look at a quarter of the way through: two clean packets, one where we started and one at the mirror-image spot. At half the period, a single packet again, perfectly mirrored. And at the end of the period, the original packet, exactly as it began. Then the whole pattern repeats forever.

Nothing here was arranged by hand. This intricate pattern, called a quantum carpet, is just three hundred sine waves, each turning at a rate proportional to n squared. It's the same mathematics as an optical effect Henry Fox Talbot noticed in 1836, when light passes through a grating.

Why does it come back? Each clock turns at n squared times the slowest rate. After one full turn of the slowest clock, the n-th clock has made exactly n squared turns. Every n squared is a whole number, so every clock is back where it started, and so is the wavefunction. The revival time is two pi h-bar over E one: four m L squared over pi h-bar.

At half the period, each clock is at plus or minus one, depending on whether n is even or odd, and that sign pattern is exactly what reflects a sine series about the middle of the box: a mirror image. At a quarter period the even and odd terms pick up different phases, and the wave splits into the packet plus its mirror image, each with half the probability: a particle in a superposition of two places, produced by nothing but waiting. For an electron in a one-nanometer box, the full revival takes eleven femtoseconds.

## Tunneling through a wall

_Scene `s08_tunneling.py::Tunneling`_

Now a question classical physics answers instantly. A particle with energy E approaches a wall of potential energy V zero, higher than E. It doesn't have enough energy to be inside the wall at all, so it must turn around. Every time.

Quantum mechanically, look at the energy equation inside the barrier, where V zero is bigger than E. Now the second derivative has the same sign as phi itself, so the solutions aren't waves at all. They're growing and decaying exponentials, with a decay rate kappa set by how far the energy falls short.

So here's the exact solution for a steady stream of particles coming from the left. Inside the barrier, the wave decays exponentially, but it doesn't reach zero before the far side. At each edge, the pieces must join smoothly: the wave and its slope continuous. So on the far side, a smaller wave carries on. On the near side, the reflected wave interferes with the incoming one, making these ripples.

Matching the pieces at both edges gives four linear equations, and solving them gives the transmission probability exactly. It involves a hyperbolic sine of kappa times the width. For a thick barrier that's dominated by a single factor: e to the minus two kappa a. Exponential in the width, and in the square root of the energy deficit.

Here's the whole curve. Below the top of the barrier, transmission is small but never zero. Above the top, where a classical particle would always get through, a quantum one is sometimes reflected, except at special energies where a whole number of half-waves fits across the barrier and the reflections cancel.

That exponential makes tunneling extremely sensitive to distance. For an electron one electron volt short of the top, kappa is about five per nanometer. Through half a nanometer, roughly six in a thousand get through; through one nanometer, about four in a hundred thousand.

That sensitivity is useful. Tunneling is how alpha particles escape from nuclei, as Gamow explained in 1928. ## Part 3: Wavefunctions are vectors

_Scene `s09_hilbert.py::HilbertSpace`_

Step back and look at what we've been doing. A wavefunction is a list of complex numbers, one for each point. Sample it at N points, and it literally is a vector with N complex components. Shrink the spacing, and N goes to infinity. That's the right way to think about a quantum state: as a vector, written with this angle bracket, called a ket.

Vectors have dot products. For complex vectors you conjugate the first one, multiply component by component, and add. In the limit, the sum becomes an integral: the inner product of two wavefunctions. And the length squared of psi is exactly the total probability. A quantum state is a unit vector.

The stationary states of the box make a natural set of axes. Multiply two different ones together and integrate: the positive and negative areas cancel exactly. Multiply one by itself and you get one. They're orthonormal: perpendicular unit vectors, in infinitely many dimensions.

Any wavefunction in the box can be written as a combination of these axes, and the coefficient along each axis is just the inner product: the shadow of psi on that axis. Adding the terms one by one rebuilds our narrow packet: one term, two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two.

And since the axes are perpendicular, the squared lengths of the shadows add up to the squared length of the vector, which is one. That's Pythagoras' theorem, in a space with infinitely many dimensions. This space of wavefunctions is called a Hilbert space.

If states are vectors, then the Hamiltonian, which acts on states, must be a matrix. Let's build it. On a grid, the second derivative at one point is the neighbors minus twice the point itself, over the spacing squared. So minus h-bar squared over two m times the second derivative is this matrix: twos on the diagonal, minus ones beside it, zeros everywhere else.

Hand this matrix to any linear-algebra routine. Its eigenvectors come out as exactly the sampled sine waves of the box, and as the grid gets finer, its eigenvalues approach one, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five times the lowest. The energy levels of a quantum system are the eigenvalues of a matrix. That's the sense in which Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and Schrödinger's wave mechanics, invented separately in 1925 and 1926, are the same theory.

Our matrix is symmetric, and for complex matrices the right notion is Hermitian: equal to its own conjugate transpose, which means H can be moved from one side of an inner product to the other. Two short consequences. First: if phi is an eigenvector with eigenvalue E, move H across the inner product and you find E equals its own complex conjugate. Energies are real numbers, as measured quantities should be. Second: for two eigenvectors with different eigenvalues, the same move shows that their inner product must be zero. They're perpendicular.

Together these give the spectral theorem: a Hermitian operator is completely described by its eigenvalues and its perpendicular eigenvectors. Here, H is a sum of energies times projections onto the stationary states.

And time evolution has the cleanest possible form in this basis. The evolution operator, e to the minus i H t over h-bar, just multiplies each coefficient by its own phase. Each coefficient is a clock running at its own rate, and since phases don't change lengths, the sum of the squares stays one forever. The state vector rotates in Hilbert space. Everything we saw in the box, the sloshing and the carpet, is this: a set of clocks, summed.

## Observables, eigenvalues and the Born rule

_Scene `s10_measurement.py::Measurement`_

We can now state the rules of the whole theory on one screen. A state is a unit vector, up to an overall phase. Every measurable quantity is a Hermitian operator, described by its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. A measurement gives one of the eigenvalues, at random, with probability equal to the squared length of the state's shadow on that eigenvector: the Born rule. Right after the measurement, the state is that eigenvector. And between measurements, the state evolves by the Schrödinger equation.

Picture it with just three dimensions, and real numbers so we can draw it. The three perpendicular axes are the eigenvectors of some observable, with outcomes a one, a two, a three. The state is a unit vector pointing somewhere in between. Its shadows on the axes, squared, are the probabilities of the three outcomes, and by Pythagoras they add up to one. That's why the Born rule squares: it's the only way to turn the components of a unit vector into probabilities that always sum to one.

Now measure. Suppose the outcome is a two. The state jumps onto that axis: measure again immediately and you're certain to get a two. That jump is the one part of the theory that isn't described by the Schrödinger equation, and what really happens in it is still debated. But the rule itself has been tested to great precision.

Let's measure the energy of our narrow packet in the box. The possible outcomes are the energies E n, with probabilities given by the squared coefficients we computed before. Each measurement gives just one of them. Here are two thousand simulated measurements on two thousand identical copies of the state. The fractions settle onto the predicted probabilities.

And each individual measurement changes the state. Here the first outcome was E seven: the packet is replaced by the seventh stationary state, and stays there. Prepare a fresh packet and measure again. This time: E four, and a different collapse.

With the probabilities in hand, the average outcome follows. It's the sum of each outcome times its probability. Write the probability as the shadow times its conjugate, pull the sum inside, and the operator itself appears: the average of A is psi, A psi. You never need the eigenvectors to compute an average.

For our packet: summing energies times probabilities gives 484.73. Computing psi, H psi directly, as an integral of the slope squared, gives the same 484.73. And the average of our two thousand random outcomes is 487.1, within the noise you'd expect from two thousand samples.

The same rules cover every observable; only the axes change. For energy, the axes are the stationary states. For position, they're infinitely narrow spikes, and the shadow on the spike at x zero is just psi of x zero. So Born's rule for position, from the start of the video, is a special case. For momentum, the axes are plane waves, and the shadow is a new function, phi of p. What is it, and how are these two sets of axes related? That's next.

## Momentum, Fourier, and [x, p] = iħ

_Scene `s11_commutator.py::Commutator`_

The momentum operator is minus i h-bar times d by d x. Its eigenvectors are the plane waves: differentiating e to the i p x over h-bar just brings down a factor of p. Normalized, these are the axes for momentum. So the amplitude to find momentum p, the shadow of psi on each plane wave, is this integral: the Fourier transform. Position and momentum are two sets of axes for the same vector, related by a Fourier transform.

Watch the two pictures together for a Gaussian packet. Squeeze it in position, and its momentum distribution spreads out. Spread it in position, and the momentum distribution sharpens. For a Gaussian, the product of the two widths never changes: it's always h-bar over two. That's a hint of the uncertainty principle, and it's a property of Fourier transforms, not of any measuring device.

Here's the single most important equation in quantum mechanics. Apply position and momentum to a test function f, in both orders. Momentum first, then position: minus i h-bar x f prime. Position first, then momentum: the product rule gives an extra term, minus i h-bar f. Subtract, and everything cancels except i h-bar times f.

Since f was arbitrary, x p minus p x equals i h-bar: the canonical commutation relation. Born and Jordan found it in 1925, in the matrix version of the theory, and it's carved on Max Born's gravestone. It says position and momentum don't share any eigenvectors: nothing is both a spike in x and a plane wave in p. Almost everything that's strange about quantum mechanics flows from this one line, starting with the uncertainty principle.

There's a deeper way to see what momentum is. Exponentiate it: e to the minus i a p over h-bar. Expand the exponential as a power series, and since p is a derivative, you get exactly Taylor's series for psi evaluated at x minus a. Watch the partial sums of that series, using only derivatives at each point, march the function over by a. Momentum is what generates translations in space.

And commutators also tell us how things change in time. Differentiate an average with the product rule. Use the Schrödinger equation for each time derivative, move H across, since it's Hermitian, and the rate of change of any average is i over h-bar times the average of the commutator of H with A.

So anything that commutes with the Hamiltonian is conserved. For a free particle, H is p squared over two m, which commutes with p, so momentum is conserved: exactly why the momentum distribution of our spreading packet never changed. The Hamiltonian doesn't care where the particle is, so momentum, the generator of translations, can't change. Symmetries give conservation laws.

## The uncertainty principle, derived

_Scene `s12_uncertainty.py::Uncertainty`_

We've seen hints of it twice now. Squeeze a packet in position and its momentum spreads. Here's the precise statement: the standard deviation of position times the standard deviation of momentum is at least h-bar over two. Heisenberg had the idea in 1927; Kennard proved this exact form the same year; Robertson generalized it two years later. And it's worth being careful about what it says. The sigmas are spreads of outcomes, over many copies of the same state. It isn't about measurements disturbing things. It's a property of the state itself, and we can now prove it in a few lines.

The proof rests on one geometric fact about vectors. The inner product of f with g is the length of f times the length of g's shadow on it. A shadow is never longer than the vector casting it, so the inner product squared is at most the product of the two lengths squared. That's the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and it holds in any Hilbert space. It's an equality only when the two vectors are parallel.

Take any two observables A and B, and a state psi. Define two vectors: f is A minus its average, acting on psi, and g is the same for B. Their squared lengths are exactly the two variances. By Cauchy-Schwarz, the product of the variances is at least the inner product squared. A complex number's size squared is at least its imaginary part squared, and the imaginary part is the difference between the inner product and its conjugate, over two i.

Multiply out that difference. The averages cancel, and what's left is the average of A B minus B A: the commutator. Take a square root: the product of the spreads is at least half the size of the average commutator. That's Robertson's uncertainty relation, for any pair of observables at all.

Now plug in position and momentum. Their commutator is i h-bar, a constant, so its average is i h-bar in every state. Half its size is h-bar over two. Notice that no measuring device appears anywhere in this proof. The uncertainty principle is a consequence of the commutator.

When is the bound reached exactly? Both inequalities must be tight: g must be parallel to f, with a purely imaginary factor. For position and momentum, that's a first-order differential equation, and its solution is a Gaussian. So Gaussian packets, the ones we've been using all along, are the least uncertain states there are.

Let's map it out. Here's the plane of position spread against momentum spread, on logarithmic axes, so the bound becomes a straight line, and everything below it is forbidden. Gaussians of every width sit exactly on the line. The box's stationary states sit above it: the lowest at 0.568 h-bar, the others higher and higher. The harmonic oscillator's states, which we'll meet next, sit at n plus one half times h-bar.

And a free Gaussian packet, as it spreads, slides away from the line: its position spread grows while its momentum spread stays fixed. So does it become more uncertain? In one sense, yes. But something subtler is going on.

In the derivation we threw away the real part of the inner product. Schrödinger kept it in 1930, and got a stronger bound: the extra term is the covariance between position and momentum. For our spreading packet, the covariance grows, because the faster components are out in front, and the product of the variances minus the covariance squared stays at exactly one quarter, h-bar squared over four, at every moment. The spreading packet never gets any less sharp than quantum mechanics allows; its uncertainty just becomes correlated.

## Part 4: The harmonic oscillator, by ladder operators

_Scene `s13_oscillator.py::Oscillator`_

Next, the most important system in physics: the harmonic oscillator. Why so important? Take any potential with a smooth minimum, and zoom in. Near the bottom, Taylor's theorem says it's a parabola. So the oscillator describes anything that vibrates a little around equilibrium: molecules, crystals, even the modes of light. Its Hamiltonian is p squared over two m plus one half m omega squared x squared.

Dirac's trick for solving it: factor the Hamiltonian. For ordinary numbers, u squared plus v squared factors as u minus i v times u plus i v. So define the operator a, a combination of x and i p, and its adjoint, a dagger, with the opposite sign. Multiply them in this order and the cross terms don't quite cancel, because x and p don't commute. What's left is the commutator, i h-bar, which turns into a constant: minus one half.

So the Hamiltonian is h-bar omega times a dagger a, plus one half. And the same commutator gives a times a dagger minus a dagger times a equals one. That half and that one are the fingerprints of i h-bar. Everything else follows from algebra.

Now the key commutators. H with a dagger gives h-bar omega a dagger, and H with a gives minus h-bar omega a. Their meaning: if psi has energy E, then a dagger psi has energy E plus h-bar omega, and a psi has energy E minus h-bar omega. They're ladder operators: a dagger climbs one rung, a descends one, and the rungs are evenly spaced, h-bar omega apart.

But the ladder can't go down forever. The average energy is h-bar omega times the length squared of a psi, plus a half, and a length squared is never negative. So there's a bottom rung, where a has nothing left to lower: a psi zero equals zero. Written out, that's a first-order differential equation, and its solution is a Gaussian, with energy one half h-bar omega. Every other state is a dagger applied n times, with energy h-bar omega times n plus one half. We never solved a second-order differential equation.

Here's the result: a parabola with evenly spaced rungs, and the states that go with them. Each application of a dagger adds one more node, one more sign change: these are the Hermite functions. Evenly spaced energies are why a vibrating molecule absorbs light at one sharp frequency.

As a check, build the Hamiltonian as a big matrix on a fine grid, the same way we did for the box, and ask a computer for its eigenvalues. One half, three halves, five halves, to four decimal places.

Look closely at the ground state. A classical particle with this energy would turn around at these two points, where all its energy is potential. The quantum wavefunction doesn't stop there: its Gaussian tails reach into the region where the kinetic energy would be negative. The probability of finding the particle out there is the complementary error function of one: 15.7 percent. Tunneling isn't exotic; it's built into every bound state.

And high up the ladder, quantum mechanics starts to look classical. Here's the thirtieth state. A classical oscillator with the same energy spends most of its time near the turning points, where it's slowest, so its probability density piles up at the edges. The quantum density oscillates rapidly, but on average it follows the classical curve. This is Bohr's correspondence principle, made visible.

## Coherent states, phase space and the classical limit

_Scene `s14_classical.py::ClassicalLimit`_

How does the classical world emerge from all this? Start with the equation for averages from the commutator chapter. For position, the commutator of H with x gives the average momentum over the mass: velocity. For momentum, the commutator with the potential gives minus the average slope of the potential: the force. This is Ehrenfest's theorem: averages obey Newton's laws. With one catch: the average of the force isn't the force at the average position, unless the force is linear in x. For the harmonic oscillator it is, so the averages follow the classical motion exactly.

Here's a Gaussian packet in a harmonic potential, displaced from the center and released, simulated by solving the Schrödinger equation numerically. It swings back and forth exactly in step with a classical particle, and its shape never changes: the width stays at its minimum, forever. These are called coherent states: eigenvectors of the lowering operator, whose label alpha simply rotates in time. They're the most classical states quantum mechanics allows, and they describe the light from a laser.

Start with a narrower packet instead, and the center still follows the classical particle exactly, as Ehrenfest promised, but the width breathes: wide, narrow, wide, twice per swing. Averages are classical; the shape is not.

There's a beautiful way to see all of this at once: put position and momentum on the same picture, like a classical phase space. The Wigner function, defined by this integral over the wavefunction, lives there. Its shadows are exactly the two probability distributions: squash it onto the x axis and you get the position density; onto the p axis, the momentum density.

For our coherent state, it's a round blob, and it simply rotates around the origin, riding on the circle a classical particle traces in phase space. A classical state is a point; a quantum state is smeared over an area about the size of h-bar, the uncertainty principle in picture form.

But the Wigner function is not a probability distribution. Here's the first excited state of the oscillator: a ring, and right in the middle it goes negative, down to minus one over pi h-bar. No classical probability can do that. And here's a superposition of two displaced packets, a Schrödinger cat state. Between the two blobs are stripes of alternating sign: that's the interference between the two parts. The sign flips as you move along p, so the interference shows up in the momentum distribution, while the position distribution shows just two lumps.

Negative regions and fringes are the signatures of genuinely quantum states. In the classical limit, when the distances involved are huge compared with the scale set by h-bar, the blobs shrink toward points and the fringes become far too fine for any measurement to resolve, and the familiar classical world appears.

## The hydrogen atom

_Scene `s15_hydrogen.py::Hydrogen`_

Now the system that started it all: the hydrogen atom. One electron, held by a proton through the Coulomb force. In three dimensions, the energy equation reads like this. Before solving anything, ask what length the answer can depend on. From h-bar, the electron's mass and the strength of the electric force, there's only one way to make a length: the Bohr radius, about half an angstrom. And the natural energy is the Coulomb energy at that distance, 27.2 electron volts.

The potential depends only on the distance r, so separate the wavefunction into a radial part and an angular part. The angular parts are the spherical harmonics, labeled by two numbers, l and m, for the total angular momentum and its component along an axis. Notice the factor e to the i m phi: as you go around the vertical axis, the phase winds m times. Angular momentum is written in the phase, just as linear momentum was.

What's left is a one-dimensional problem for u, r times R, in an effective potential: the Coulomb well plus a centrifugal barrier that keeps electrons with angular momentum away from the nucleus.

Here's a short route to the ground state. Guess a solution that behaves like a power of r near the nucleus and decays exponentially far away. Differentiate it twice. Put it into the radial equation: you get a constant, plus a term proportional to one over r. For the equation to hold at every r, that term's coefficient must vanish. That fixes the decay rate, one over l plus one Bohr radii, and the energy: minus 13.6 electron volts over l plus one, squared.

For l equal to zero, that's the ground state: a simple exponential, with energy minus 13.6 electron volts. The probability of finding the electron at distance r, counting the whole shell of that radius, peaks at exactly one Bohr radius: the radius of Bohr's 1913 orbit, now as the most likely distance in a cloud.

For the other states, the same idea works with a polynomial in front of the exponential. Integrate outward from the nucleus at a trial energy: if the energy is a little off, the solution blows up far from the atom, one way or the other. Only at special energies does it decay. Requiring that the series stops gives the famous result: minus 13.6 electron volts over n squared.

And here's something remarkable. Solve the radial equation numerically for l equals zero, one and two, and the energies line up: they depend only on n, not on l. That's an accidental degeneracy, the fingerprint of a hidden symmetry of the one-over-r force that Pauli used in 1926 to solve hydrogen with matrix mechanics.

Here are the stationary states themselves, computed from the exact solutions and rendered as glowing clouds: the brightness is the probability density, the color is the phase. The first number is the energy level, the second the angular momentum, the third how many times the phase winds around the vertical axis. For each energy level there are n squared of them. These aren't orbits; they're stationary waves, and each would only rotate its colors as time passes.

And where do chemistry's dumbbell-shaped p orbitals come from? Take the states with m equals minus one and plus one: two rings of probability whose phases wind in opposite directions. Subtract them. On one side the phases agree and the waves add; on the other they cancel. The result is the dumbbell, a superposition of two angular momenta. Interference again.

Finally, light. Remember the box: a superposition of two energies sloshes back and forth at the difference frequency. Here's the same thing in hydrogen: the ground state plus the 2 p state. The electron cloud swings up and down, a tiny oscillating charge, at the Bohr frequency, E two minus E one over h: about two and a half million billion times per second. An oscillating charge radiates, at exactly that frequency: 121.6 nanometers, the Lyman alpha line, the strongest line hydrogen emits.

Every spectral line is a Bohr frequency. Transitions down to the second level give the visible Balmer lines: the red line at 656 nanometers, then 486, 434 and 410, exactly where astronomers find them in glowing hydrogen clouds across the sky. The colors of the universe are differences between eigenvalues.

## Part 5: Spin, the simplest quantum system

_Scene `s16_spin.py::Spin`_

In February 1922, in Frankfurt, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms through a deliberately uneven magnetic field. Each atom is a tiny magnet, and the uneven field pushes it up or down depending on which way its magnet points. Classically, the magnets point every which way, so you'd expect a continuous smear on the screen. What they saw was two separate spots. Every atom was deflected either fully up or fully down, never in between.

The explanation came a few years later: the electron carries an intrinsic angular momentum, its spin, whose component along any axis you measure is only ever plus or minus h-bar over two. It's not a little spinning ball; it's a quantum system with exactly two outcomes, and the simplest one there is.

A spin state is a vector in a two-dimensional complex space: alpha times up plus beta times down, with the squared lengths adding to one. Two complex numbers is four real numbers; the length condition removes one, and the overall phase, which doesn't matter, removes another. Two angles remain, and every state can be written as cosine theta over two times up, plus e to the i phi sine theta over two times down.

Two angles are a point on a sphere: the Bloch sphere. Up is the north pole, down the south pole, and their equal superpositions lie around the equator. Notice the half angles. Up and down are perpendicular vectors in the state space, but they sit at opposite poles: the sphere doubles every angle.

The spin observables are two-by-two matrices: h-bar over two times the Pauli matrices. The eigenvectors of sigma z are up and down; the eigenvectors of sigma x are their sum and difference, on the sphere's equator. And the components don't commute: S x S y minus S y S x is i h-bar S z. So, by the uncertainty relation we derived, no state has definite values of two different components. Knowing spin up along z means knowing nothing about x.

Now chain measurements. Prepare atoms with spin up along z, then measure along a direction n tilted by an angle theta. The Born rule says the probability of up along n is the squared overlap, and with the half angles from the sphere, that's cosine squared of theta over two. A thousand simulated atoms at each angle land right on the curve: always up at zero degrees, fifty-fifty at ninety, never at one eighty.

Here's the strangest version. Select spin up along z. Then measure along x, and keep only the atoms that came out up along x. Now measure z again. Before, every one of these atoms was up along z. Now it's fifty-fifty: 487 up, 513 down. The x measurement didn't just reveal something; it replaced the state, and the z information is gone.

Finally, put the spin in a magnetic field along z. The Hamiltonian is proportional to S z, so the up and down components just pick up opposite phases, and on the Bloch sphere the arrow precesses around the field like a spinning top: Larmor precession, about 28 billion turns per second per tesla for an electron. It's how MRI machines read the spins of protons.

But watch the two components themselves. They turn at half the rate of the arrow. After one full turn of the arrow, three hundred sixty degrees, both components have turned only halfway: the state has come back to minus itself. It takes seven hundred twenty degrees to get back to where you started. An overall minus sign is invisible on its own, but not in interference with an unrotated beam: neutron interferometers confirmed the sign flip in 1975.

## Entanglement and Bell's theorem

_Scene `s17_bell.py::Bell`_

With two particles, quantum mechanics gets truly strange. The key point first: two particles don't have two wavefunctions. They share one, a single complex number for every pair of positions, x one and x two. If it factors into a function of x one times a function of x two, the particles are independent. But most two-particle states don't factor. In this one, the probability lies along the diagonal: find particle one at 1.5, and particle two is pinned near 1.5 too, though on its own it could have been almost anywhere. That's entanglement.

The cleanest example uses spin. Two spin-half particles can be prepared in the singlet state, with total spin zero: up-down minus down-up. They fly apart, to Alice and to Bob, who each measure spin along an axis of their choosing. Work out the Born rule: if their axes differ by an angle theta, they get the same answer with probability sine squared of theta over two, and opposite answers with cosine squared. Score plus one for same and minus one for opposite. The average score, the correlation, is minus cosine theta. Yet each side, looked at alone, is a perfect coin flip.

In 1935 Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued that such correlations mean quantum mechanics is incomplete: surely each pair leaves the source carrying hidden instructions, lambda, that decide every possible answer in advance. In 1964 John Bell showed that this idea can be tested. Suppose Alice's answer depends only on her own setting and the instructions, and Bob's on his. Give Alice two possible settings, a and a prime, and Bob two, b and b prime.

For any one pair, all four answers are plus or minus one. Look at this combination: B and B prime either agree or disagree, so one of the brackets is zero and the other is plus or minus two. The whole thing is always plus or minus two. Average over many pairs, and the combination of four correlations, called S, can never exceed two in size. That's the CHSH inequality, and it follows from nothing but pre-existing answers and locality.

Now ask what quantum mechanics predicts. Choose the four directions forty-five degrees apart. Three of the correlations are minus cosine forty-five, about minus 0.707, and the fourth, at one hundred thirty-five degrees, is plus 0.707, which enters S with a minus sign. All four contributions point the same way: S is two root two, about 2.83. More than two. No theory of pre-existing local answers can produce these correlations. And two root two is itself a hard ceiling for quantum mechanics.

We can watch the difference in a simulation. Sample a hundred thousand pairs per setting from the Born rule, and the correlation traces out minus cosine theta. Now a classical model with hidden instructions: each pair carries a random direction, and each side answers according to which side of it their axis falls on. Its correlation is a straight line. The two agree at zero, ninety and one hundred eighty degrees, but the cosine bulges past the straight line in between, and that bulge is exactly what CHSH detects. From the samples: S is 2.828 for quantum mechanics, and 1.996 for hidden instructions, within noise of the bound of two.

And in both cases, Alice's results on their own are always fifty-fifty, whatever Bob does. The correlation only appears when they compare notes, so entanglement can't be used to send a message.

Experiment decided it. Freedman and Clauser saw the violation with photons in 1972. Alain Aspect's group switched the settings while the photons were in flight in 1982. In 2015, a team in Delft closed the remaining loopholes with electron spins 1.3 kilometers apart, measuring S equals 2.42, plus or minus 0.20, and groups in Vienna and at NIST did the same with photons. The 2022 Nobel Prize went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger. Nature violates Bell's bound: the outcomes are not simply written in advance, in any local way.

## Recap

_Scene `s18_outro.py::Outro`_

Let's step back and see how far we've come. The whole theory fits on one line: the Schrödinger equation, the Born rule, and the commutation relation between position and momentum. States are vectors whose amplitudes add before you square them. Observables are Hermitian operators. Time evolution turns each energy component at its own rate. Position and momentum can't both be sharp. Energies are quantized when a wave has to fit. And entangled particles share a single wavefunction, with correlations no local model can match.

And every picture in this video, the wave through the slits, the carpet, the orbitals, the phase-space pictures, came from solving those equations, not from an artist's impression. A hundred years after Schrödinger, the same equation runs the transistors in your phone, the lasers in fiber-optic cables and the magnets in MRI scanners.

There's much more. Identical particles and the Pauli exclusion principle, which make chemistry possible. Perturbation theory, for real atoms. Dirac's relativistic equation, and quantum field theory beyond it. Decoherence, and the still-open question of what measurement really is. And quantum computing, which is the same linear algebra you've just seen, in two to the n dimensions. But all of it is built on what's on this screen: complex amplitudes, evolving by the Schrödinger equation. Thanks for watching.
