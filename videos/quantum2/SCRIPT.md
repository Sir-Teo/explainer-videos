## Where do the rules come from?

At the end of Part 1, the whole theory fit on one screen: the Schrödinger equation, the Born rule, and the commutator of position and momentum. But look at how we got them. We guessed the equation by making it work for plane waves. We took the Born rule as a postulate. And we computed the commutator from a formula for momentum that we had also guessed. In this video, we derive them, and then we put them to work.

Start with the double slit again, seen Richard Feynman's way. A source on the left, two slits, a screen. Pick one point on the screen, and consider every way to get there: through any point of either slit. Each path contributes an arrow, and the arrow turns one full revolution for every wavelength of path length, so the color of each path shows which way its arrow points. Now add all the arrows, tip to tail. The paths through each slit give a gently curving string of arrows; the two strings join; and the length of the total, squared, is the brightness at that point.

Now move the point. As it moves, the path lengths change, the arrows swing around, and the total grows and shrinks. Tracing its length squared along the screen draws the interference fringes. In Part 1 we had two arrows; here every point of each slit has its own, and the fringes come out with the slits' finite width built in.

Feynman's real claim is far stronger: not just straight paths through the slits, but every path at all, however wild, contributes an arrow, turning at a rate set by a quantity from classical mechanics, the action. That single idea contains the Schrödinger equation, and it explains why a thrown ball follows just one path.

It's one of three roads we'll take. The first is symmetry: the laws don't change from moment to moment, or from place to place, and those two facts alone force the Schrödinger equation and the commutator. The second is Feynman's sum over paths. The third runs backward from classical mechanics, and explains the old quantum rules of Bohr and Sommerfeld. All three arrive at the same theory.

## Part 1: Time symmetry gives the Schrödinger equation

_Scene `s02_time.py::TimeSymmetry`_

Part 1: Symmetry.

What is a symmetry, in quantum mechanics? It's a change in how we describe things that leaves every prediction unchanged: turn your lab around, start your clock later, move everything a meter to the left. Every prediction is a probability, the squared size of an inner product, so a symmetry must preserve the size of every inner product. Eugene Wigner showed in 1931 that such a map can always be chosen to be unitary, U dagger U equals one, which preserves inner products exactly, or antiunitary, which also conjugates them; that second kind is needed only for time reversal. Geometrically, a unitary is a rigid rotation of Hilbert space: lengths and angles all stay the same.

Now apply this to time. Let U of t take any state at time zero to the state at time t. If the laws are the same at every moment, evolving for s and then for t is the same as evolving for s plus t: U of t plus s equals U of t times U of s, with U of zero the identity. A family like this is called a one-parameter group, and Marshall Stone proved in 1932 that every continuous one has the form e to the minus i t H over h-bar, for a unique self-adjoint operator H.

Here's the heart of that argument. For a tiny time epsilon, U is the identity plus something small: call it minus i epsilon H over h-bar. That defines H. Now demand unitarity. Multiply out U dagger U: the first-order term is i epsilon over h-bar times H dagger minus H, and it must vanish. So H equals its own adjoint: it's Hermitian.

Now differentiate the group law: the change in U over a short time is U of epsilon minus one, over epsilon, times U of t. By the first line, that's minus i over h-bar, H, U. Act on any state, and you get i h-bar times the time derivative of psi equals H psi. The Schrödinger equation, with no waves anywhere: only the assumption that the laws don't change with time, and that probabilities are conserved.

And the i is no longer mysterious. A unitary operator close to the identity is the identity plus a small anti-Hermitian operator; writing it as minus i times a Hermitian one is bookkeeping that makes H's eigenvalues real. You've seen the same thing in the plane: rotating a vector, its velocity is always perpendicular to it, and the matrix J that turns a vector a quarter turn squares to minus one. Exponentiate a generator like that, term by term, and the partial sums spiral onto the circle. Rotations come from an i.

What is H? It's the generator of time translations, and it commutes with U, so its average never changes. That's what energy is, in quantum mechanics and in classical mechanics alike. The constant h-bar just converts the rate at which phases turn into units of energy, and its value comes from experiment. For an energy eigenstate the evolution is a single turning phase, e to the minus i E t over h-bar: Part 1's clocks. And for a spin in a magnetic field, U of t is a rigid rotation of the whole Bloch sphere about the field.

## Translations and boosts: deriving [x, p] = iħ

_Scene `s03_translations.py::Translations`_

Space works the same way. Let T of a be the operator that slides every wavefunction a distance a to the right: psi of x becomes psi of x minus a. Sliding twice adds the distances, and nothing about probabilities changes, so T is a one-parameter unitary group, and by Stone's theorem it has a generator. Call it P: T of a equals e to the minus i a P over h-bar. This is the definition of momentum: the generator of translations. If the Hamiltonian doesn't care where things are, H commutes with T, and momentum is conserved.

Now we can derive the commutator, instead of computing it from a guess. A position eigenstate, slid by a, is the position eigenstate at x plus a. So measuring position after the slide gives x plus a, and before it gives x: X and T fail to commute, by exactly a times T. That's true for every x, so it's an operator equation.

Expand T for a small slide: one minus i a P over h-bar. The identity commutes with everything, and what's left is minus i a over h-bar times X P minus P X, equal to a. Divide by a: X P minus P X equals i h-bar. The canonical commutation relation is just the statement that momentum moves position.

And the formula for momentum follows. In the position basis, the slid wavefunction is psi of x minus a. Expand both sides to first order in a: Taylor's theorem on one side, the generator on the other. Matching them, P acting on psi is minus i h-bar times the derivative. Part 1 guessed this; here it's forced.

One consequence is startling. No finite matrices can satisfy X P minus P X equals i h-bar. The trace of any commutator is zero, because the trace of X P equals the trace of P X. Here's a random five-by-five example, whose diagonal entries cancel exactly. But the trace of i h-bar times the identity is i h-bar times the dimension. So the Hilbert space of a particle on a line has to be infinite-dimensional, and, as Stone and von Neumann proved, any operators satisfying the relation are just x and minus i h-bar d d x in disguise. That's why Heisenberg's matrices and Schrödinger's waves had to agree.

Boosts, changing to a frame moving at velocity v, give the rest. Seen from the moving frame, every momentum shifts by m v, and on a wavefunction that's multiplication by e to the i m v x over h-bar: a twist of the phase that winds the colors faster, while the density doesn't change at all. Watch the momentum distribution slide over by exactly m v.

Now require that velocities add properly: the rate of change of X, which by the Heisenberg equation is i over h-bar times the commutator of H with X, must be P over m in every frame. The kinetic energy, P squared over 2m, has exactly that commutator with X. So H minus P squared over 2m commutes with X, and anything that commutes with position is a function of position. H equals P squared over 2m plus V of X. The form of the Hamiltonian, derived from Galilean relativity.

One last detail will come back again and again. Slide by a and then boost, or boost and then slide: the results differ only by a phase, e to the minus i m v a over h-bar. And m v a is the area of a rectangle in phase space, a wide and m v tall. Phase equals area over h-bar. We'll meet it next with magnetic flux, and later in the old quantum rules.

## Rotations: angular momentum and spin from algebra

_Scene `s04_rotations.py::Rotations`_

Rotations are the third symmetry, and the most interesting, because they don't commute. Turn a book a quarter turn about one axis and then about another; now do it in the opposite order. The book ends up facing a different way.

Even for tiny turns: rotate by epsilon about x, then about y, then undo both, and you're left with a rotation about z by epsilon squared. Here are the numbers, for real rotation matrices: the leftover angle divided by epsilon squared goes to one, and its axis to z.

Each rotation is a unitary, e to the minus i theta J over h-bar, and the three generators are the components of angular momentum. Expand that loop of four rotations to second order, match it to the leftover rotation about z, and you get J x J y minus J y J x equals i h-bar J z, and the same for every cyclic pair. That one algebraic relation determines every possible value of angular momentum, without solving any equation.

Here's how. Combine J x and J y into J plus and J minus. Their commutator with J z is plus or minus h-bar times themselves, so J plus raises the z component by one unit of h-bar, and J minus lowers it. The total, J squared, commutes with all three components, so the whole ladder shares one value of J squared.

But J z squared can't exceed J squared, so the ladder has a top rung, where J plus gives zero, and a bottom rung. Multiply out J minus J plus, apply it to the top rung, and J squared equals h-bar squared, j times j plus one. Do the same at the bottom with J plus J minus, and the bottom rung must be at minus j. Getting from minus j to j takes a whole number of steps, so two j is a whole number.

So j can be zero, one, two, but also one half, three halves. Orbital angular momentum, x cross p, only gives whole numbers, because the phase e to the i m phi has to come back to itself after a full turn. The half-integers are spin, and the algebra allowed them before anyone knew they were needed: a rotation by three hundred sixty degrees multiplies such a state by minus one, the sign Part 1 found for the electron.

Here's the ladder for j equals two, painted on spheres: the color is the phase of each spherical harmonic, the brightness its size. Going up, the phase winds once more around the vertical axis, and each step multiplies by exactly the square root of l times l plus one, minus m times m plus one, checked here on a grid to better than one part in ten thousand.

And because J squared is j times j plus one, h-bar squared, while J z is at most j h-bar, angular momentum can never point exactly along an axis: the other two components can never both be zero.

## Local phase symmetry and the Aharonov–Bohm effect

_Scene `s05_gauge.py::Gauge`_

Part 1 showed that multiplying the whole wavefunction by one phase, e to the i alpha, changes nothing. So here's a bolder question. What if we change the phase by a different amount at every point? The colors scramble, but every probability stays exactly the same.

The Schrödinger equation, however, notices. Apply the momentum operator to the new wavefunction. The product rule brings down an extra term, the gradient of the phase: as far as the equation is concerned, the particle has been given a momentum kick that varies from place to place. A pure change of bookkeeping would change the physics.

There's only one way to rescue the symmetry. Introduce a field, A, that shifts by the same gradient whenever the phase is changed, and use p minus q A in place of p. Now the extra terms cancel exactly: p minus q A acting on the new wavefunction is just the phase factor times p minus q A acting on the old one. Do the same with time, and you need a second field, phi.

The Hamiltonian becomes p minus q A, squared, over 2m, plus q phi: exactly how a charge couples to the electromagnetic potentials, with the electric and magnetic fields, built from derivatives of A and phi, unchanged by the phase change. Fock, London and Weyl found this between 1926 and 1929: demand that local phase changes be a symmetry, and electromagnetism appears.

This has a startling consequence. A charged particle moving along a path picks up an extra phase, q over h-bar times the integral of A along the path. For two paths that start and end together, the difference is the integral around the loop, which by Stokes' theorem is the magnetic flux through the loop. So the relative phase is q times the flux, over h-bar, even if the magnetic field is zero everywhere the particle goes. Phase equals area over h-bar again; this time the area is threaded by magnetic flux. Aharonov and Bohm pointed this out in 1959.

Here's the effect, simulated. An electron wave passes through two slits; hidden inside the wall between them is a thin tube of magnetic flux. The wave never reaches it: the density there stays below a ten-thousandth of its peak. On the left, no flux. On the right, half a flux quantum, and the bright central fringe has become a dark line.

Now turn the flux up slowly, and watch the pattern on the screen. The fringes slide sideways under a fixed envelope, by one whole fringe for each flux quantum, h over e, and then the pattern is exactly what it was. The electrons never touch the magnetic field, yet they know how much flux they went around.

In 1986 Akira Tonomura's group at Hitachi did it with electrons passing around a tiny ring-shaped magnet, sealed inside a superconductor so that no field could leak out. A superconducting ring traps flux only in multiples of h over 2e, half a flux quantum, so the fringes could only be shifted by zero or by half a fringe. That's exactly what they saw.

## Part 2: Feynman's sum over paths

_Scene `s06_path_integral.py::PathIntegral`_

Part 2: Sums over paths.

Everything about time evolution is packed into one function: the amplitude to start at x a and be found at x b a time t later, called the propagator, K. Any initial wavefunction evolves by adding up the propagator from every starting point. For a free particle it's a Gaussian with an imaginary exponent: its size is the same everywhere, and its phase grows with the square of the distance, so the colors cycle faster and faster. A particle that starts at one point can be found anywhere a moment later.

Feynman's way of thinking about K starts with the double slit. Add a third slit: three paths. Add a second screen with its own slits: the amplitude is a sum over every way through both. Keep adding screens and drilling holes, until there's nothing left of the screens. Now the amplitude is a sum over every path from source to detector.

Here's the same idea as mathematics. Cut the time into N short steps of length epsilon, so the evolution is U of epsilon, applied N times. At each intermediate time, the particle could be anywhere: x 1, x 2, and so on. One choice of all of them is a zigzag path; every choice is a different zigzag.

Write the propagator as N short evolutions. Between each pair of factors, insert a complete set of positions: one integral over x k for each intermediate time. For one short step, the propagator is the free one times a phase from the potential, and its exponent is i epsilon over h-bar times the kinetic minus the potential energy: the Lagrangian.

Multiply N of these together and the exponents add up to i over h-bar times the action of the zigzag path through all the x k. Integrating over every x k means adding up every zigzag. In the limit, K is the integral over all paths of e to the i S over h-bar. Dirac noticed in 1933 that e to the i S over h-bar plays this role; Feynman, in 1948, showed it is all of quantum mechanics.

Can we actually do this sum? In real time the arrows spin so fast that sampling paths is hopeless. But if we let time be imaginary, each path gets a positive weight, e to the minus the action, and the paths become Brownian motion, the subject of our stochastic calculus video. Then the sum is ordinary arithmetic. Here's the short-time kernel of the harmonic oscillator, as a matrix.

Multiply it by itself again and again: any starting shape relaxes onto the ground state, the Gaussian, and the rate at which it shrinks gives the ground-state energy. With epsilon equal to one, the estimate is 0.481; at 0.05 it's 0.49995. The error falls like epsilon squared, toward exactly one half.

Or sample the paths themselves. Here are closed random paths in imaginary time, each drawn with its proper weight, each with a hundred time slices. Histogram where they spend their time, and you get the ground-state density, computed from nothing but a sum over paths.

## The Schrödinger equation from the path integral

_Scene `s07_path_to_schrodinger.py::PathToSchrodinger`_

Feynman's formulation has to agree with Schrödinger's, and checking that is a beautiful piece of calculus. Advance the wavefunction by one short step epsilon: integrate the short-time kernel against psi, over every displacement eta. The kernel's phase is m eta squared over two h-bar epsilon. Outside a window of width about the square root of h-bar epsilon over m, it oscillates so fast that its contributions cancel. As epsilon shrinks, the window shrinks, but only like the square root of epsilon. That square root is the whole story.

Expand psi of x plus eta in a Taylor series. Normally we'd keep only the first-order term, but here eta squared is as big as epsilon, so the second derivative stays in. Now we need three Gaussian integrals. The kernel's normalization makes the first one exactly one. The second vanishes by symmetry. And the third, the average of eta squared, is i h-bar epsilon over m: the spread of the step, with an i in it.

Collect everything to first order in epsilon. On the left, psi plus epsilon times its time derivative; on the right, the potential's phase times psi plus i h-bar epsilon over 2m times psi double prime. The psi terms cancel. Multiply by i h-bar over epsilon, and out comes the Schrödinger equation, every factor in place: the i, the h-bar squared over 2m, and the potential. Feynman did exactly this in his 1948 paper.

That i h-bar epsilon over m deserves a second look. A step of size eta, whose square is proportional to the time step: it's the same rule as d W squared equals d t, the heart of the stochastic calculus video, just with an i. It means the paths that matter in Feynman's sum are not smooth curves at all. Here is a typical path, in imaginary time, at four zoom levels, each eight times closer: it never smooths out. The kinetic term in the Schrödinger equation is the trace of that roughness.

The time-slicing isn't just a proof device; it's an algorithm. Split each short step into half a step of potential phase, the free kernel, and another half step of potential. The free kernel is just multiplication in momentum space, one fast Fourier transform away. That's how every simulation in Part 1 was computed. Here's a wave packet in an anharmonic well, evolved with fewer and fewer slices, compared with the exact answer: the error falls like epsilon squared.

## Stationary phase: how Newton's laws emerge

_Scene `s08_stationary_phase.py::StationaryPhase`_

If every path counts, why does a thrown ball follow just one? Take a ball thrown straight up and caught again two seconds later. The classical path is a parabola in time. Now consider a whole family of other paths with the same start and end, bent by different amounts a.

For each one, compute the action, the integral of kinetic minus potential energy. Plotted against a, it's a parabola, smallest at a equals zero: the classical path. Each path's arrow turns by S over h-bar, and that's its color. Near the bottom, neighboring paths have almost the same action, so their arrows point the same way. Far from it, the action changes quickly, and their arrows spin.

Add the arrows tip to tail, in order of a. The middle of the chain, from paths near the classical one, is a long straight run. The ends curl into tight spirals that go nowhere: those paths cancel each other out. This curve is a Cornu spiral, the same one that describes light at the edge of a shadow.

Now make h-bar smaller. The spirals wind tighter, the straight run shrinks, and fewer and fewer paths around the classical one survive.

With two ways to bend the path, the picture is a whole plane of arrows. Color each point by its arrow's direction: the rings crowd together everywhere except at the center, where the action is stationary. That's the principle of stationary phase.

So the paths that survive are those where small changes don't change the action, to first order. Vary the path by delta x, keeping its ends fixed. Integrate by parts, and the first-order change in S is the integral of delta x times something. For that to vanish for every delta x, the something must be zero: the Euler-Lagrange equation. For L equals one half m x dot squared minus V, it's m x double dot equals minus V prime. Newton's second law, recovered as the limit of quantum mechanics.

How small is h-bar, really? For a one-gram ball moving at a meter per second for one second, the action is about four point seven times ten to the thirty h-bars: bending its path by the width of a single atom turns its arrow some forty billion times. Only the classical path survives. For an electron in an atom, the action around an orbit is about h itself; many paths contribute, and the world is quantum. Fermat's principle for light rays is the same story: rays are where the phases of waves are stationary.

## WKB and the Bohr–Sommerfeld rule

_Scene `s09_wkb.py::WKB`_

The third road runs backward, from classical mechanics. Look for solutions with a classical-looking action in the exponent: an amplitude A times e to the i S over h-bar, both functions of x. Put that into the time-independent Schrödinger equation, and sort the terms by powers of h-bar.

At order h-bar to the zero: S prime squared over 2m plus V equals E. That's the Hamilton-Jacobi equation of classical mechanics: S prime is the classical momentum, p of x, and the phase is the integral of p. At the next order, two A prime S prime plus A S double prime equals zero, which says A squared times p is constant: A goes like one over the square root of p. Where the particle is slow, it's more likely to be found, just as a classical particle spends more time where it's slow. Dropping the next order is the WKB approximation, after Wentzel, Kramers and Brillouin in 1926, and Jeffreys before them.

Here's how well it works, for a particle in a quartic well, V equals x to the fourth. The exact sixth state, computed numerically, and the WKB wave: inside the well they're nearly indistinguishable, with short wavelengths where the particle is fast, and big amplitudes where it's slow. But at the turning points, where p goes to zero, one over the square root of p blows up.

Near a turning point the potential is nearly a straight line, and the exact solution there is an Airy function. Matching it to the WKB wave shows that each turning point shifts the phase by pi over four, an eighth of a cycle. So fitting a standing wave between two turning points requires the integral of p dx, there and back, to be n plus one half, times h. That's Bohr and Sommerfeld's quantum condition, with the half that Kramers found in 1926: his paper's title is "wave mechanics and half-integer quantization."

The test: exact energies against this rule. The ground state is off by eighteen percent: it has barely one bump, hardly a short wavelength. But by the tenth state the error is three parts in ten thousand, and by the twentieth it's less than one in ten thousand. The old rule, without the half, is wrong at every n.

And here's the geometry. Each energy level is a closed orbit in phase space, and the orbit for level n encloses an area of n plus one half times h. Consecutive orbits are separated by exactly one h of area: each quantum state occupies one cell of size h. Phase equals area over h-bar, one more time. And the Wigner function of the eighth state, computed from the exact wavefunction, lies right along its classical orbit, with interference ripples inside.

## Alpha decay: 24 orders of magnitude from one integral

_Scene `s10_gamow.py::Gamow`_

WKB's formula for tunneling solved one of the great puzzles of the 1920s. Uranium-238 emits alpha particles with about 4.27 million electron volts of energy, and has a half-life of four and a half billion years. Polonium-212 emits them with 8.95, and lasts a third of a microsecond. Twice the energy, ten to the twenty-four times faster. Geiger and Nuttall had found the pattern in 1911; nobody could explain it.

In 1928, George Gamow, and independently Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon, pictured the alpha particle rattling around inside the nucleus, held in by the electric repulsion of the rest of the nucleus once it's outside. The repulsion makes a barrier about twenty-eight million electron volts high at the nuclear surface, far above the alpha's energy. Classically it could only escape beyond sixty femtometers, and it could never get there.

Quantum mechanically, it tunnels, and WKB gives the probability: e to the minus two times the integral of kappa across the forbidden region, where kappa is the square root of 2 mu times V minus Q, over h-bar. For a Coulomb barrier the integral can be done in closed form. For uranium the exponent is 88: each time the alpha hits the wall, it gets through with a probability of about one in ten to the thirty-eight. It hits the wall nearly ten to the twenty-one times a second, so it takes billions of years. For polonium-212, with twice the energy, the barrier is thinner, the exponent drops to 32, and the decay takes a fraction of a microsecond.

Here are 29 alpha emitters, their half-lives from the nuclear data tables spanning twenty-four orders of magnitude, plotted against the nuclear charge over the square root of the energy. The crosses are Gamow's formula, with one assumed nuclear radius and no fitting to these data. It follows the measurements across all twenty-four decades, typically within a factor of two. The worst misses, polonium-210 and 208, sit at a closed shell of 126 neutrons, where forming an alpha particle inside the nucleus is harder: physics this simple picture leaves out. One integral, from the semiclassical limit of the Schrödinger equation, explains the rest.

## Part 3: Perturbation theory, and why its series diverge

_Scene `s11_perturbation.py::Perturbation`_

Part 3: Approximations.

The box, the oscillator and hydrogen are almost the only systems we can solve exactly. Add a second electron, an electric field, or a slightly different shape, and exact solutions vanish. So most of quantum mechanics is approximation, done carefully. The workhorse is perturbation theory: split the Hamiltonian into a part we can solve, H zero, plus a small extra piece, lambda V.

Expand both the energy and the state in powers of lambda, put them into the eigenvalue equation, and match powers. At first order, take the inner product with the unperturbed state. Because H zero is Hermitian, its terms cancel, and the first-order energy shift is simply the average of V in the unperturbed state.

Taking the inner product with any other state m gives the first-order change of the state: V's matrix element, divided by the energy difference. And at second order, the energy shift is a sum of squared matrix elements over energy differences. For the ground state every denominator is negative, so second order always pushes the ground state down.

Try it on a particle in a box, in a uniform field F. The first-order shift is zero: by symmetry, the average of x minus L over two vanishes. The second-order sum can be done in closed form: minus fifteen minus pi squared, over twenty-four pi to the fourth, times F squared. The computed sum agrees to six digits, and the exact energy follows the parabola at small fields, before higher orders take over.

Notice the energy differences in the denominators: when two levels come close, they push each other apart. For two levels it's exact: the eigenvalues are hyperbolas, and the closest approach is twice the coupling.

Here's a rectangular drum of fixed area, stretched one way and squeezed the other. Its levels cross freely, because the problem separates into x and y: states with different symmetries don't talk to each other. Add a small dent off-center, and the symmetry is gone. Every crossing opens up into a gap: levels of the same symmetry never cross. Wigner and von Neumann proved this in 1929.

Last, a warning. For the anharmonic oscillator, the energy as a series in the coupling g can be computed exactly, to any order: one half, plus three quarters g, minus twenty-one eighths g squared, and on. But the coefficients grow like n factorial. The series converges for no value of g except zero: Bender and Wu proved it in 1969. Dyson saw why: flip the sign of g and the well opens up; the particle can tunnel out, and no smooth formula can cover both signs.

Yet the partial sums are wonderfully accurate if you stop in time. At g equals 0.02, the error falls to three parts in a hundred million at order sixteen, then grows without bound. At g equals 0.05, the best you can do is order six. Perturbation series are asymptotic: use them, but stop at the smallest term.

## Fermi's golden rule: where exponential decay comes from

_Scene `s12_golden_rule.py::GoldenRule`_

So far, energies. Now rates: how fast do things happen? Let a perturbation V act on a system that starts in state i. Write the state as a sum over energy eigenstates, with each one's clock factored out. The exact equations for the coefficients involve V's matrix elements, times phases turning at the Bohr frequencies. To first order, the amplitude to have jumped to state f is an integral of V times that turning phase.

For a perturbation switched on at time zero and then held fixed, the integral is elementary, and the probability is V's matrix element squared, over h-bar squared, times this function of the detuning, omega.

This function controls everything. As time goes on, its peak grows like t squared, while its width shrinks like one over t. So its area grows exactly like t, and the function concentrates into a spike: two pi t times a delta function.

If there's just one final state, that means nothing much: the probability sloshes back and forth. But if the final states form a continuum, with rho states per unit energy, adding up the spike over all of them gives a probability that grows in proportion to t: a constant rate. Gamma equals two pi over h-bar, times V squared, times the density of states. Dirac derived it in 1927; Fermi called it the golden rule.

Let's test it, exactly. One level, coupled with the same strength to 601 evenly spaced levels. Solve the Schrödinger equation exactly, and watch the probability of staying put, on a logarithmic scale. It falls off exponentially, at a rate within one percent of the golden rule's.

Then, at two pi h-bar over the level spacing, every phase lines up again, and the probability comes back. A finite ladder of levels is not a true continuum; it only acts like one for times shorter than that.

And the energy of whatever was emitted is spread out. Here are the populations of the 601 levels after the decay: a Lorentzian line whose full width is exactly h-bar gamma. A short lifetime means a broad line.

In an atom, the continuum is the electromagnetic field itself. An excited hydrogen atom in its 2p state is coupled to the vacuum's photon modes, whose density grows like the frequency squared. Put in the dipole matrix element, and the golden rule gives a lifetime of 1.596 nanoseconds, matching measurement. In that time the electron cloud sloshes about four million times, at the Lyman-alpha frequency, before the light gets out.

## Part 4: Identical particles: bosons, fermions, Pauli

_Scene `s13_identical.py::Identical`_

Part 4: Many particles.

Two particles share one wavefunction on the plane of their positions, x one and x two, as in Part 1. If the particles are identical, two electrons say, swapping them can't change any prediction. And swapping is reflecting the picture across the diagonal. So the swap, P, is a symmetry, and since swapping twice does nothing, P squared is one: its only possible eigenvalues are plus one and minus one.

Every kind of particle picks one: symmetric wavefunctions for bosons, like photons, antisymmetric ones for fermions, like electrons. Which particles are which is the spin-statistics theorem: integer spin means boson, half-integer means fermion; its proof needs relativity. In a flat, two-dimensional world there's a third option, called anyons.

Here's what that does. Two particles in a box, one in the lowest state and one in the second. If they were distinguishable, the wavefunction would be this. Symmetrize it, and it piles up along the diagonal: bosons are found close together more often. Antisymmetrize it, and it vanishes along the entire diagonal: two identical fermions are never found at the same place.

The chance of finding the two within a tenth of the box of each other: twenty percent if they're distinguishable, thirty-eight for bosons, under two for fermions. There's no force here. The Hamiltonian is the same in all three cases; this is a correlation produced by symmetry alone.

Put two fermions in the same state, and the antisymmetric combination is zero: there's no such state. That's Pauli's exclusion principle, from 1925. For N fermions, the antisymmetric state is a determinant of their single-particle wavefunctions, and a determinant with two equal rows vanishes. Hold one fermion still, and the others are kept away from it: a hole travels with every electron.

Bosons do the opposite, and the most striking demonstration uses light. Send two identical photons into a half-silvered mirror, one from each side. Each photon is either transmitted, with amplitude one over root two, or reflected, with amplitude i over root two. For the photons to leave by different ports, both must be transmitted, or both reflected. Those two amplitudes are one half and minus one half: they cancel exactly. The photons always leave together.

Delay one photon so they no longer overlap, and they act independently, splitting up half the time. As the delay goes to zero, coincidences vanish. This dip was measured by Hong, Ou and Mandel in 1987, and it's now a standard test of whether two photons are truly identical.

## Helium, exchange and the periodic table

_Scene `s14_helium.py::Helium`_

Helium has two electrons, and the term that couples them, their repulsion, one over r one two, makes it impossible to solve exactly. Ignore that term, and each electron sits in a hydrogen-like orbit around a nucleus of charge two: an energy of minus 108.8 electron volts. The measured value is minus 79.0. The repulsion matters.

First-order perturbation theory says: average the repulsion over the unperturbed state. Each electron is a spherical cloud, and by Newton's shell theorem, a spherical shell of charge acts like a point charge at its center from outside, and like nothing at all from inside. That reduces a six-dimensional integral to a two-dimensional one, and the answer is exactly five eighths of Z, in atomic units: 34 electron volts. So the estimate becomes minus 74.8: much better, but now too high.

We can do better with the variational principle. Expand any trial state in the true eigenstates: its average energy is a weighted average of the eigenvalues, so it can never be below the lowest one. Every guess gives an upper bound, and the best guess in a family is the one with the lowest energy. Let each electron see an effective charge zeta instead of two, because each electron partly screens the nucleus from the other. The energy is a parabola in zeta, with its minimum at twenty-seven sixteenths, giving minus 77.5 electron volts.

A computer solution of the full two-electron equation, keeping only the spherically symmetric part of the repulsion, gets to minus 78.3. With everything included, theory and experiment agree at minus 79.0.

Now the excited state with one electron in the one S orbital and one in the two S. Here the two electrons can have opposite spins or parallel ones. The total wavefunction must be antisymmetric, so parallel spins force an antisymmetric spatial part. Here are both states, computed on the plane of the two radii, r one and r two. The triplet's wavefunction vanishes along the diagonal: the electrons keep apart, repel each other less, and have lower energy. The computed splitting is 0.82 electron volts; the measured one is 0.80. That's the origin of Hund's rule, and of magnetism in materials: an energy that depends on spin, although no magnetic force is involved.

With more electrons, the exclusion principle forces them into higher and higher orbitals, filling shells. Here is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron, for every element up to radon, from NIST's measurements. It peaks at the noble gases, where a shell has just been completed, and plunges at the alkali metals, whose one extra electron starts a new shell, far out and well screened. The periodic table is the exclusion principle, written in chemistry.

## Bloch's theorem: why solids have bands

_Scene `s15_bands.py::Bands`_

A solid is a lattice of atoms, each a potential well. Let's build one, well by well. One well has its own levels. Put a second well beside it, and each level splits in two: the particle can tunnel between the wells, and the even and odd combinations have slightly different energies. Three wells: each level becomes three. Eight, sixteen, thirty-two: each level becomes a band of closely spaced levels, with gaps in between where there are no states at all. The deepest band is the narrowest, because tunneling through the barriers is hardest there.

For an infinite lattice, symmetry tells us what the states look like. The potential repeats with period a, so the Hamiltonian commutes with the translation by one period. They share eigenstates, and since the translation is unitary, its eigenvalue is a phase: shifting by a multiplies psi by e to the i k a. So every stationary state is a plane wave, e to the i k x, times a function with the period of the lattice. That's Bloch's theorem, from 1928. Here's one: the size repeats from cell to cell, and the color advances by a quarter turn per cell.

Now we can find the bands exactly. Carry a solution across one cell with a two-by-two transfer matrix. Bloch's condition says its eigenvalues are e to the plus and minus i k a, so cos k a equals half its trace: an explicit function of the energy, worked out by Kronig and Penney in 1931. But a cosine lies between minus one and one. Wherever this function strays outside that strip, there's no real k: those energies are forbidden, the gaps.

Plot energy against k, and the bands are smooth curves. The levels of a ring of eight wells, computed separately, sit exactly on them, at the eight allowed values of k.

Now fill the bands with electrons. Each band holds two electrons per cell, one of each spin. With one electron per cell, a band is half full, and the electrons at the top of the filled states can move into empty ones with an arbitrarily small push: a metal. With two per cell, the band is full, and the nearest empty states are across a gap: an insulator. Alan Wilson worked this out in 1931. That's why copper conducts and diamond doesn't.

## Part 5: The density matrix and Gleason's theorem

_Scene `s16_density.py::Density`_

Part 5: Open systems.

Here are two very different kinds of uncertainty. A spin in the superposition up plus down, over root two: pointing along x. And a spin that's up or down depending on a coin toss. Measure either one along z, and you get up half the time. But measure along x, and the superposition gives plus every time, while the coin-flip spin is still fifty-fifty.

A list of states with probabilities is not a superposition, and to describe it we need a new object: the density matrix, rho, the probability-weighted sum of projectors onto the states. The Born rule becomes the trace of rho times the projector for the outcome. The superposition's density matrix has off-diagonal terms, called coherences; the coin flip's has none.

For a spin, every density matrix is one half, times one plus r dot sigma, with r a vector no longer than one. Pure states have length one: the Bloch sphere. Mixtures fill the inside: the Bloch ball. And different mixtures can give the same rho: a coin flip between up and down, or between plus x and minus x, both land at the center, and no measurement can ever tell them apart. The density matrix is everything there is to know.

Mixed states don't only come from ignorance. Take two spins in the singlet: a pure state of the pair. Ask what Alice alone can measure, by tracing out Bob's spin: summing over his possible outcomes. The result is one half times the identity, the center of the ball, with the largest entropy a spin can have. The whole is perfectly definite; each part, on its own, is completely random. That's entanglement, seen from one side.

And the density matrix closes a gap from the start of this video: why the Born rule? Suppose you assign a probability to every possible outcome of every measurement, such that for any set of mutually perpendicular outcomes, the probabilities add to one. In 1957 Andrew Gleason proved that in three or more dimensions, the only such assignments are the trace of rho times the projector, for some density matrix rho. Given the vector structure of quantum states, the Born rule is the only consistent way to get probabilities out of them.

## Decoherence: where the interference goes

_Scene `s17_decoherence.py::Decoherence`_

Why don't we see cats in superpositions? The answer starts with the density matrix. Take a qubit, a times zero plus b times one, and let it interact with its environment: a stray photon, an air molecule. If the environment ends up in a state that depends on the qubit, E zero or E one, the qubit and its environment are now entangled. Trace out the environment, and the qubit's density matrix keeps its diagonal, the probabilities, but its coherences are multiplied by the overlap of the two environment states. Once the environment has recorded the difference, the overlap is zero, and so are the coherences.

A real environment has many parts, and each takes a slightly different record. The overlap is a product of many numbers, each a little less than one. Here's a computed example: one qubit, dephased by N environment spins. With one, the coherence oscillates and comes back. With five, it comes back in bursts. With twenty, it's gone and barely flickers. With two hundred, it decays and never comes back on any time scale we could wait for. On the Bloch ball, the state's arrow shrinks onto the axis: a superposition has become a mixture.

For the double slit, the environment records which slit the electron went through, and the interference term is multiplied by the overlap of the two records. A perfect record erases the fringes completely, leaving the sum of the two single-slit patterns; a partial record leaves faded fringes. This is complementarity, made quantitative.

Here's a Schrödinger cat state, two separated packets, as a Wigner function, losing its coherence to a scattering environment. Each scattered particle multiplies the density matrix by a factor that depends on how far apart the two positions are: the off-diagonal terms decay like e to the minus Lambda t times the separation squared. The interference fringes between the blobs fade, exactly at the predicted rate, while the blobs themselves survive.

And because the rate grows with the square of the separation, big superpositions die fast. Estimates from the literature: a dust grain a hundredth of a millimeter across, in air, loses a superposition of two positions its own size apart in about ten to the minus thirty-one seconds. Even the cosmic microwave background alone would do it in about a second.

That's why the world looks classical: the environment is constantly measuring positions, and only states that survive that are ever seen. Serge Haroche's group watched it happen, step by step, with photons in a cavity in 1996. But decoherence doesn't explain why one particular outcome occurs. The density matrix ends up looking like a list of possibilities with probabilities, yet the whole state is still one superposition; which outcome we see is the measurement problem, and the interpretations of quantum mechanics part ways exactly there.

## Part 6: The Dirac equation

_Scene `s18_dirac.py::Dirac`_

Part 6: Relativity.

Everything so far has been non-relativistic. Relativity demands that energy and momentum be related by E squared equals p squared c squared plus m squared c to the fourth. The obvious move is to replace E and p by their operators. That gives the Klein-Gordon equation, but it's second order in time, so the wavefunction now no longer determines its own future, and the quantity it conserves can be negative: it can't be a probability. In 1928 Paul Dirac insisted on keeping the Schrödinger form, first order in time, which means a Hamiltonian that is linear in momentum.

So write H as c times alpha dot p, plus beta m c squared, with four unknown coefficients. Square it, carefully keeping the order of the factors. For this to equal p squared c squared plus m squared c to the fourth, each alpha and beta must square to one, and every pair must anticommute: alpha i times alpha j equals minus alpha j times alpha i. No numbers behave like that. They have to be matrices.

The smallest matrices that work are four by four, built from the Pauli matrices of Part 1. Two by two is too small: only three two-by-two matrices anticommute, and we need four. So the wavefunction has four components. Two of them are spin up and spin down: spin one half appears without being put in. The doubling comes from something new: solutions with negative energy.

The energies come in pairs: plus and minus the square root of p squared c squared plus m squared c to the fourth, two branches separated by a gap of two m c squared. The negative branch was a crisis. Dirac proposed in 1930 that those states are all filled, so that a missing one, a hole, would act like a particle of positive charge; a year later he predicted an anti-electron with the electron's mass. Carl Anderson found it in cosmic rays in 1932: the positron. Today the negative-energy solutions are understood through quantum field theory, as describing antiparticles.

Mixing the two branches has a strange consequence. Here's a packet in a one-dimensional version of the Dirac equation, half positive energy and half negative, solved exactly. Its center doesn't sit still: it trembles, at a frequency of about two m c squared over h-bar, around the center of its positive-energy part, which doesn't move at all. Schrödinger called this Zitterbewegung, trembling motion. It isn't a point particle jittering; it's the interference between the two energy branches, beating at their energy difference. For a free electron it would be far too small and fast to see, but it has been simulated with trapped ions.

## g = 2, fine structure and the Lamb shift

_Scene `s19_fine_structure.py::FineStructure`_

Dirac's equation predicts more than antimatter. At low speeds, its upper two components obey a Schrödinger equation with a twist: the kinetic energy appears as sigma dot pi, squared, over 2m, where pi is p minus q A. The Pauli matrices obey this identity: sigma dot a times sigma dot b is a dot b plus i sigma dot a cross b. And because the components of pi don't commute in a magnetic field, pi cross pi isn't zero: it's i q h-bar B. So the kinetic energy contains a magnetic term, and it says the electron's spin is a magnet with g equal to two: twice as strong, for its angular momentum, as an orbiting charge.

Measured, g is not exactly two. The best value, from a single electron held in a trap, is 2.00231930436, to thirteen digits. The small excess comes from the next layer of the theory, quantum electrodynamics: its first term, computed by Julian Schwinger in 1948, is the fine-structure constant over two pi. With the higher terms included, theory and experiment agree to about one part in a trillion.

Dirac's equation can be solved exactly for hydrogen, and the energies depend not just on n but on j, the total angular momentum including spin. Zoom in on the second level. In Schrödinger's theory it's a single energy, minus 3.4 electron volts. In Dirac's, it splits: the 2 P three-halves states sit higher by 10.94 gigahertz, about forty-five millionths of an electron volt, within a quarter of a percent of the measured splitting. But Dirac's theory leaves 2 S one-half and 2 P one-half exactly together. In 1947 Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford found them apart by about a thousand megahertz, now measured as 1057.8. Explaining that shift, the electron interacting with fluctuations of the field itself, launched quantum electrodynamics.

Each layer of the theory contains the one before it, and each new decimal place has been checked: Schrödinger's equation, Dirac's, and quantum electrodynamics, the most precisely tested theory in science.

## Recap

_Scene `s20_outro.py::Outro`_

Let's look back. The rules that Part 1 guessed came out of symmetry: time gave the Schrödinger equation, space gave the commutator, boosts gave the Hamiltonian, rotations allowed spin, and local phase demanded electromagnetism. Feynman's sum over paths gave the same equation, and in the limit of small h-bar, Newton's laws, the old quantum rules and alpha decay. Perturbation theory and the golden rule turned it into numbers for real atoms. Exchange symmetry built the periodic table and the bands of solids. Density matrices gave us the Born rule and decoherence. And relativity, through Dirac, gave spin's magnetism, fine structure and antimatter.

And one idea kept coming back: a phase is an area divided by h-bar. The area of a rectangle in phase space, when translations and boosts fail to commute; the magnetic flux through a loop, in the Aharonov-Bohm effect; and the area of a classical orbit, in the quantum condition.

As in Part 1, every picture you've seen came from solving equations, not from an artist's impression: the electron going around a flux tube, the arrows of a thrown ball's paths, an orbit in phase space, the two electrons of helium keeping apart, and a cat state about to lose its fringes.

From here, the roads lead to quantum field theory, where particles are excitations of fields and the vacuum itself shifts atomic levels; to quantum information, where entanglement is a resource; to the measurement problem, still open; and to gauge theories, where the same local-symmetry argument that gave us electromagnetism gives the other forces of nature. Thanks for watching.
