## 1. Complex derivatives are rotations

_Scene `s02_holomorphic.py::Holomorphic`_

Chapter one: what it means for a function of a complex variable to have a derivative.

A complex derivative is a limit of difference quotients, where h may approach zero from any direction in the plane. That's a much stronger demand than in two real variables. Take a small square near z zero. Under z squared, it comes out as a square again, turned and enlarged: the derivative is multiplication by the complex number two z zero, which is a rotation by 33.7 degrees and a stretch by 1.44. A real-linear map that isn't complex-linear, like z plus 0.4 i times z bar, turns the square into a rhombus. In coordinates, the Jacobian must be a rotation times a scaling: the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

Since the derivative is a rotation and a scaling, small shapes keep their angles. Here's a grid under z squared: its lines bend, but they still cross at right angles. Under the exponential, the vertical lines become circles and the horizontal ones become rays, still perpendicular. Measured at four points, the angles are ninety degrees to every digit shown. Wherever the derivative isn't zero, a holomorphic map is conformal; at a zero of the derivative, like z squared at the origin, angles get multiplied.

Two first consequences. A power series is holomorphic inside its disk of convergence, and you can differentiate it term by term: one more interchange of limits, made legal. And zeros behave. On the real line, x squared sine one over x is differentiable, and its zeros pile up at zero. A holomorphic function can't do that unless it's zero everywhere: its zeros are isolated, like the integers for sine of pi z, each a dark point with every color around it once.

## 2. Path integrals and winding numbers

_Scene `s03_integration.py::Integration`_

Chapter two: integrals along paths, and the number that counts turns.

To integrate along a path, parametrize it and integrate f of gamma times gamma prime. Around the unit circle, the powers of z all give zero, except one. One over z gives two pi i. Every other power has an antiderivative, and around a closed path an antiderivative returns to where it started. One over z would need a logarithm, and no single-valued logarithm exists around the origin. Those two pi i's are the whole story of this part.

Divide by two pi i, shift the singularity to a point a, and the integral of one over z minus a counts how many times the curve winds around a. Follow an arrow from a point inside the small loop to the moving point of the curve. By the time the curve closes, the arrow has turned exactly twice. Compute the integral at twenty points: every answer is an integer, zero, one or two, constant on each piece of the complement, and zero outside the curve.

Cauchy's theorem starts with triangles, and Goursat's proof is bisection, the fourth time in this series that halving has proved a theorem. Cut the triangle into four by its midpoints. The four boundary integrals add up to the whole, so one of them is at least a quarter of it. Keep that one, and repeat: the triangles shrink to a point z zero. Near z zero, f is linear plus a small error, and the linear part integrates to zero around any closed path. So the n-th integral is at most epsilon n times four to the minus n, with epsilon n going to zero, here for the exponential. But bisection kept at least four to the minus n of the original. The only way out: the original integral was zero. For z bar, which isn't holomorphic, the corresponding ratio stays fixed, and the argument fails, as it must. Rudin extends this from triangles to every convex set.

## 3. Cauchy's formula and its miracles

_Scene `s04_cauchy.py::CauchyFormula`_

Chapter three: Cauchy's integral formula, the engine of the whole subject.

Cauchy's formula: for a holomorphic function, the value at any point inside a circle is an integral of the values on the circle. The boundary knows the inside. Move a point a around inside. The integral and the value agree, digit for digit. At thirty random points the largest disagreement is a rounding error. At the center the formula says the value is the average over the circle.

Expand the Cauchy kernel, one over z minus a, as a geometric series, and the formula turns into a power series. The coefficients are integrals over a circle. Try it on one over one minus z minus z squared, integrating over the circle of radius one half. The integrals come out as one, one, two, three, five, eight: the Fibonacci numbers. The series converges out to the nearest pole, at 0.618, the golden ratio's reciprocal. That's the first miracle: one complex derivative gives a convergent power series, and so derivatives of every order.

Cauchy's formula also bounds derivatives: on a circle of radius R where f is at most M, the n-th derivative is at most n factorial M over R to the n. A bounded function that's holomorphic on the whole plane can take R as large as it likes, so its derivative is zero: that's Liouville's theorem. If a polynomial had no roots, one over it would be bounded and entire, hence constant. So every polynomial has a root: the fundamental theorem of algebra, the promise from Part 3. The integral of p prime over p counts the roots inside a circle: it's the winding number of the image curve around zero. For five circles it counts zero, one, two, three and five roots, exactly the roots inside.

## 4. Zeros, open maps, the maximum modulus

_Scene `s05_openmap.py::OpenMapping`_

Chapter four: what a holomorphic function's zeros and values are allowed to do.

Here's the third miracle. Suppose you know a holomorphic function only on this short white arc. Then you know it everywhere it can be continued: the power series at a point of the arc is determined by the values on the arc, and that series decides the next disk, and the next. The identity theorem: two holomorphic functions that agree on a set with a limit point agree on the whole region, because their difference has zeros that aren't isolated. Nothing like it holds on the real line.

Near any point, a nonconstant holomorphic function looks like a power: f of z minus f of z zero is z minus z zero to the m, times a nonvanishing factor. For z cubed, color a small disk in twelve sectors. Each sector opens to three times its angle, and the image disk is covered three times over: every point has three preimages, checked at two hundred random points. Either way, the image of a small disk contains a disk around f of z zero. That's the open mapping theorem: nonconstant holomorphic functions send open sets to open sets.

Here's the size of z squared plus e to the z over the closed unit disk, bright where it's large, with level curves. The largest value is on the boundary, at z equals one, where it's one plus e. Inside, even at radius 0.98, it never gets there. That's the maximum modulus principle, and the open mapping theorem proves it: near an interior point, f covers a whole disk around f of z zero, and that disk contains values of larger size. The landscape of a holomorphic function's size has no peaks, only passes.

## 5. The global Cauchy theorem and residues

_Scene `s06_residues.py::Residues`_

Chapter five: Cauchy's theorem in its global form, and the calculus of residues.

Rudin's global Cauchy theorem says a closed contour only sees what it winds around. If f is holomorphic except at isolated points, its integral is two pi i times the sum of the residues, each counted by its winding number. Use it on a real integral: one over one plus x to the fourth. In the upper half plane it has two poles. Close the interval from minus R to R with a semicircle. As R grows, the arc's contribution fades, but the total around the closed contour never changes, because the contour keeps enclosing the same two poles. Two residues, one over four z cubed at each pole, give pi over root two, and quadrature agrees to nine digits.

Two more classics. The integral of sine x over x is pi: take e to the i z over z, and let the contour step around the pole at zero with a tiny semicircle. And the Gaussian times a cosine needs no poles at all: Cauchy's theorem on a long rectangle shifts the line of integration up to height 0.7, where the integral is easy. Each answer matches quadrature to at least seven digits.

How many roots does z to the fifth plus three z plus one have inside the unit circle? On the circle, it differs from three z by z to the fifth plus one, which is at most two, while three z has size three. Picture a person walking around a tree on the circle of radius three, with a dog on a leash shorter than that. The dog must go around the tree exactly as often as the person: once. So the polynomial has as many roots inside as three z: exactly one. That's Rouché's theorem.

Rudin collects the region's side of the story in one theorem. For a region in the plane, nine conditions are equivalent: topological ones, like having no holes; analytic ones, like Cauchy's theorem for every closed path, or the existence of logarithms and square roots, or approximation by polynomials, which is Runge's theorem, two chapters on. The square roots will build the Riemann mapping.

## 6. The Schwarz lemma and three lines

_Scene `s07_schwarz.py::Schwarz`_

Chapter six: the maximum principle, used as a tool.

Take a holomorphic map of the unit disk into itself that keeps the center fixed. The Schwarz lemma: it can't move any point farther from the center. Here, three circles and their images: each image stays inside its circle. And the derivative at zero is at most one in size; here it's one half. The proof is one line: f of z over z is holomorphic, and by the maximum principle it's at most one over r on the circle of radius r, so at most one in the limit. Only rotations achieve equality.

The lemma pins down every symmetry of the disk. The map z minus alpha over one minus alpha bar z slides the point alpha to the center, keeps the circle on itself, and bends the polar grid into circles that are no longer concentric. Combined with rotations, these are all the conformal self-maps of the disk: apply Schwarz to any other and it must be a rotation.

The maximum principle has a quantitative cousin on a strip. Let M of x be the largest size of f on the vertical line at x. Hadamard's three lines theorem: the log of M is a convex function of x. Here it lies below its chord, so the size in the middle is at most the geometric mean of the sizes on the two edges. On an unbounded strip some growth condition is needed, which is what Phragmén and Lindelöf made precise. The payoff comes later: Riesz and Thorin turned this into interpolation of operator norms between L p spaces, and Part 9 uses it for the Fourier transform.

## 7. Runge's theorem

_Scene `s08_runge.py::Runge`_

Chapter seven: Runge's theorem, Weierstrass's approximation theorem for the complex plane.

Can polynomials approximate one over z, uniformly on a compact set K that avoids zero? It depends on the shape of K. On a horseshoe, yes. The best fits improve steadily, from 1.5 at degree four to two ten-thousandths at degree one twenty-eight. On the full annulus, never: the error is stuck at 1.33 whatever the degree. The reason is an integral. Around the unit circle, one over z integrates to two pi i and every polynomial integrates to zero, so the difference can't be uniformly small. The pole at zero is trapped in the hole; in the horseshoe it can escape through the gap.

That's Runge's theorem. A function holomorphic near a compact set K is a uniform limit of rational functions, with poles placed anywhere you like, one in each piece of the complement; if the complement is connected, polynomials suffice. The proof: Cauchy's formula on a cycle around K, approximated by a Riemann sum, gives a sum of simple poles. Then push each pole: a pole at a is a convergent series of poles at a nearby b, so it can hop, step by step, along a path in the complement. Out at infinity, a pole becomes a polynomial. Part 10 needs exactly this for its functional calculus.

## 8. Normal families

_Scene `s09_montel.py::NormalFamilies`_

Chapter eight: Montel's theorem, where Arzelà and Ascoli pay off in the complex plane.

On the real line, sine n x stays between minus one and one, but gets steeper and steeper: bounded, and not equicontinuous, as Part 3 showed. In the complex plane the same functions aren't even bounded: on the imaginary axis sine becomes hyperbolic sine, and on the disk of radius one half, sine of twelve z reaches two hundred. For holomorphic functions a bound is also a speed limit: Cauchy's estimate bounds the derivative on a smaller disk by the bound on the larger one. So a uniformly bounded family is automatically equicontinuous on compact sets.

Here's a bounded family on the disk: a rotating mixture of z and z squared, plus z to the n, over two. Pick n so that n is nearly a multiple of two pi: six, forty-four, seven hundred and ten. The functions converge to z over two. Brightness shows the error. It retreats to a thinner and thinner rim: on the disk of radius 0.9 it drops to ten to the minus five, but right at the edge, z to the n keeps it at one half. Convergence is uniform on every compact subset, not on the whole disk. Montel's theorem: every uniformly bounded family of holomorphic functions has such a subsequence.

Montel's theorem is Arzelà and Ascoli in the complex plane, with equicontinuity coming for free. And the limits stay holomorphic, by Morera's theorem. In Part 3 the payoff was existence of solutions to differential equations. Here it's the Riemann mapping theorem.

## 9. The Riemann mapping theorem

_Scene `s10_riemann.py::RiemannMapping`_

Chapter nine: the Riemann mapping theorem, computed for a real polygon.

The Riemann mapping theorem: every simply connected region of the plane, other than the plane itself, can be mapped one-to-one and conformally onto the disk. The proof is an extremal problem. Look at all one-to-one maps of the region into the disk that send a chosen point to zero. There is one, built from a square root, which exists because the region is simply connected. Among them, pick the one with the largest derivative at the point. Montel's theorem guarantees a maximizer. And it must be onto: if it missed a point, another square-root trick would produce a map with a larger derivative. Compactness solves the extremal problem, and the solution is the map.

The theorem says such a map exists; for a polygon we can compute it. Schwarz and Christoffel found its form: the derivative is a product of powers, one for each corner, with exponents set by the angles. The unknowns are the six points on the circle that go to the corners, solved for numerically so that the side lengths come out right. Here's the result. The polar grid of the disk flows onto the L-shaped hexagon: circles become nested loops, rays become curves that bend around the inner corner. Every little square stays a square: the grid lines meet at ninety degrees to within ten to the minus seven, and the center goes to the point 0.8 plus 0.8 i. Nothing about the theorem's proof constructs this map, but the map is really there.

## 10. A tour: harmonic functions

_Scene `s11_harmonic.py::Harmonic`_

Chapter ten begins a short tour of the rest of the complex half of Rudin's book, with results stated rather than proved. First, harmonic functions.

The real part of a holomorphic function is harmonic: its Laplacian is zero. Think of it as a steady temperature. Fix the temperature on the boundary circle, here a pattern with three warm and three cool arcs. Then there's exactly one harmonic function inside with those boundary values, given by Poisson's integral. Its Laplacian, checked at random points, is zero to within ten to the minus four, the error of the finite difference. At the center, the temperature is the average around the edge: 0.3. That's the mean value property, the harmonic version of Cauchy's formula.

Poisson's kernel is an old friend: positive, average one, and narrowing as r approaches one, the same bump as the Landau and Fejér kernels. That's why the solution takes the right boundary values. And there's a probabilistic reading, due to Kakutani: the temperature at a point is the average boundary temperature where a random walk started there first hits the circle.

## 11. A tour: products and continuation

_Scene `s12_products.py::ZerosProducts`_

Chapter eleven, still on tour: functions built from their zeros, and functions continued along paths.

A polynomial is a product over its roots. Euler guessed that sine of pi z should be one too, over the integers. Build the product one pair of zeros at a time: each factor plants two more dark points, and the picture fills in toward the sine. The error shrinks by a factor of ten for every factor of ten in the number of terms. Weierstrass proved that every entire function is such a product, once convergence factors are attached, and that any sequence of points going to infinity is the zero set of some entire function.

In the disk, zeros can pile up toward the edge, but not too fast. Here's a Blaschke product, built from forty zeros spiraling outward. Each factor has size one on the circle, so the product stays bounded. What makes it converge is that the zeros approach the boundary quickly enough: the distances to the circle have a finite sum. For a bounded function, that's also necessary: by Jensen's formula, every zero costs size.

The identity theorem makes continuation unique along any path. Follow the logarithm around the origin twice. Its imaginary part, the angle, climbs without ever returning: two pi after one turn, four pi after two. The continued logarithm lives on a spiral staircase. The monodromy theorem says paths that can be deformed into each other give the same result, and the modular function proves Picard's little theorem: a nonconstant entire function takes every complex value, with at most one exception.

## 12. A tour: H^p and the shift

_Scene `s13_hp.py::Hp`_

Chapter twelve, the last stop of the tour: Hardy spaces, and the first operator of the series.

Which functions on the circle are boundary values of holomorphic functions on the disk? Expand one in a Fourier series. A power series has only nonnegative frequencies, so on the circle the negative half of the spectrum is empty. The Hardy space H two is exactly that: square-integrable functions on the circle whose negative Fourier coefficients vanish, or equivalently, power series with square-summable coefficients.

Multiply by z. On the coefficients, that moves everything one step to the right and leaves the first slot empty. Do it again, and again. Lengths never change: the shift is an isometry. But it isn't onto: nothing lands on the constant one. An isometry of a space onto a proper part of itself can't happen in finite dimensions. This operator will be the star of Part 10, where its spectrum is the whole closed disk.

Which closed subspaces does the shift map into themselves? Beurling's answer: exactly the multiples of an inner function, a bounded holomorphic function with size one on the circle. This one has no zeros at all, yet it presses everything near the point one down to nothing: an essential singularity on the boundary. It's the first theorem of the series about the invariant subspaces of an operator, and the tour ends here. Part 9 will use the Paley-Wiener theorem from the same part of the book.
