## Every equation you can solve, you solve by a contraction

_Scene `s01_hook.py::Hook`_

Three rows of the ledger are filled: the real line, limits, and the first space of functions. Part 4 takes calculus into many dimensions, and it runs on one idea.

Here are two equations in two unknowns: a circle and a hyperbola, and we want a point where they cross. Newton's method starts from a guess. At the guess, replace each curve by its tangent line, the zero set of its best linear approximation. Two lines are easy: they cross at one point, the next guess. Repeat. After four steps the error is ten to the minus eleven. Once close, the number of correct digits doubles at every step.

Behind Newton's method is a simpler picture. Take a map of the plane that shrinks every distance by a fixed factor, here 0.62, while it turns and slides. Apply it again and again, and the whole plane collapses onto one point, the only point the map leaves where it is. That's the contraction principle, and this part uses it four times: for Newton's method, for differential equations, and for the inverse and implicit function theorems. Every equation you can solve, you solve by a contraction.

Here's the plan, following chapter nine of Rudin's Principles. First the linear maps, and the derivative as a linear map, and why partial derivatives alone don't make one. Then the contraction principle, and what it builds: solutions of differential equations, and the inverse and implicit function theorems. We finish with mixed partial derivatives, one more swap of limits, and a guided tour of chapter ten.

## 1. Linear maps and their norms

_Scene `s02_linear.py::LinearMaps`_

Chapter one: linear maps, the raw material of derivatives in many variables.

A linear map of the plane respects addition and scaling. Watch what it does to a square grid: lines go to lines, equally spaced lines stay equally spaced, and the origin stays put. It's determined by where it sends the two unit vectors, and those images are the columns of its matrix.

How big is a linear map? Rudin measures it by the largest stretch: the norm of A is the supremum of the length of A x over unit vectors x. Run a unit vector around the circle and watch its image: the length of A x rises and falls. The images trace an ellipse, and the norm is its longest semi-axis, here about 2.03; the shortest is about 0.73. The norm is at most the square root of the sum of the squared entries, and it behaves like a length: the triangle inequality holds, and the norm of a product is at most the product of the norms. So the linear maps form a metric space of their own.

Which linear maps can be undone? Rudin's theorem 9.8: the invertible ones form an open set. Around an invertible A there's a ball of radius one over the norm of A inverse, and every map in it is invertible. That radius is the shortest semi-axis of A's ellipse. Push A along its weakest direction and the ellipse flattens. It collapses to a segment, and the map becomes singular, exactly when the push reaches the radius: the ball is as large as it can be.

The proof is a geometric series. Write B as A times the identity minus a small map; then B inverse is the sum of the powers of that small map, times A inverse, just as one over one minus q is the sum of the powers of q. The partial sums, drawn as images of the unit circle, close in on B inverse's ellipse, with the error shrinking geometrically. This little series comes back in Part 10, where it runs the theory of the spectrum.

## 2. The derivative is a linear map

_Scene `s03_derivative.py::Derivative`_

Chapter two: what the derivative becomes when there is more than one variable.

Here's a function of two variables, drawn by its level curves, the way a map shows a landscape. Zoom in at a point. The curves straighten, and their spacing evens out. In the limit they become the level lines of a linear function: parallel, and equally spaced. That linear function is the derivative. In one variable, a curve under the microscope becomes a line; in two, a landscape becomes a tilted plane.

Rudin's definition: f is differentiable at x if some linear map A approximates the change in f so well that the error is small even compared with the size of the step: the error divided by the length of h goes to zero. That A is unique, and it is the derivative. Check it at our point. For each step length, try two hundred directions and record the worst ratio. On a log scale the ratios fall along a line of slope one: the error is of the order of the step squared, far smaller than the step itself.

With derivatives as linear maps, the chain rule says: the derivative of a composition is the composition of the derivatives, which for matrices is their product. Follow a small square. The first map bends it into a curvy quadrilateral, nearly the parallelogram its derivative predicts. The second map bends it again, and the result is nearly the parallelogram of the product of the two matrices.

In coordinates, the matrix of the derivative is the Jacobian, the array of partial derivatives. For a real-valued function it's a single row, the gradient. On the level-curve map the gradient points uphill, perpendicular to the curves, longest where they crowd together. The rate of change in a direction u is the gradient dotted with u. As u turns, that's a pure cosine of the angle: a linear function of the direction. Remember this shape: in the next chapter, a function will fail it.

So the dictionary from one variable to several: the derivative stops being a number and becomes a linear map; the tangent line becomes the best linear approximation; the chain rule multiplies matrices instead of numbers; and the mean value inequality survives word for word, with the norm of the derivative in place of the absolute value.

## 3. Partial derivatives are not enough

_Scene `s04_partials.py::Partials`_

Chapter three: two exhibits for the museum, and the theorem that repairs the damage.

The first exhibit: x y over x squared plus y squared, with value zero at the origin. Its colors show its values, and they depend only on the direction from the origin. Along both axes the function is identically zero, so both partial derivatives at the origin exist, and both are zero. But along the diagonal it equals one half, right up to the origin. So it isn't even continuous there, let alone differentiable. Partial derivatives only look along the axes, and a function can misbehave in every other direction.

The second exhibit is subtler: x squared y over x squared plus y squared. It is continuous, and at the origin it has a derivative in every direction, not just along the axes. Plot that directional derivative against the angle of the direction. If g were differentiable, this would be the gradient dotted with u, a pure cosine. The gradient is zero, so it would be flat. It isn't. Cos squared times sine is a quarter of sine theta plus a quarter of sine three theta, and the sine three theta part is not linear in the direction. No linear map fits, so there is no derivative: directional derivatives in every direction are not enough either.

The repair is Rudin's theorem 9.21: if the partial derivatives exist and are continuous, the function is continuously differentiable. The idea: to go from x to x plus h, move one coordinate at a time. On each leg only one variable changes, so the one-variable mean value theorem gives the change as a partial derivative at some point of that leg, times the step. If the partials are continuous, their values at those points are close to their values at x, and the change is the gradient dotted with h, up to an error small compared with h. In the museum, the partials jump at the origin, and the argument breaks.

## 4. The contraction principle

_Scene `s05_contraction.py::Contraction`_

Chapter four: the contraction principle, the engine of this part.

To solve cosine of x equals x, just iterate: start anywhere, apply the cosine, and apply it again. On the graph, bounce between the curve and the diagonal: the path spirals in on the crossing. Why it works: on this interval the cosine's slope is at most sine of one, about 0.84, so each application shrinks distances by that factor. The errors shrink geometrically, and the theorem we're about to prove even predicts a bound for them in advance, the last column. The solution is 0.7390851332, the only one there is.

The theorem, Rudin 9.23: on a complete metric space, a map that shrinks every distance by a factor c less than one has exactly one fixed point. Start anywhere and iterate. Each step is at most c times the one before, so the n-th step is at most c to the n times the first. Adding up the steps between the n-th point and any later one gives a geometric series: every later point lies in a disc of radius c to the n over one minus c times the first step. The discs shrink to nothing, so the sequence is Cauchy. The space is complete, so the points converge, and because the map is continuous, the limit is fixed. And two fixed points would have to be closer to each other than they are, so there's only one.

Completeness can't be dropped. The map x over two plus one over x sends rationals to rationals, and on the interval from one to two it shrinks distances by at least half. Iterate from one: three halves, seventeen twelfths, five hundred seventy-seven over four hundred eight. The iterates form a Cauchy sequence, but the point they close in on is the square root of two, the hole in the rationals from Part 1. Among the rationals, there is no fixed point at all.

And Newton's method from the opening is a contraction too. Its step is the map g of x equals x minus f over f prime, and at a simple root its derivative is zero. For the square root of two, g is flat where it crosses the diagonal, so near the root it shrinks distances by a factor as small as we like. That's why Newton's method is so fast: each step contracts harder than the one before, the error squares, and the number of correct digits doubles.

## 5. Picard: differential equations in C[a, b]

_Scene `s06_picard.py::Picard`_

Chapter five: the function space of Part 3 earns its keep.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, a differential equation with an initial value is the same as an integral equation. Read the right side as a machine T that takes a continuous function y and returns another one. Solutions are exactly the functions T leaves unchanged. If F is Lipschitz in y with constant L, then T shrinks sup distances by the factor L times h, the length of the interval. Here are two functions and their images: the largest gap shrinks to less than half. For h small, T is a contraction of the complete space C of zero h, Part 3's space, and the contraction principle hands us a solution, and only one.

Watch it work on the simplest equation, y prime equals y, starting from y equal to one. Start the iteration from the zero function. The first iterate is the constant one, the next is one plus t, then one plus t plus t squared over two. The Picard iterates are the Taylor polynomials of e to the t, and they converge to it uniformly. On the camera, each iterate is a single point, plotted by its values at one half and one, and the points march to the point of the exponential function.

Now an equation with no formula for its solution: y prime equals t plus sine y. The same iteration closes in on the solution, drawn in white from an independent numerical method. On the whole interval from zero to one, L times h is one, so T is not quite a contraction there, but a sharper estimate shows the k-th change is at most h to the k over k factorial times the first, which is even better than geometric. After twelve steps the iterate agrees with the numerical solution to about eight digits.

The Lipschitz condition is not a technicality. y prime equals twice the square root of y, starting from zero. The zero function solves it. So does t squared. So does the function that waits until any time c and then follows t minus c squared. Infinitely many solutions through one point. Peano's theorem, proved with Arzelà–Ascoli from Part 3, still gives existence for any continuous right side, but uniqueness needs the Lipschitz bound that makes T a contraction.

## 6. The inverse function theorem

_Scene `s07_inverse.py::InverseFunction`_

Chapter six: the inverse function theorem, built from one contraction.

Here's the map that sends x, y to e to the x times cosine y, e to the x times sine y: the complex exponential, seen as a map of the plane. It wraps the strip on the left around the origin: vertical lines become circles, horizontal lines become rays. Its derivative is invertible everywhere, and near each point the map is one-to-one: a small square goes to a small curved square. But a square two pi higher lands in exactly the same place. The map can be undone near every point, and not globally. The inverse function theorem is precisely the local statement.

The theorem, Rudin 9.24: if f is continuously differentiable near a and its derivative at a is invertible, then f maps some neighborhood of a one-to-one onto a neighborhood of f of a, and the inverse is continuously differentiable too, with derivative the inverse matrix. The proof has four steps, and the first carries the weight. To solve f of x equals y, start at a and correct x by the linear guess: add A inverse times the remaining error. A fixed point of that correction is exactly a solution. Its derivative is the identity minus A inverse times f prime of x, which is zero at a and small nearby. So on a small ball it's a contraction, and the contraction principle finds the solution, and only one.

Here it is, run. Choose a target y near f of a. Start at a and apply the correction again and again. On the left, the iterates in the domain; on the right, their images, closing in on y. On the green ball the correction map shrinks distances by at most about a third, so each error is about a third of the one before. If we updated A at every step, using the derivative at the current point, this would be Newton's method from the opening, and it would converge even faster.

## 7. The implicit function theorem

_Scene `s08_implicit.py::ImplicitFunction`_

Chapter seven: when an equation can be solved for some of its variables.

The equation x cubed plus y cubed equals three x y defines a curve, the folium of Descartes. Does it define y as a function of x? Not globally, but often locally. Near the point two thirds, four thirds, a small window contains a piece of the curve that every vertical line meets exactly once: there it's the graph of a function g. Its slope is minus the ratio of the two partial derivatives, here 0.8. At the fold, the curve turns back, and vertical lines meet it twice or not at all: there's no function of x there. That's exactly where the partial derivative in y vanishes. And at the origin both partials vanish, and two branches cross.

The theorem, Rudin 9.28, here with one variable on each side: if f vanishes at a, b, and its partial derivative in y is not zero there, then near a the equation defines y as exactly one continuously differentiable function of x. It comes straight from the inverse function theorem. Build a map of the plane that keeps x and records the value of f. Its derivative is a triangular matrix whose determinant is the partial derivative in y, so the inverse function theorem inverts it near a, b. The points where f is zero are the points that map to the x-axis, so the inverse, applied to the x-axis, traces the graph. Differentiating the identity f of x, g of x equals zero gives the slope.

The same circle of ideas gives Rudin's rank theorem, which we'll only picture. If the derivative of a map has the same rank everywhere, then in suitable coordinates the map is just a projection. Here the rank is one: a whole family of lines in the plane, each collapsed to a single point, and the plane folded onto a curve.

## 8. Mixed partials, and differentiating under the integral

_Scene `s09_higher.py::HigherOrder`_

Chapter eight: two more interchanges of limits, one that fails and one that works.

In Rudin's Exercise 9.27, a function has two mixed partial derivatives at the origin that disagree. Both come from one quantity: the second difference across a small rectangle with corners at the origin, divided by its area. For this function that quotient depends only on the rectangle's shape. A tall, thin rectangle gives minus one: that's differentiating in x first, then in y. A short, wide one gives plus one: the other order. The two iterated limits disagree, the same way as the grid of m over m plus n from Part 3.

The cure is continuity again. Rudin's theorem 9.41: if the mixed partial f x y exists near a point and is continuous there, then the other one exists and they're equal. In the museum piece, f x y is plus one along the x-axis and minus one along the y-axis, so it can't be continuous at the origin. The swap fails exactly where continuity does.

The swap that works is differentiation under the integral sign, Rudin 9.42: if phi and its partial derivative in t are continuous on a closed rectangle, the derivative of the integral is the integral of the derivative. Try it on the integral of x to the t from zero to one, which is one over t plus one. Differentiating the answer gives minus one over t plus one squared; integrating the derivative of the integrand, x to the t times log x, gives the same numbers. A derivative is a limit, so this is one more interchange of limits, made legal by the uniform continuity of Part 3. It's the swap Part 4 adds to the ledger.

## 9. A tour of integration in many variables

_Scene `s10_forms.py::FormsTour`_

Chapter nine is a guided tour of Rudin's tenth chapter, integration in many variables. We'll state and picture the main results, and check them numerically, but we won't prove them.

First, the change of variables formula. When a map T reshapes a region, integrals pick up a factor: the absolute value of the determinant of T's derivative. Here's why. Cut the region into small squares; each becomes, nearly, the parallelogram of the derivative, whose area is the determinant times the square's area. Computed for all sixty-four cells, the ratio of the true area to the determinant's prediction is one, to within a few millionths.

Second, partitions of unity: smooth bumps, each living on a small piece of the space, that add up to exactly one. They let us cut an integral into local pieces, do each piece in convenient coordinates, and add the results.

The summit of the chapter is Stokes' theorem: the integral of a form over the boundary of a region equals the integral of its exterior derivative over the region. Green's theorem, the divergence theorem and the classical Stokes theorem are all special cases. In the plane, with the right form, it says that the area of a region is a line integral around its edge. Walk once around the loop of the folium from chapter seven, and the line integral gives its area: exactly three halves.

Last stop: a form with zero exterior derivative, a closed form, need not be the derivative of a function, an exact form. The classic example is the angle form in the plane with the origin removed. A loop that misses the hole gives zero. A loop around the hole gives two pi, which no exact form could: the angle can't be defined continuously all the way around. On a convex set this can't happen, which is Poincaré's lemma. Holes are detected by integrals: the beginning of topology, and a preview of Part 7's winding numbers.

## The fourth row of the ledger

Let's take stock. Linear maps have a norm, the largest stretch, and the invertible ones form an open set. The derivative is the linear map you see when you zoom in, and partial derivatives make one only when they're continuous. The contraction principle solves equations by iteration in any complete space: differential equations in the space of continuous functions, and the inverse and implicit function theorems. Mixed partial derivatives commute when they're continuous, and derivatives pass through integrals. And chapter ten's tour ended at Stokes' theorem, where integrals detect holes.

The ledger's fourth row. The spaces: R n, and the continuous functions again. Completeness now pays off as fixed points of contractions. Compact sets are the same as before. The new swap: a derivative may pass through an integral when everything is continuous on a closed box. And Fourier's question hasn't moved; it waits for a better integral.
