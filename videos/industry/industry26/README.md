# Industry, Built: Part 26, Property

Part 26 of a sixty-four-part series on how each industry works (see [the series plan](../README.md) and
[the syllabus](../SYLLABUS.md)), the third of Act IV, *Building*. It asks: what is a building worth, and why does that
number move so much when the building does not? It answers with the Forms 10-K for 2025 of Prologis, BXP, Simon Property
Group, Realty Income, SL Green, Hudson Pacific, American Tower, Equinix, Blackstone and CBRE; BXP's Forms 10-K since 2017
and its third-quarter 2026 results; Realty Income's Forms 10-K since 2015; WeWork's registration statement of August
2019 and its bankruptcy filing of November 2023; the Federal Reserve's Financial Stability Reports of October 2023 and
November 2026; Gupta, Mittal and Van Nieuwerburgh's study of offices after the pandemic (2022); the SEC company facts of
thirty-four landlords, managers, services firms, a lender and WeWork; the Federal Reserve's commercial property price
index, bank loans and delinquencies, the Treasury yield, the Census Bureau's construction spending and e-commerce share
and the BLS's rents (all via FRED); and the Works' building. The answer: a building is worth its income divided by a
rate, and at low rates small changes in the rate move the price a great deal; its income moves more than its rent,
because its costs are fixed; what a landlord really owns is its leases, and a long promise from a strong tenant is worth
most; and when income and the rate both move against a building, its loan decides who loses. Warehouses filled as
offices emptied, and the same arithmetic made one richer and left the other worth less than its loan.

It opens on the Federal Reserve's commercial property price index (2001 = 100): 175 in mid-2007, down 39% by the end of
2009; 233 in the spring of 2023, down 13% in two years, up 9% by mid-2026; on Gupta, Mittal and Van Nieuwerburgh's
estimate that New York's offices lost 45% of their value in 2020 and 39% in the long run ($454bn for all American offices);
and on value = net operating income / capitalization rate. From there it covers:

## What is computed, and from what

`compute.py` reads the Filing Room and the Register. The building is the Works' (`explainer/industry/works/building.py`):
net operating income is the rent roll (square feet × rent × share let) less fixed running costs, and value is that income
over the capitalization rate. The loan is interest-only and due at maturity; a lender lends the least of 60% of value, the
amount whose interest at 7% the income covers 1.25 times, and ten times the income (a 10% debt yield). The 2019 office
(400,000 sq ft, $50, 95%, $9.0m of costs, 5%) becomes in 2026 a building 65% let at $48 with $9.5m of costs, valued at
7.5%; the warehouse (1,000,000 sq ft, $11 to $16.50, 97% to 95%, about $0.7m of costs, 4.5% to 5.5%). Loans are 65% of the
2019 price at 4%. The one-point move is r / (r + 0.01) - 1. A development's margin is the yield on cost over the cap rate,
less one, so Prologis's 6.7% yield and 25.4% margin imply 6.7 / 1.254 = 5.34%. The price index is the Federal Reserve's Z.1
series rebased to the first quarter of 2001; the real index divides it by the CPI-U. Realty Income's buying yields are the
initial cash yields (2019-2025) or contractual lease rates (2015-2018) it reports on each year's investments. Annual series
are averages of monthly or quarterly values. Margins are net income attributable to the parent over revenue, the median of
the last five fiscal years.

## Problems and solutions

**1.** *A building earns $10m of net operating income. What is it worth at a 4% cap rate, and at 5%? What share of its
value does the one-point rise take?*

*Solution.* 10 / 0.04 = $250m; 10 / 0.05 = $200m. The fall is 50 / 250 = 20%, which is 0.04 / 0.05 - 1. From 7.5% to 8.5%
the same point takes only 1 - 7.5 / 8.5 = 12%: the lower the rate, the more each point matters.

**2.** *An office of 400,000 square feet lets at $50 a square foot and costs $9m a year to run whether let or not. Below
what share let does it earn nothing? What does it earn at 95% and at 65%?*

*Solution.* Income is 400,000 × 50 × s / 1,000,000 - 9 = 20s - 9 ($m), zero at s = 45%. At 95%: 19 - 9 = $10m. At 65%:
13 - 9 = $4m: about a third fewer tenants, 60% less income. (With 2026's $48 rent and $9.5m of costs, the break-even is
9.5 / 19.2 = 49%.)

**3.** *The building earns $2.98m and is valued at 7.5%. A lender at 7% will lend the least of 60% of value, the amount
whose interest the income covers 1.25 times, and ten times the income. How much, and how large is the gap to a $130m
loan?*

*Solution.* Value 2.98 / 0.075 = $39.7m; 60% of that is $23.8m. Interest cover: 2.98 / (1.25 × 0.07) = $34.1m. Debt
yield: 2.98 / 0.10 = $29.8m. The lender offers $23.8m; the owner must find 130 - 23.8 = $106.2m.

**4.** *Prologis reported a 6.7% stabilized yield and a 25.4% development margin on the buildings it finished in 2025.
What cap rate do these imply, and what is a $100m development worth?*

*Solution.* Margin = yield / cap - 1, so cap = 6.7% / 1.254 = 5.34%. The development earns $6.7m a year, worth 6.7 /
0.0534 = $125.4m: $25.4m more than it cost.

**5.** *WeWork owed $47.2bn of future rent on leases with about 14 years left, and its members had committed for a little
over 15 months on average. Roughly what share of its rent obligations was matched by its members' commitments?*

*Solution.* If the obligations ran evenly over 14 years, about 47.2 / 14 = $3.4bn a year was owed. Members' commitments
covered about 15 / 168 = 9% of the term, so at most about a tenth of the obligations was matched by promises to pay; the
rest depended on members renewing. (This treats rents as level and members' commitments as running from the same date;
it is a rough comparison, not WeWork's own figure.)

## Glossary

- **Net operating income (NOI):** a building's rent and other income less its running costs, before interest, tax and
  depreciation.
- **Capitalization (cap) rate:** a building's net operating income divided by its price; the yield a buyer accepts.
- **Net lease:** a lease in which the tenant also pays the property's taxes, insurance and maintenance.
- **Occupancy:** the share of a building's space let to paying tenants (leased also counts leases signed but not begun).
- **Mark-to-market:** the gap between today's market rents and the rents tenants pay under their existing leases.
- **Loan to value:** a loan as a share of the building's value.
- **Debt service coverage:** a building's income divided by its loan payments.
- **Debt yield:** a building's income divided by its loan.
- **CMBS:** commercial mortgage-backed securities, bonds backed by pools of commercial property loans.
- **REIT:** a real estate investment trust, a company that pays no corporate income tax if it distributes at least 90% of
  its taxable income.
- **Stabilized yield, development margin:** a new building's income once let, over its cost; its value over its cost, less
  one.
- **Impairment:** a write-down of an asset's book value to what it is now expected to be worth.
