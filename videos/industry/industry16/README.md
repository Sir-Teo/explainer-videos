# Industry, Built: Part 16, Electronics

Part 16 of a sixty-four-part series on how each industry works (see [the series plan](../README.md) and
[the syllabus](../SYLLABUS.md)), the second of Act III, *Making*. It asks: why does the company that builds the device keep
the least of it? It answers with Stan Shih's own account of the smile curve (his oral history at the Computer History
Museum); Hon Hai's audited statements for 2025; the annual reports of Apple, Intel, HP, Dell, Jabil, Flex, Sanmina,
Celestica, Plexus, Benchmark, Supermicro, Amphenol, TE Connectivity, Corning and Logitech, and of Sony, LG Display and ASE;
UN Comtrade's figures for America's imports of phones, laptops, servers and televisions; TrendForce's estimates of a phone's
parts cost; the BLS's price indexes for
televisions, computers and smartphones and its count of electronics jobs; the Section 301 notices in the Federal Register;
and the Works' device and assembler. The answer: a device is built from other companies' parts, put together by contract
manufacturers who pass those parts through their revenue and keep two or three cents in the dollar, for brands that hold
the customer and can move the work; the ends of the chain keep more when they own something that cannot be swapped, a part
nobody else makes or a name with services attached; and tariffs moved the assembly, slowly, from China to India, Vietnam and
Mexico rather than to America.

It opens in 1992, when Shih analysed 'the value-add imparted by each segment of the entire industry' and found that 'it
turned out to result in a smile-shaped curve', with manufacturing 'the lowest in terms of value'. Then two sets of accounts:
Hon Hai took in NT$8.1 trillion in 2025, about $260 billion, and kept 2.3 cents in the dollar; Apple took in $479 billion in
its fiscal 2026 and kept 28 cents. From there it covers:

- Run it: an assembler offered 20 million devices a year for three years at $30 each, if it builds an $800m plant: worth
  -$194m today if the brand moves the work after three years and +$420m if it renews, so building pays only above a 32%
  chance of renewal; a plant in a new country, with half the labour cost and a slower start, pays only above about 50%;
  if the brand pays for half the plant, the same three years leave the assembler $206m up instead of $194m down;
- from design to box: a surface-mount line (solder paste, pick and place, the reflow oven, inspection), final assembly, test
  and the box; Apple's hardware 'manufactured by outsourcing partners that are located primarily in China mainland, India,
  Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam'; Flex's 149,686 people in more than 100 facilities in about 30 countries;
  141 million smartphones (average declared value $372) and 100 million laptops ($492) imported into the US in 2025;
- where the device's dollar goes: the Works' $900 device, $450 of parts (their makers keeping $112), a $30 fee for assembly
  and test, $160 of the brand's own costs and $260 of brand profit, drawn as a smile; a $300 device whose brand keeps $18,
  6% of the price; and in real accounts, Amphenol 18.5
  cents in the dollar, Hon Hai 2.3, Jabil 2.2, Apple 28, HP and Dell a few;
- contract manufacturing: six assemblers' net margins since 2010, almost all between 0 and 5 cents; turnkey revenue (parts
  plus fee) against consigned (fee only): the same $300m of profit is a 3% or a 50% margin; Hon Hai's 2.3% margin, 1.59
  asset turnover and 2.59 leverage make a 9.6% return on equity; 53 days of inventory; 1.5% of revenue on R&D; Flex's
  people by region (61,841 in the Americas, 60,870 in Asia, 26,975 in Europe);
- Hon Hai in its own numbers: cloud and networking up 63% to NT$3.26 trillion in 2025, overtaking smart consumer
  electronics (NT$3.08 trillion); its largest customer, 'Client E', 54% of revenue in 2024 and 44% in 2025; its servers arm
  earning 4.7 cents of operating income per dollar against 1.4 for the rest; its revenue by where customers are registered
  (the USA 45%, Singapore 22%, Ireland 11%, China 5%);
- the brand's side: Apple's products at a 39.3% gross margin and its services at 76.2%; Apple's $132bn of profit against
  Hon Hai's $6bn; services 26% of Apple's sales but 40% of its gross margin; Jabil's largest customer 20% of its revenue;
- components with moats: Amphenol, TE Connectivity, Corning and ASE since 2012, and LG Display, which lost money in six of

  electronics jobs halved;
- the PC, the earlier case: Intel's PC chips at 29-35 cents of operating income per dollar, HP's PCs at 5-6, Dell's at 6-8,

  and component makers by revenue and five-year margin, and what to watch;
- *Called it?*: the Commerce Secretary's forecast of April 2025 that iPhone assembly 'is going to come to America' (not so
  far: imports held at $52bn and moved from China to India; electronics jobs fell) and this series' call that India will
  supply more than half of America's smartphone imports in 2026 (55%): India needs about 38% of September-December's
  imports, against 38.5% a year earlier;
- the smile, measured: the 2025 accounts in a row, and the same smile in a server (Nvidia's 71% gross margin, Hon
  Hai's servers arm 4.7%, Dell's server group 11.7%).

## What is computed, and from what

`compute.py` reads the Filing Room and the Register. Run it, the device and the assembler are the Works'
(`explainer/industry/works/device.py`), with illustrative parameters labelled on screen; values are discounted at 8% a
year, and the renewal's value is weighted by its probability. Net margins are net income over revenue from the companies'
10-Ks and 20-Fs, by fiscal year (a year ending in January to March counts as the calendar year before); Plexus's XBRL
revenue for 2011-2014 is not its total revenue and those years are left out. Hon Hai's figures are its audited statements
in NT$, converted at the Federal Reserve's 2025 average of NT$31.17 to the dollar; Sony's, LG Display's and ASE's at the
Federal Reserve's annual averages. The DuPont terms divide Hon Hai's profit to owners by revenue, revenue by total assets,
and total assets by total equity; inventory days divide inventories by operating costs over 365 days. Trade shares divide
US imports from a partner by imports from the world (UN Comtrade, as reported by the United States); phones are HS 851712 to
2021 and 851713 from 2022. The television's real price divides the BLS's index by the all-items CPI, both annual averages.

## Problems and solutions

**1.** *An assembler makes $12 of profit on each device. The parts cost $400 and its fee is $25. What is its margin if it
buys the parts and resells them (turnkey), and if the brand supplies them (consigned)?*

*Solution.* Turnkey revenue is $425 a device: 12 / 425 = 2.8%. Consigned revenue is the $25 fee: 12 / 25 = 48%. The
profit is the same; only what counts as revenue changes. An assembler's thin margin says as much about its bookkeeping as
about its business.

**2.** *An assembler keeps 2.5 cents per dollar of sales, turns its assets 1.8 times a year and has $2.50 of assets for each
dollar of equity. What do its owners earn? What if the margin slips to 2 cents?*

*Solution.* 2.5% × 1.8 × 2.5 = 11.25%. At 2 cents: 2% × 1.8 × 2.5 = 9%. A half-cent change in margin moves the return on
equity by more than two points, because everything else multiplies it: a thin-margin business lives or dies by its
speed and its prices.

**3.** *A plant costs $600m. The order brings in $100m, $200m and $200m in its three years; if renewed, $200m a year for
three more. At 8%, what is the plant worth without renewal and with it? Above what chance of renewal should the assembler
build?*

*Solution.* The order alone: -600 + 100/1.08 + 200/1.08² + 200/1.08³ = -600 + 92.6 + 171.5 + 158.8 = -$177m. The renewal:
200 × (1/1.08⁴ + 1/1.08⁵ + 1/1.08⁶) = 147.0 + 136.1 + 126.0 = $409m. Building pays if p × 409 > 177, p > 43%. If the brand
paid for half the plant, the order alone would be worth +$123m and the assembler would build whatever the chance.

**4.** *A $1,000 device has $420 of parts, whose makers keep 20% of what they are paid; an assembly fee of $28, of which the
assembler keeps $3; and $200 of the brand's own costs. What does each link keep, as a share of the price?*

*Solution.* Parts makers: 0.2 × 420 = $84, 8.4%. Assembler: $3, 0.3%. Brand: 1,000 - 420 - 28 - 200 = $352, 35.2%.
Plotted from parts to assembly to brand, 8.4%, 0.3% and 35.2% make Shih's smile.

**5.** *The BLS's price index for televisions was 15,242.5 in 1951 and 96.0 in 2025, while all consumer prices rose 12.4
times. By how much did a television's price fall after inflation, and at what average rate a year over the 74 years?*

*Solution.* In money: 96.0 / 15,242.5 = 0.0063, a fall of 99.4%. After inflation: 0.0063 / 12.4 = 0.00051, a fall of
99.95%. Per year: 0.00051^(1/74) = 0.903, a fall of about 9.7% a year, every year, for three-quarters of a century. The
index adjusts for quality, so much of the fall is better sets for the same money.

## Glossary

- **Smile curve:** Stan Shih's picture of value added along an industry's chain: high for components and research at one
  end and brands and service at the other, lowest for manufacturing in the middle.
- **Contract manufacturer (EMS, ODM):** a company that builds products for other companies' brands; an ODM also designs them.
- **Turnkey and consigned:** whether the assembler buys the parts and resells them in the finished product (turnkey) or
  the brand supplies them and pays only for the work (consigned).
- **Bill of materials (BOM):** the list and cost of every part in a product.
- **Surface-mount technology (SMT):** placing components directly on the pads of a circuit board and soldering them in an
  oven.
- **Single source:** a part available from only one supplier.
- **Asset turnover:** sales divided by assets; how many times a year the business turns its capital over.
- **Return on equity:** profit over the owners' equity; margin × asset turnover × leverage (the DuPont identity).
- **HS code:** the Harmonized System's six-digit customs category, such as 851713 for smartphones.
- **Section 301:** the US trade law under which the 2018-2019 tariffs on Chinese goods were imposed.
