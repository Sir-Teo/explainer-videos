# Industry, Built: Part 18, Aerospace and Defence

Part 18 of a sixty-four-part series on how each industry works (see [the series plan](../README.md) and
[the syllabus](../SYLLABUS.md)), the fourth of Act III, *Making*. It asks: why are there only two makers of large
airliners, and why does the engine maker sell at a loss? It answers with the annual reports of Boeing (2025, and those of
2005, 2011 and 2013 for its forecasts), its 10-Q for the third quarter of 2026, Airbus's 2025 annual report (its ESEF
filing), the 10-Ks of GE Aerospace, RTX, Lockheed Martin and TransDigm and the 20-F of AerCap; the SEC company facts of
fifteen aerospace and defence companies; the WTO's summaries of the two subsidy disputes, DS316 and DS353; the Census
Bureau's orders and shipments of aircraft since 1992 and America's defence spending since 1947 (via FRED); SIPRI's
military expenditure database; and the Works' airliner programme, engine, launch aid and the Run it duel. The answer: a
new airliner costs tens of billions and years before its first delivery, and pays back only after about a thousand
aircraft, once the learning curve has brought each one's cost below its price; the two makers that exist have built
thousands each and hold a decade of orders, so a newcomer must pay for learning they have already paid for, against
incumbents who can cut their price; and engines, like many parts, are sold for the decades of servicing that follow, which
is where much of the industry's money is made. Defence runs on different rules: one buyer per country, budgets set in
public, contracts that pay costs plus a fee, and margins it keeps in check.

It opens on 2025: Airbus delivered 793 airliners and Boeing 600; Airbus had 8,754 more on order; Boeing, which earned
$5bn to $10bn a year to 2018, lost $36bn from 2019 to 2024; and GE's commercial engine business sold $8.3bn of new engines
and $25.0bn of spare parts and servicing. From there it covers:

  a rival choosing at the same time, buyers splitting each year's market by fuel burn. Each programme is worth $66.5bn if

- how an airliner is made: sections, wings, tail and engines from many places, joined on a final assembly line; Boeing's
  sole-source components and its year to qualify a new source of aluminium, titanium or composites; Spirit AeroSystems,
  the 737's fuselage maker, bought by Boeing for $8.4bn in December 2025; 2025's deliveries by model (737 447, 787 88,
  A320 family 607, A220 93) and the single-aisle jets' 75% and 88% shares; the 737 at 42 a month and moving to 47 with the

## What is computed, and from what

`compute.py` reads the Filing Room and the Register. The programme, the engine, launch aid and the duel are the Works'
(`explainer/industry/works/aircraft.py`), with illustrative parameters labelled on screen. The programme spends its
development evenly over six years, then delivers 18, 48, 84 and then 120 aircraft a year for 25 years at $110m each; the
n-th aircraft costs $450m × n^log2(0.82); cash is discounted at 8%. The program-accounting average is the mean cost of
aircraft 1 to 1,500. The newcomer is the same programme with its price varied. The engine sells for $12m, costs $15m, flies
3,000 hours a year and earns $350 an hour of servicing at a 35% margin for 25 years, discounted at 8%. Launch aid lends a
third of development interest free, repaid at $8m per aircraft delivered until paid off. In the duel two makers share 1,000
aircraft a year for 25 years at a $12m margin; each year's buyers split by fuel burn, share = (1 / burn)^12 over the sum,
and until its new product arrives a maker sells its old one. Backlog years divide unfilled firm orders by 2025's deliveries.
Net margins are profit attributable to the parent over revenue from the companies' filings, by fiscal year (TransDigm's
ends in September; Lockheed's and L3Harris's near the turn of the year); the landscape averages 2021-2025, except Airbus
(2025, converted at the Federal Reserve's average rate of US$1.13 to the euro) and GE Aerospace (2025, its first full year
alone). Aircraft orders and shipments are sums of the Census Bureau's seasonally adjusted monthly values. US defence
spending is the BEA's national defense consumption expenditures and gross investment, an annual average of quarterly rates,
deflated by the CPI-U (the BLS published no October 2025 CPI, so 2025 averages eleven months). SIPRI's figures are in
constant 2024 dollars.

## Problems and solutions

**1.** *The first aircraft off a new line costs $450m, and each doubling of the number built cuts the next one's cost to
82%. What do the second, fourth and thousandth cost, and from which aircraft does each cost less than its $110m price?*

*Solution.* The n-th costs 450 × n^b with b = log2(0.82) = -0.286. The second costs 450 × 0.82 = $369m, the fourth
450 × 0.82² = $303m, the thousandth 450 × 1,000^-0.286 = $62m. The cost equals the price when n^0.286 = 450 / 110 = 4.09,
so n = 4.09^(1 / 0.286) = 137: from about the 137th aircraft on, each costs less than it sells for.

**2.** *Under program accounting a maker books every delivery at the average cost of the 1,500 aircraft it expects to
build, $77m, and sells each for $110m. What gross margin does it report on the very first delivery, and how much of that
aircraft's $450m cost is carried on the balance sheet instead of counted as a loss?*

*Solution.* It reports (110 - 77) / 110 = 30% on every delivery, the first included. The first aircraft's cost above the
average, 450 - 77 = $373m, is carried as a deferred production cost, to be recovered from later aircraft that cost less
than $77m. If the maker sells fewer than 1,500, or the later ones cost more than planned, the deferral cannot be recovered
and becomes a reach-forward loss.

**3.** *In the duel each programme is worth (you, rival, in $bn): both new 53.1 and 53.1; you new and the rival
re-engines 58.8 and 60.8; you re-engine and the rival goes new 60.8 and 58.8; both re-engine 66.5 and 66.5. What do two
sensible makers do? With a bigger fuel saving for the new plane the values become 53.1 and 53.1; 66.9 and 52.7; 52.7 and
66.9; 66.5 and 66.5. What now?*

*Solution.* In the first game, if the rival goes new, re-engining (60.8) beats new (53.1); if the rival re-engines,
re-engining (66.5) beats new (58.8). Re-engining is better either way, so both re-engine and each has $66.5bn. In the
second, if the rival goes new, new (53.1) beats re-engining (52.7); if the rival re-engines, new (66.9) beats re-engining
(66.5). New is better either way, so both go new and each has $53.1bn, although both would have $66.5bn by re-engining: a
prisoner's dilemma, set off by a few points of fuel.

**4.** *An engine sells for $12m and costs $15m to build. It flies 3,000 hours a year for 25 years, and its maker earns
$350 an hour of servicing at a 35% margin. At an 8% discount rate, what is the engine worth to its maker, and in which year
does the servicing pay back the loss on the sale?*

*Solution.* Servicing earns 3,000 × 350 × 0.35 = $367,500 a year. Over 25 years at 8% the annuity factor is
(1 - 1.08^-25) / 0.08 = 10.67, so the servicing is worth 0.3675 × 10.67 = $3.92m today, and the engine 3.92 - 3 = $0.92m.
Payback needs an annuity factor of 3 / 0.3675 = 8.16, which (1 - 1.08^-T) / 0.08 reaches at T = 13.8: in year 14.

**5.** *At the end of 2025 Boeing had 4,404 undelivered 737s under firm orders. How many years of work is that at 42 a
month, and at 47? Airbus had 8,754 aircraft on order and delivered 793 in 2025: how many years is that?*

*Solution.* 4,404 / (42 × 12) = 8.7 years; 4,404 / (47 × 12) = 7.8 years. Airbus: 8,754 / 793 = 11.0 years. An airline
ordering a new single-aisle jet today joins a queue that ends in the mid-2030s, unless it takes someone else's place, or
leases one from a company that ordered years ago.

## Glossary

- **Airframer:** a company that designs an aircraft and assembles it, buying the engines and many systems from others.
- **Single-aisle and twin-aisle:** narrowbody jets with one aisle, for short and medium flights; widebody jets with two, for
  long ones.
- **Learning curve:** the fall in the cost of each unit as the number built grows; Wright's form cuts it by a fixed share at
  each doubling.
- **Program accounting:** booking each delivery at the programme's estimated average cost over its accounting quantity.
- **Accounting quantity:** the number of aircraft a programme expects to build in the period its estimates cover.
- **Deferred production costs:** the costs of early aircraft above the programme's average, carried as inventory.
- **Reach-forward loss:** a loss booked at once when a programme's estimated costs exceed its estimated revenue.
- **Backlog:** firm orders not yet delivered.
- **Launch aid:** government loans for developing a new airliner, repaid from its sales (LA/MSF in the WTO's term).
- **Turbofan:** a jet engine whose large fan pushes most of its air around the core.
- **Aftermarket:** spare parts and servicing for aircraft and engines already in service.
- **Proprietary part:** a part designed and certified by one supplier, which then has no approved competitor for it.
- **Operating lessor:** a company that owns aircraft and rents them to airlines for years at a time.
- **Prime contractor:** the company that holds a defence contract and leads the work, with suppliers beneath it.
- **Cost-reimbursable and fixed-price contracts:** a buyer paying costs plus a fee, against a price agreed up front.
