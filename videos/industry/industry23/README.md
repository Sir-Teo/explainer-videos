# Industry, Built: Part 23, Fashion and Luxury

Part 23 of a sixty-four-part series on how each industry works (see [the series plan](../README.md) and
[the syllabus](../SYLLABUS.md)), the ninth and last of Act III, *Making*. It asks: how do two industries that sell clothes run on
opposite rules? It answers with Inditex's FY2025 results and interim results for the first half of 2026; Hermès's
full-year results for 2023, 2024 and 2025; LVMH's 2025 results; Kering's full-year results for 2023 and 2025; the Forms
10-K of NIKE (fiscal 2026), TJX (fiscal 2026), Tapestry (fiscal 2026), Capri (fiscal 2026), Lululemon (fiscal 2025) and
Levi Strauss (fiscal 2025); the SEC company facts of twenty-three apparel, footwear, luxury, off-price and department-store
companies; UN Comtrade's clothing exports by country; the BLS's apparel prices and apparel manufacturing jobs (via FRED);
the WTO's page on the Agreement on Textiles and Clothing; the ILO on Rana Plaza; and the Works' season. The answer: both
face the same problem, clothes ordered before anyone knows what will sell, with every wrong guess ending in the sale; fast
fashion answers it with speed, buying a base early and making the hits again close to home; the off-price shops answer it
by buying everyone else's wrong guesses; luxury answers it with the wait, making fewer than people want, owning the shops
and never holding a sale; and all of them rest on contract factories they do not own and on a taste that can turn in a
season.

It opens on Inditex, €39.9bn of sales from 5,460 shops in 214 markets, and Hermès, €16.0bn of revenue with 41% kept as
operating profit; then on Inditex's gross margin quarter by quarter (60.6%, 56.4%, 62.2%, 54.8%): the saw-tooth of a
business that clears what did not sell at the end of each season. From there it covers:

## What is computed, and from what

`compute.py` reads the Filing Room and the Register. The season is the Works' (`explainer/industry/works/fashion.py`):
demand is lognormal with a mean of 100,000 and a coefficient of variation of 60% months ahead; the first weeks of selling
reveal part of the log error, leaving 20%; a single order is the newsvendor's quantile at the critical ratio (price - cost)
/ (price - clearance price); the chase orders a base early at $12 and tops up, after the first weeks, to the late
newsvendor level at $16; expectations over what the first weeks reveal use 64-point Gauss-Hermite quadrature, and the base
is chosen by bounded scalar search. Days of stock are 365 × inventory / cost of sales in the latest fiscal year (TJX's and
Gap's cost of sales include buying and occupancy; NIKE's inventory is its 10-K's $7.5bn; Inditex's cost of sales is the sum
of its four quarters). Margins in the landscape are profit attributable to the parent over revenue, the median of the last
five fiscal years for companies filing with the SEC and 2025 alone for Inditex (to January 2026), Hermès, LVMH and Kering,
whose revenue is converted at 2025's average dollar-euro rate (On's at the dollar-franc rate). SEC figures are keyed by the
calendar year each fiscal year ends in; most retailers' years end in late January or early February. The off-price index
leaves out the pandemic year ending early 2021. Clothing exports are HS chapters 61 and 62, exports to the world as each
country reported them (Vietnam to 2023; Bangladesh's last report in the extract is for 2018). Consumer prices and jobs are
annual averages (eleven months of CPI in 2025: the BLS published no October 2025 CPI). What the second half of FY2026
needs assumes its sales grow as the first half's did (7.6%).

## Problems and solutions

**1.** *A shirt sells for $40, costs $12 if ordered early and $16 if ordered late, and anything left at the end goes for
$8. What chance of selling should the last shirt ordered have, in each case?*

*Solution.* Ordering one more shirt gains price minus cost if it sells and loses cost minus clearance price if it does
not. Early: (40 - 12) / ((40 - 12) + (12 - 8)) = 28 / 32 = 87.5%. Late: (40 - 16) / (24 + 8) = 24 / 32 = 75%. The cheaper
the shirt and the better the clearance price, the more it pays to over-order.

**2.** *Inditex held €3,249m of inventory at 31 January 2026; its cost of sales for the year was €16,641m. NIKE held $7.5bn
against $26.49bn. How many days of cost of sales did each hold?*

*Solution.* Inditex: 365 × 3,249 / 16,641 = 71 days. NIKE: 365 × 7.5 / 26.49 = 103 days. Each day of stock is money tied
up and a chance that the guess was wrong.

**3.** *Inditex's inventories were €3,249m, its trade and other receivables €1,166m and its trade and other payables
€8,587m. What was its operating working capital, and what does the sign mean?*

*Solution.* 3,249 + 1,166 - 8,587 = -€4,172m. Negative: on balance, it owes more to suppliers and others than it has tied
up in stock and in money owed to it, so the people it buys from fund its day-to-day trade.

**4.** *America's CPI for apparel was 1.06 times its 1990 level in 2025, and the CPI for all items 2.46 times. By how much
did clothes get cheaper relative to everything else?*

*Solution.* 1.06 / 2.46 = 0.43: clothes cost 43% of what they did in 1990 relative to other prices, 57% less. The sewing
left America over the same years: jobs making clothes fell from 903,000 to 79,000.

**5.** *Inditex's gross margin was 58.7% on €19.755bn of sales in the first half of 2026. Its second half of 2025 sold
€21.507bn at 58.2%. If the second half of 2026 grows 7.6%, what second-half margin gives 58.5% for the year?*

*Solution.* Second-half sales: 21.507 × 1.076 = €23.14bn; the year: 19.755 + 23.14 = €42.90bn. Needed gross profit: 0.585 ×
42.90 = €25.10bn; the first half gave 0.587 × 19.755 = €11.60bn; so the second half needs 13.50 / 23.14 = 58.3%, about what
it managed a year earlier.

## Glossary

- **Season:** the weeks in which a collection is on sale, usually spring-summer and autumn-winter.
- **Markdown:** a price cut to clear stock that did not sell at full price.
- **Newsvendor problem:** choosing how much to order once, before demand is known, when unsold units are worth less.
- **Critical ratio:** (price - cost) / (price - clearance price): the chance of selling the last unit at which ordering it
  breaks even.
- **Proximity sourcing:** making clothes in factories near the shops, so they can be ordered during the season.
- **Contract manufacturer:** an independent factory that makes goods to another company's design and under its brand.
- **Wholesale:** selling to other shops, which resell; **direct to consumer:** selling in the brand's own shops and online.
- **Off-price:** shops that buy other companies' excess stock and sell it below full-price shops' prices.
- **Working capital:** stock plus money owed by customers, less money owed to suppliers.
- **Quota:** a limit on the quantity of a good a country may import from a given country.
- **Gross margin:** what is left of sales after the cost of the goods sold.
