## Every swirl obeys one equation

_Scene `s01_hook.py::Hook`_

This is a computer simulation of fluid flowing past a cylinder. Notice the swirls peeling off, alternating between the top and the bottom, one after another, in a perfectly rhythmic pattern.

This pattern is called a Kármán vortex street. You can see it in the clouds downwind of islands, and it's why a telephone wire hums in the wind.

Every swirl here, every detail of this flow, comes from a single set of equations, written down two centuries ago: the Navier–Stokes equations.

They govern the weather, ocean currents, the blood in your arteries, the air over an airplane's wing, and the cream swirling in your coffee.

At first glance they look intimidating. But the core idea is something you already know. They are nothing more than Newton's second law, F equals m a, applied to every tiny parcel of fluid.

By the end of this video, we'll have derived every one of these terms from scratch, and every symbol will feel not just familiar, but inevitable. Along the way, we'll see why these innocent-looking equations hide one of the great problems in mathematics, with a million-dollar prize attached, and one that an AI system, in September 2026, claimed to have cracked.

## Describing a fluid: fields

_Scene `s02_fields.py::VelocityField`_

So, how do you even describe a fluid? A glass of water holds roughly ten to the twenty-five molecules, all jostling around, colliding billions of times per second. Tracking each one is hopeless.

So we zoom out. We pretend the fluid is a smooth, continuous substance, and at every point in space we ask just one question: which way, and how fast, is the fluid moving right here?

The answer is a vector, which we'll call u, and color blue throughout this video. Its direction is the direction of flow, and its length is the speed.

Do this at every point and you get a velocity field, written u of x and t, since it depends on position and on time. In three dimensions it has three components: u, v, and w.

If you sprinkle tiny specks of dust into the flow, they get carried along by these arrows, tracing out the motion of the fluid.

We'll need one more field: pressure, a single number at each point, colored in red. You can think of it as how tightly the fluid is being squeezed at that spot. Notice that it's lowest in the middle of each swirl. Hold on to that; we'll see why soon.

So the unknowns are the three components of velocity, and the pressure: four unknown functions of space and time. The Navier–Stokes equations are the rules that determine how these fields evolve.

## F = ma for a fluid parcel

_Scene `s03_newton.py::NewtonForParcels`_

Now for the physics. Follow one tiny blob of fluid, a parcel, as it drifts along. Picture it as small enough that the velocity is essentially uniform across it, yet large enough to contain an enormous number of molecules.

Its volume is dV, and if the fluid's density is rho, its mass is rho times dV. Newton's second law says mass times acceleration equals the total force on it.

Since the parcel is tiny, it's more natural to divide both sides by its volume. On the left, density times acceleration. On the right, force per unit volume.

So what forces act on a parcel of fluid? Only three kinds. First, the surrounding fluid pushes on it, perpendicular to its surface. That's pressure.

Second, neighboring fluid sliding past drags on it, tangent to its surface. That's viscosity, a kind of internal friction.

And third, there can be forces acting on the whole body of the parcel from outside, like gravity.

So the equation we're after has this shape. Each piece has a clean mathematical form, and we're going to derive every one of them. Let's start with the left side, which turns out to be the subtlest: acceleration.

## Acceleration: the material derivative

_Scene `s04_material_derivative.py::MaterialDerivative`_

Here's a puzzle. Water flows steadily through a pipe that narrows. 'Steady' means the picture never changes: at any fixed spot, the velocity now is the same as the velocity a minute from now.

So if you take the time derivative of the velocity field at a fixed point, partial u, partial t, you get zero. Everywhere.

And yet, follow a single parcel of water. As it enters the narrow section, it must speed up, since the same amount of water per second has to squeeze through a smaller gap. The parcel is clearly accelerating.

The resolution: there are two different rates of change here. How the velocity changes at a fixed location, and how the velocity changes for a particular parcel as it travels. Newton's law is about the second one.

Let's compute it. Say the parcel's position at time t is x of t, y of t. Its velocity is just the velocity field evaluated wherever the parcel happens to be.

Focus on the horizontal component, u. As time passes, it changes for two reasons. Time is ticking, so the field itself may change. And the parcel moves, so it samples the field at new locations. The chain rule accounts for both.

Here's the key physical input: the parcel moves with the flow. So its rate of change of x is just u, and its rate of change of y is v.

Pull the velocity out, and what's left is an operator: u times partial partial x, plus v times partial partial y. That's the derivative in the direction of the flow, scaled by the speed, which we write compactly as u dot del.

The vertical component works exactly the same way, so in vector form: the acceleration of a parcel is partial u partial t, plus u dot del, u. This combination is called the material derivative, written with a capital D.

The first term is the change you'd see sitting still. The second is the change that comes purely from moving to a place where the flow is different. It's called advection.

Here's a nice way to see that second term. Take a fluid spinning steadily in a circle. Nothing changes in time, so partial u partial t is zero. But take the velocity at one point, follow the flow a small step, and compare with the velocity there. The difference points toward the center.

So u dot del u is the centripetal acceleration: the speed squared over the radius, pointing inward, exactly what any object moving in a circle must have.

Finally, notice that u appears twice in this term: the flow is carrying itself along. Double the velocity, and this term quadruples. This makes the equation nonlinear, and nearly everything that makes fluids rich, chaotic, and mathematically difficult traces back to this one term.

## The pressure force

_Scene `s05_pressure.py::PressureForce`_

Now the forces, starting with pressure. Pressure pushes on any surface, perpendicular to it, with a force equal to the pressure times the area.

If the pressure is the same on all sides of our parcel, the pushes cancel perfectly. A uniform pressure, no matter how large, produces no net force at all. What matters is how pressure varies.

So suppose the pressure varies from left to right. Take a box of width dx and face area A. The left face feels the pressure at x, pushing rightward with force p of x times A. The right face feels p at x plus dx, pushing leftward.

The net force is the difference. For a tiny box, p of x plus dx minus p of x is just the derivative of p times dx. And dx times A is the volume of the box.

So the horizontal force per unit volume is minus partial p partial x. The same argument in the y and z directions gives the other components, and together they make minus the gradient of p.

The gradient points uphill, toward higher pressure, so the force points downhill. Fluid gets pushed from high pressure toward low pressure.

It's exactly why wind blows from high-pressure regions toward low-pressure ones.

And remember those swirls with low pressure in their cores? Now we can see why. For fluid to move in a circle, something must supply the inward, centripetal acceleration we just found. The only candidate is pressure, so the pressure has to drop toward the center.

Comparing the inward parts, the pressure gradient balances density times speed squared over radius. It's why the core of a tornado is a region of very low pressure, and why the water's surface dips down in the middle of a whirlpool.

## The viscous force

_Scene `s06_viscosity.py::ViscousForce`_

Next, viscosity: the internal friction that makes honey ooze differently from water. Picture a flow made of thin horizontal layers sliding past each other, faster at the top, slower at the bottom.

Each layer rubs against its neighbors. A faster layer above drags a slower one forward, and the slower layer below holds it back.

For fluids like water and air, this frictional force per unit area, called the shear stress, is proportional to how quickly the velocity changes from one layer to the next: mu times partial u partial y. The constant mu, in green, is the viscosity.

Now let's derive the force. Here's a velocity profile, and a slab of fluid of thickness dy. The layer above pulls it forward with stress tau at y plus dy, times the area A. The layer below drags it backward with tau at y, times A.

Once again, the net force comes from a difference. It's partial tau partial y, times dy times A. Plug in the stress, and dividing by the volume, the force per unit volume is mu times the second derivative of u with respect to y.

Notice what this means. If the velocity profile is a straight line, the second derivative is zero: the pull from above exactly balances the drag from below. Viscosity only produces a net force where the profile curves.

When you account for sliding in every direction, and use the fact that the flow is incompressible, all these second derivatives add up to one operator: the Laplacian, written del squared.

The Laplacian has a wonderful intuition. Compare a value at some point to the average of its neighbors.

Using a Taylor expansion on both sides and adding, the first derivatives cancel, and the second derivative is, up to a constant, the average of the neighbors minus the value at the point itself.

So the Laplacian is positive where the value sits below its surroundings, like the bottom of a valley, and negative at a peak.

In two or three dimensions it's the same story, just averaging over neighbors in every direction.

So viscosity nudges each parcel's velocity toward the average velocity of its neighbors. Fast spots get slowed down, slow spots get pulled along. Peaks get shaved off, valleys get filled in, and the velocity profile smooths out.

Here's the effect on a full flow, colored by how fast the fluid is spinning. On the left, a swirling flow with low viscosity, like water. On the right, the same starting flow with high viscosity, like honey. The honey version smears out and quickly dies away, while the water keeps its intricate structure much longer.

## Conservation of mass

_Scene `s07_continuity.py::Incompressibility`_

We now have one vector equation, three equations in three dimensions. But there are four unknowns: three velocity components and the pressure. The missing equation comes from conservation of mass.

Water is, for all practical purposes, incompressible. You can't squeeze a parcel of it into a smaller volume. So for any small region, whatever flows in must flow out.

Let's count the flow through a small box. Through the left face, fluid enters at a rate of u at x, times the face's length, dy. Through the right face, it leaves at a rate of u at x plus dx, times dy.

The net horizontal outflow is the difference, partial u partial x times dx dy. Likewise, the net outflow through the top and bottom is partial v partial y times dx dy.

The total, per unit volume, is partial u partial x plus partial v partial y, plus partial w partial z in three dimensions. This is the divergence of u, written del dot u. It measures how much fluid is streaming out of each point.

Here's a field with positive divergence: fluid gushes out of every point, like a source. Here's negative divergence, a sink. And here's a field with zero divergence: fluid swirls and streams around, but around every little region, inflow exactly balances outflow.

Incompressibility means the divergence is zero everywhere, always. That's our fourth equation.

And it explains our nozzle. The flow rate through the pipe must be the same at every cross-section, so where the area shrinks, the speed must grow.

## What decides the pressure?

_Scene `s08_pressure_poisson.py::PressureEnforcer`_

Here's something curious. The velocity has an equation telling it how to change in time. But the pressure doesn't: there's no partial p partial t anywhere. So what decides the pressure?

The answer: pressure is whatever it needs to be to keep the flow incompressible.

Imagine fluid flowing straight toward a wall. Left alone, with no pressure, it would just pile up against the wall, compressing.

Instead, a region of high pressure forms right in front of the wall. Its gradient pushes back on the incoming fluid, slowing it down and steering it aside, exactly enough that nothing ever gets compressed.

We can make this precise. Take the divergence of the whole momentum equation. The time derivative term vanishes, since the divergence of u is always zero. So does the viscous term. What's left is an equation for the pressure alone.

This is a Poisson equation, the same kind of equation that describes gravitational and electric potentials. Its solution at any point depends on the flow everywhere else, all at once. In an incompressible fluid, a push anywhere is felt everywhere instantly.

Physically, the news travels as sound waves. In water, sound moves at about fifteen hundred meters per second, so fast compared to everyday flows that 'instantly' is an excellent approximation.

So pressure isn't really an independent player. It's an enforcer, mathematically a Lagrange multiplier, constantly adjusting itself so the velocity field never violates the incompressibility constraint.

## Putting it together

_Scene `s09_full_equation.py::FullEquation`_

We have all the pieces. Let's assemble them.

Density times the acceleration of a parcel, made of the local change plus the advection term, equals the pressure force, plus the viscous force, plus any external force per unit volume, like gravity, which would be rho times g. And alongside it, incompressibility.

These are the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. Claude-Louis Navier first wrote them down in 1822, starting from a model of forces between molecules, and George Gabriel Stokes rederived them in 1845 from continuum reasoning much like ours.

Often you'll see them divided through by the density. Then the viscosity divided by the density becomes nu, the kinematic viscosity, which measures how quickly momentum diffuses through the fluid.

Read the equation like a sentence: a parcel of fluid, carried along by the flow, changes its velocity because it's pushed from high pressure to low, because it's dragged toward the velocity of its neighbors, and because of outside forces. And through it all, the fluid never compresses.

Four equations, four unknowns. Add an initial velocity field, and conditions at the boundaries, for instance, that fluid touching a solid wall sticks to it, the so-called no-slip condition, and in principle, the entire future of the flow is determined.

## The Reynolds number

_Scene `s10_reynolds.py::ReynoldsNumber`_

So which effect wins? Inertia, the fluid carrying itself along, or viscosity, smoothing everything out? Let's make a rough estimate.

Suppose the flow has a typical speed U, and changes over a typical length L, like the diameter of a cylinder in a wind tunnel. The advection term is a velocity times a velocity gradient, so it's roughly U times U over L.

The viscous term has two derivatives, so it's roughly nu times U over L squared.

Their ratio, U L over nu, is called the Reynolds number. It's arguably the single most important number in fluid mechanics.

In fact, we can make this exact. Measure lengths in units of L, velocities in units of U, time in units of L over U, and pressure in units of rho U squared. Substitute these into the equation, and divide through by U squared over L.

Everything cancels except a single parameter: one over the Reynolds number, sitting in front of the viscous term.

Two flows with the same geometry and the same Reynolds number are the same flow, just rescaled. That's why engineers can test a small model in a wind tunnel and trust the results, as long as the Reynolds number matches.

The range in nature is staggering. A swimming bacterium lives at a Reynolds number around one ten-thousandth. For it, water is so syrupy that the moment it stops swimming, it stops dead. Blood in your aorta flows at Re in the thousands. A person swimming, around a million. The air flowing over a jetliner's wing, tens of millions.

## Instability and turbulence

_Scene `s11_turbulence.py::Turbulence`_

Where does all that complexity come from? Here's a clue. Start with a simple layered flow: the middle band moves to the right, and the outer bands move to the left.

Along each interface, tiny ripples get amplified by the nonlinear term, and the shear layers roll up into chains of spiraling vortices. This is the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability, the same mechanism that sculpts those rare clouds shaped like breaking ocean waves.

Then the vortices themselves start to interact, pairing up and merging, and the orderly picture dissolves.

In a turbulent flow, this kind of thing happens at every scale at once. Large swirls stretch, fold, and break into smaller swirls, which break into smaller ones still, until the eddies are so tiny that viscosity finally smooths them away, turning their energy into heat.

The physicist Lewis Fry Richardson put it as a poem: Big whorls have little whorls that feed on their velocity; and little whorls have lesser whorls, and so on to viscosity.

That's the picture in three dimensions. But two dimensions, which is what all of these simulations are, behaves differently, in a surprising way. Watch this two-dimensional turbulent flow. Instead of shattering into ever-smaller eddies, the swirls tend to merge into larger and larger vortices.

That difference between two and three dimensions turns out to be at the heart of the deepest open question about these equations.

## Vortex stretching and the $1M question

_Scene `s12_millennium.py::MillenniumProblem`_

Here's the question. Start with a smooth, well-behaved flow in three dimensions, with finite total energy. Let it evolve according to Navier–Stokes. Does it remain smooth forever?

Or could the velocity somewhere blow up to infinity in a finite amount of time, a singularity, where the equations themselves stop making sense?

For decades, nobody knew. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute made this one of its seven Millennium Prize Problems, offering a million dollars for a proof either way.

To see what's at stake, look at vorticity, omega, the curl of the velocity. It measures how fast the fluid is spinning locally, like a tiny paddle wheel dropped into the flow.

Take the curl of the Navier–Stokes equation. The pressure term disappears, since the curl of a gradient is always zero. And after some vector calculus, the advection term splits in two.

The result says that vorticity is carried along by the flow, smoothed by viscosity, and has one extra term: omega dot del u. This is vortex stretching.

When a spinning tube of fluid gets stretched along its axis, it gets thinner, and just like a figure skater pulling in their arms, it spins faster. Faster spin can drive more stretching, a feedback loop that could, in principle, run away.

In two dimensions, this term is exactly zero. The spin axis always points straight out of the plane, and nothing varies in that direction, so there's nothing to stretch. That's why the two-dimensional problem was settled long ago: smooth solutions stay smooth forever.

In three dimensions it's a battle. Vortex stretching tries to concentrate the spin into ever smaller, ever more intense regions; viscosity tries to smooth it all out. Does viscosity always win?

We do know a lot. In 1934, Jean Leray proved that weak, generalized solutions always exist. Smooth solutions are known to exist at least for a while. And if singularities ever do form, they must be extremely rare, confined to vanishingly small sets in space and time. But for nearly a century, whether they can happen at all remained unknown.

## 2026: OpenAI's forced blow-up claim

_Scene `s13_forced_blowup.py::ForcedBlowup`_

Which brings us to a remarkable recent development. On September 8th, 2026, OpenAI announced that an internal AI system had produced a proof that the answer is no: solutions can break down.

There's a subtlety in how the prize problem is phrased. The official statement, written by Charles Fefferman, offers four ways to win. Two ask you to prove that smooth solutions last forever, in all of space or in a periodic box, with no external force at all. The other two ask for the opposite: show that a solution can break down. And for those, you're allowed to push on the fluid with an external force, as long as the force itself is perfectly smooth.

That second door, breakdown with a smooth force, is the one OpenAI's result walks through.

Here is the claim, as announced. For any viscosity, there is a smooth force, confined to a bounded region, which, acting on a fluid that starts completely at rest, keeps the total energy bounded, yet drives the flow to a singularity in a finite amount of time.

Why does the force matter so much? Without it, you would need a flow that tears itself apart entirely on its own. With a force, you get to keep feeding the flow, as long as you never cheat by using a force that is itself rough, or infinite.

The strategy goes back to Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, who had spent years building what they call an infinite cascade. Roughly speaking: an infinite sequence of layers, each one a smaller, faster swirl, each perfectly smooth on its own, timed so that infinitely many of them pile up in a finite amount of time, producing a singularity.

Each layer needs its own little push, and the total force is the sum of all of them. In their earlier constructions, that infinite sum ended up rough, which falls short of the prize rules. The missing step was to build the cascade so that the total force stays smooth.

According to OpenAI, about ten thousand AI agents running on an unreleased model worked in parallel for about 88 hours to close that gap. Another model then translated the proof into Lean, a language in which a computer can check every single logical step.

Charles Fefferman, who wrote the official problem statement, said he was thrilled that the problem was solved, and credited Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa as the heroes of the story.

The story is also tangled. Just hours before the announcement, mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge, who had been using AI tools themselves, posted Lean-verified forced blow-up results for closely related equations, including Euler, the version with no viscosity. The two sides dispute how the timelines relate, and OpenAI has said it will not claim the prize.

So, is it over? Not quite. As of this video, the proof is still being checked by human mathematicians, and the Clay Institute only recognizes a solution after publication and review. A formal proof is only as good as the check that its formal statement really says what we think it says.

And notice what it doesn't answer: whether a fluid left alone, with no force at all, can ever form a singularity. For the physical question that started all of this, the mystery remains.
