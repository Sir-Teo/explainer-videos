## Eddington-Finkelstein: light cones that tip over

To find better coordinates, follow light. A light ray moving straight in or out has zero interval. So dt d r is plus or minus one over one minus two M over r. Integrating, t is plus or minus a new radial coordinate, r star, plus a constant. Physicists call r star the tortoise coordinate, for a reason you can see in its graph. As r approaches two M, r star plunges to minus infinity: measured in t, light takes forever to reach the horizon, like Zeno's tortoise.

So label events by the ingoing light ray they sit on. Define v equals t plus r star; it's constant along every ingoing ray. Then dt is d v minus d r over one minus two M over r. Eddington wrote down this form in 1924, but didn't remark on it. David Finkelstein rediscovered it in 1958, and saw what it meant.

## Kruskal-Szekeres: the whole Schwarzschild spacetime

Finkelstein's coordinates see through the horizon, but only for things falling in. To see the whole spacetime, use both kinds of light rays. So squeeze it: define capital U and V as exponentials of u and v. Their product depends only on r, and vanishes exactly at the horizon.

In U and V, the metric's factor is finite, and nonzero, at r equals two M. And with T and X, the sum and difference, it's the flat metric of special relativity times a positive factor. So light travels at forty-five degrees everywhere in this picture. These are the coordinates Martin Kruskal and George Szekeres found, independently, in 1960.

Let's see how the familiar picture fits inside this one. Here's the outside of the black hole in Schwarzschild's coordinates: blue lines of constant r, teal lines of constant t. The equations, solved in full, also contain two more regions: a mirror-image exterior on the left, and below, a white hole, a region that things can only leave. This is the maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime.

Light cones are forty-five degrees everywhere, so causality is easy to read. Here's our astronaut's worldline: it crosses the horizon smoothly, and ends on the singularity. Look at their light cone inside. Every direction in it hits the singularity. Inside the black hole, the lines of constant r run sideways: r has become a time coordinate. The singularity isn't a place you could steer around. It's a moment in your future, as unavoidable as next Tuesday.

This picture also contains a wormhole. Take a slice of constant Kruskal time T. At T equals zero, it runs from the other exterior on the left, through r equals two M, to ours on the right. Its geometry, embedded as a surface, is two copies of Flamm's funnel from part one, joined at the throat: the Einstein–Rosen bridge, a tunnel between two universes. But it isn't static. Move the slice up in T, and the throat shrinks, and pinches off, at the singularity. Running backward, it was born from the white hole's singularity. It closes too fast for anything, even light, to get through: a light ray sent across ends on the singularity.

Is any of this real? The extra regions are there because Schwarzschild's solution describes a black hole that has existed forever. Real black holes form from collapsing stars, and to see what that changes, we need one more way of drawing spacetime.

## Penrose diagrams and gravitational collapse

_Scene `s05_penrose.py::Penrose`_

Kruskal's picture still runs off to infinity in every direction. Roger Penrose found a way to fit all of spacetime, infinity included, on a finite page. Start with flat spacetime, in the null coordinates u and v. Squeeze each of them with an arctangent, so their infinite ranges become finite. The metric picks up a factor that blows up at the edges. Now the trick: throw that factor away. Multiplying a metric by a positive function changes lengths, but not angles, so light cones keep their shape, and the causal structure, who can signal whom, is exactly preserved. What's left is a finite picture of an infinite spacetime.

Here's what that does to flat spacetime. A grid of lines, one per unit of time and of distance, stretching off forever. Apply the squeeze. The whole infinite half-plane folds into a triangle. Every line of constant r runs from one point at the bottom to one point at the top: that's where every inertial observer comes from, and goes to. Lines of constant t all meet at the far corner, spatial infinity. And the two diagonal edges are where light rays come from and go to, called scri minus and scri plus. Light still moves at forty-five degrees, so you can read off at a glance what can influence what.

Now do the same to the black hole. Start from Kruskal's diagram, and squeeze U and V with arctangents. The exterior becomes a diamond, the black hole a triangle on top, and the singularity, which was a hyperbola, becomes a straight horizontal line: r equals zero is where the arctangents of U and V add up to pi over two.

This is the Penrose diagram of the Schwarzschild black hole. Our universe is the diamond on the right, with its own infinities: scri plus, where outgoing light ends up, and future and past timelike infinity. Here's the astronaut. And now the definition of a black hole becomes visual: it's the region from which no light ray can reach scri plus. Light from here gets out. Light from inside the horizon can only go up, into the singularity.

But real black holes weren't always there: they form when a star collapses. Here's a star that sits at r equals five M and then collapses. Outside its surface, by Birkhoff's theorem, the geometry is exactly Schwarzschild's, so this part of the picture is exact. Inside is the star itself, not empty space. So the left half of Kruskal's diagram, with the white hole, the other universe and the wormhole, is simply covered up by the star, and never exists. The standard Penrose diagram of a collapse looks like this: the star's surface falls through the horizon and meets the singularity, and outside, everything is as before.

And look at where the event horizon is. It's the boundary of the region that can never send light to scri plus, and it starts at the center of the star, before the surface has reached r equals two M: a horizon grows outward from the center, through matter that hasn't yet noticed anything. To know where the event horizon is today, you need to know the entire future. Nothing local marks it.

That leaves the singularity. Every picture so far has been perfectly spherical. Maybe, in a real, lumpy collapse, the matter would miss the center and bounce back out? For decades, many physicists thought so.

## Raychaudhuri's equation and the singularity theorem

_Scene `s06_focusing.py::Focusing`_

In 1963, two Soviet physicists, Lifshitz and Khalatnikov, argued that the singularity was an artifact of symmetry: in a realistic, irregular collapse, they concluded, no singularity would form. Two years later, Roger Penrose proved the opposite. Once a certain kind of surface forms, a trapped surface, a singularity is unavoidable, whatever the symmetry. The key tool is an equation found in 1955 by Amal Kumar Raychaudhuri, about how a bundle of free particles, or light rays, spreads out or converges.

Picture a whole family of freely falling worldlines, side by side, like the ball of test particles from part one. Let u be their four-velocity, and B the tensor that says how u changes across the bundle: it tells you how the separation between neighbors grows. B splits into three parts, each with its own meaning. Its trace, theta, the expansion, says how fast the bundle's volume grows. Its symmetric, traceless part, the shear sigma, changes the shape without changing the volume. And its antisymmetric part, the rotation omega, twists it.

Now let's derive how theta changes along the bundle. The rate of change of B is u dotted into a second covariant derivative of u. Swap the order of the two derivatives. Their commutator, as we found in part one, is the Riemann tensor. Use the product rule on the first term. The worldlines are free, so they're geodesics: u dot grad u is zero, and what's left is B times B, and curvature.

Take the trace, and Riemann contracts to the Ricci tensor. Finally, write B as its three parts: the trace gives a third of theta squared, the shear and rotation give their squares, and the cross terms vanish. This is Raychaudhuri's equation.

Read it term by term. Theta squared: once a bundle starts converging, convergence feeds on itself. Shear always adds to the focusing. Rotation is the only term that pushes the other way, like a centrifugal force. And the last term is where gravity enters. By Einstein's equation, for a fluid it's four pi times rho plus three p. We've seen this before: it's exactly Baez and Bunn's statement about a ball of particles starting at rest. That was Raychaudhuri's equation all along. As long as rho plus three p is positive, which is true of all ordinary matter, gravity focuses. For light rays, the condition needed is even weaker: rho plus p non-negative.

Now the key consequence, for light rays. Suppose there's no rotation, which is true for the light rays leaving any surface, and the energy condition holds. Then every term on the right is negative or zero, so d theta d lambda is at most minus one half theta squared. Divide by theta squared and integrate. If the bundle starts out converging, with theta naught negative, then theta must reach minus infinity within an affine distance two over the magnitude of theta naught. Here's that bound, and an example with matter, which gets there even sooner. Minus infinity means the rays cross: a caustic, a focal point. Gravity is a lens that only ever converges.

So where would light rays start out converging? Take a sphere outside a black hole and set off a flash of light on it. The flash splits into two fronts, one moving out and one moving in. The inner front shrinks, but the outer one grows, as you'd expect. Now do the same inside the horizon. Both fronts shrink. Even the light aimed outward converges, because inside, r is a time and it only decreases. A closed surface whose outgoing and ingoing light both converge is called a trapped surface.

Here is Penrose's theorem. If gravity focuses light, if space at one moment is infinite, and if a trapped surface forms, then some light ray must come to an end after a finite affine distance: spacetime has an edge, a singularity. The idea of the proof: the light rays leaving a trapped surface are all converging, so by Raychaudhuri, they all reach caustics within a finite distance. So the boundary of everything the surface can influence is finite, closed off. But that finite boundary would have to map onto the whole of an infinite space, which is impossible, unless some of those light rays simply end.

Nothing in this argument needs symmetry, and a slightly deformed trapped surface is still trapped, so the conclusion is robust. In 2020, Penrose received the Nobel Prize for exactly this: showing that black hole formation is a robust prediction of general relativity. Stephen Hawking turned the same argument around in time: an expanding universe filled with ordinary matter must have a singularity in its past, a precise version of the Big Bang. What the theorems don't say is what the singularity is like. They predict where general relativity itself breaks down, and a quantum theory of gravity must take over.

## II. Gyroscopes: geodetic precession and frame dragging

_Scene `s07_gyroscopes.py::Gyroscopes`_

In part one, Mercury and the bending of light came from a mass sitting still. What changes when the mass moves, or spins? Go back to the linearized field equation. For a static source, the time-time part gives Newton's potential. But a moving source also has momentum, and mass currents feed the time-space part of the metric, through exactly the equation electric currents use to make a magnetic vector potential. Far from a spinning body, the solution has the shape of a magnetic dipole's field, with the angular momentum J playing the role of the magnetic moment.

This gravitomagnetic field has two effects. First, it drags things around. Drop something with zero angular momentum near a spinning body, and it doesn't fall straight in: it circles, with angular velocity two J over r cubed. Spacetime itself is being dragged around by the rotation. Second, it twists gyroscopes. A gyroscope's spin axis precesses at a rate with the shape of a dipole field, worked out by Lense and Thirring in 1918. Over the poles, the gyroscope is dragged along with the rotation; at the equator, the other way.

There's a second, larger effect on gyroscopes, which doesn't need rotation at all. Send a gyroscope around a mass in a circular orbit, its spin axis pointing outward to start. A gyroscope is the best definition of a fixed direction we have: its spin is parallel transported along its worldline. Yet after one orbit, it no longer points where it started. Computed exactly, it has turned by two pi times one minus the square root of one minus three M over r, in the direction of the orbit: fifty-nine degrees per orbit at r equals ten M. This is geodetic precession, predicted by Willem de Sitter in 1916.

Where does that turning come from? Part of it is the curvature of space. Here's Flamm's paraboloid from part one: the geometry of space around the mass at one moment. Transport a vector around the circle at r equals ten M, staying on the surface. Near that circle, the surface is like a cone. Cut the cone open and unroll it, and you get a flat sheet with a wedge missing. On the flat sheet, parallel transport is trivial: the arrows stay parallel. But when you glue the edges back together, the last arrow is turned relative to the first, by the angle of the missing wedge, thirty-eight degrees here. In weak gravity, curved space supplies exactly two thirds of the geodetic effect, and the warping of time the other third.

Both effects were measured in orbit. Gravity Probe B, launched in 2004, carried four gyroscopes around the Earth in a polar orbit, pointed at a guide star. General relativity predicts that their axes should turn by six thousand six hundred milliarcseconds a year from geodetic precession, and by thirty-nine milliarcseconds a year, sideways, from the Earth's frame dragging: about the width of a human hair seen from half a kilometer away. The measurements: six thousand six hundred and two, plus or minus eighteen, and thirty-seven, plus or minus seven. Laser-tracked satellites see the Earth drag their orbits as well. Space really is dragged around by rotating mass.

## The Kerr black hole

_Scene `s08_kerr.py::Kerr`_

Real stars rotate, and a collapsing star spins faster and faster, like a skater pulling in their arms. So real black holes spin. The spacetime of a spinning black hole wasn't found until 1963, when Roy Kerr discovered this: the Kerr metric, with mass M and angular momentum J equal to M times a. Like Schwarzschild's, it's a solution of Einstein's equation in empty space; our symbolic code confirms that all ten components of its Ricci tensor vanish. With no spin, it's Schwarzschild. With no mass, it's flat spacetime in unusual coordinates. And far away, its time-space term is exactly the gravitomagnetic field we just derived. Remarkably, it's the only possibility: every stationary black hole in empty space is a Kerr black hole, described by just two numbers. Black holes have no hair.

What does a Kerr black hole look like? Here's a slice through it, containing the spin axis. The horizon is where g r r blows up, where Delta vanishes. That happens at two radii, r plus and r minus. Let's turn up the spin. As a grows, the outer horizon shrinks, from two M toward M, and an inner horizon appears inside it. Outside the horizon there's a new surface, where g t t vanishes, which touches the horizon at the poles and bulges out at the equator: the boundary of the ergosphere. And the singularity is no longer a point: it's a ring, of radius a, here cut in two places. If a exceeded M, there'd be no horizon at all, and the singularity would be naked, visible from outside. Penrose conjectured that nature never allows that. This cosmic censorship conjecture is still unproven, but no generic process that violates it is known.

The ergosphere is where frame dragging becomes irresistible. Ask: at a fixed radius, how fast can something orbit, as seen from far away? For its worldline to be timelike, slower than light, its angular velocity Omega has to make this quadratic negative, which confines it to a band. Far out, the band includes zero, and in either direction you can go is fine. At the ergosurface, the lower edge reaches zero. Inside, the whole band is positive: no rocket, however powerful, can hold you still relative to the distant stars. You must rotate with the hole. At the horizon, the band closes to a single value, Omega H: the horizon itself rotates rigidly at that rate.

Here's a way to see the dragging directly. In the equatorial plane, set off a flash of light at each dot, and draw where the light has reached a moment later. Without spin, each flash is a circle around its source. Turn up the spin. The circles are swept around in the direction of rotation, most strongly near the hole. Inside the ergosurface, every circle is carried completely off its source: even light can't move against the rotation, or even stay put. That's why it's called the ergosphere, from the Greek for work: as we'll see next, energy can be extracted from it.

## The ergosphere and the Penrose process

_Scene `s09_extraction.py::Extraction`_

The ergosphere allows something impossible anywhere else: negative energy. A particle's conserved energy, the one measured from far away, is minus its momentum dotted into the time direction. Split it into what a local observer, carried around by the dragging, measures, which is always positive, plus a term from frame dragging times the angular momentum. If the particle moves backward against the rotation, L is negative, and inside the ergosphere, the dragging is strong enough that the total can be negative. Geometrically, the time direction of the distant observers is spacelike there, so this dot product can have either sign. A particle on such an orbit can never escape. But it can fall in.

Roger Penrose saw what this allows, in 1969. Send a particle into the ergosphere. There, let it split in two, throwing one piece backward, against the rotation, fast enough that its energy is negative. That piece falls into the hole. Energy is conserved, so the other piece leaves with more energy than the original particle brought in. In this computed example, almost ten percent more. The extra energy came from the black hole's rotation: swallowing negative energy and negative angular momentum, the hole gets a little lighter and spins a little slower.

Is there a limit? Anything that crosses the horizon must move forward in time relative to the horizon's own light rays, which rotate with angular velocity Omega H. For the piece that falls in, that means E minus Omega H times L is at least zero. So the hole's mass can drop, but only by so much: the change in M is at least Omega H times the change in J. And here's a surprise. Differentiate the area of Kerr's horizon, and this inequality says exactly that the area can't decrease. Spin energy can be extracted, but the horizon's area only grows. We'll meet this again.

Demetrios Christodoulou made this precise in 1970. A Kerr black hole's mass splits into two parts: an irreducible mass, fixed by the horizon's area, and the energy of its spin. Here are curves of constant area in the plane of J and M. A Penrose process, done as gently as possible, moves along one of these curves, removing spin and mass together, until the hole stops spinning. Starting from a maximally spinning hole, up to twenty-nine percent of its mass can be extracted. For comparison, nuclear fusion turns seven tenths of a percent of hydrogen's mass into energy. Nature's version probably uses magnetic fields threading the ergosphere, which may power the jets of quasars, like the one shooting out of M87.

## Orbits and the shadow of a spinning black hole

_Scene `s10_shadow.py::Shadow`_

Spin also changes how close matter can orbit. In part one we found that around a non-spinning hole, the innermost stable circular orbit is at six M. Around a spinning one, orbits going the same way as the spin, prograde, can get much closer: down to M itself for a maximally spinning hole, while backward orbits are pushed out to nine M. That matters, because gas in an accretion disk spirals in through these orbits, radiating away its binding energy, until it reaches the innermost one and plunges. The fraction of its rest mass radiated is one minus the energy of that orbit: five point seven percent for a non-spinning hole, and up to forty-two percent for a maximally spinning one. Kip Thorne showed that accretion itself spins holes up to about a equals zero point nine nine eight, where the efficiency is thirty-two percent: the most efficient engines in the universe.

What does a spinning black hole look like? The dark shadow is outlined by light rays that barely escape. In Kerr, light rays obey a separated radial equation, thanks to a hidden conserved quantity found by Brandon Carter in 1968. The critical rays are the ones that can circle forever at a fixed radius, where R and its derivative both vanish. Solving those two equations gives the ray's constants for every radius between the prograde and retrograde photon orbits. And each pair of constants marks one point on the sky of a distant observer. Together, they trace the edge of the shadow, as James Bardeen worked out in 1973.

Here's that edge for a black hole seen edge-on. Without spin, it's a circle, of radius the square root of twenty-seven M, as we found in part one. Spin it up. Photons orbiting with the rotation can get closer before being captured, so on that side the edge moves in and flattens, while the other side bulges out. Near maximal spin, the shadow becomes a letter D.

Now the full picture, ray-traced. On the left, a non-spinning black hole, like part one's; on the right, the same disk and camera around a hole spinning at ninety-nine percent of the maximum. The disk reaches much closer in, because the innermost orbit has moved in, and the approaching side is brighter still, because the gas there moves faster. Overlay Bardeen's analytic shadow edge: it's exactly the boundary of the rays our tracer sees captured, pixel for pixel.

Seen nearly face-on, which is roughly how we see the black hole in M87, the shadow is almost round whatever the spin, and its size changes by less than seven percent. That makes the shadow a good way to weigh a black hole, and a hard way to measure its spin.

For the black hole in the galaxy M87, six and a half billion solar masses, fifty-five million light-years away, the shadow should span about forty micro-arcseconds. In 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope, a planet-sized array of radio dishes, imaged a bright ring forty-two micro-arcseconds across, around a dark center. In 2022 it imaged Sagittarius A star, at the center of our own galaxy. Both are consistent with Kerr black holes; models of the glowing gas favor spinning ones, but neither spin has been pinned down yet.

## III. The quadrupole formula, derived

_Scene `s11_quadrupole.py::Quadrupole`_

In part one, we found that ripples in spacetime obey a wave equation, and quoted the formula for the waves a source makes. Let's derive it. Start from the linearized field equation. Like the equations of electromagnetism, it's solved by a retarded integral: the field here and now adds up the source everywhere, as it was when the signal left it. Far from a slowly moving source, the delay is the same for all of it, and the spatial part of the wave is four over r times the integral of the stresses, T i j. That looks hard to know: it involves pressures and stresses deep inside the source.

Conservation comes to the rescue. Energy and momentum are conserved: two equations, one for the energy density and one for the momentum density. Differentiate the first with respect to time and substitute the second: the second time derivative of the energy density is a double divergence of the stresses. Now multiply by y i y j and integrate by parts, twice. The left side becomes the second time derivative of the mass distribution's second moment; the right side, twice the integrated stress. So the wave is two over r, times the second time derivative of the quadrupole moment. Only its traceless, transverse part is physical, which is the formula we quoted in part one.

How much energy does the wave carry? Gravitational waves carry energy and momentum, with an effective energy density, averaged over a few wavelengths, given by this expression, due to Richard Isaacson. Integrate the flux over a big sphere around the source, and insert our wave. The angular integral of the transverse projection comes out to eight pi over five, which we checked numerically, and the result is the quadrupole formula: the luminosity is one fifth of the third time derivative of Q, squared. In everyday units, there's a factor G over c to the fifth, which is tiny. A huge steel beam spun almost to breaking radiates about ten to the minus twenty-nine watts. To radiate appreciably, you need stars' worth of mass moving at a good fraction of the speed of light.

Now apply it to two stars, or black holes, in a circular orbit. The waves spiral out from the binary like water from a rotating sprinkler, here computed in the orbital plane. Pink is stretching in one direction, blue in the other. The quadrupole moment of the pair is mu a squared times n i n j, where n points along the line between them. The radiation is strongest along the orbit's axis, eight times stronger than in its plane.

## The chirp, derived, and the binary pulsar

_Scene `s12_inspiral.py::Inspiral`_

The energy radiated has to come from somewhere: the orbit. The orbital energy of the pair is minus mu M over two a. It decreases at the rate the waves carry energy away. Solve for the separation: the orbit shrinks, slowly at first, and faster and faster as a gets smaller. Integrating, two bodies starting a distance a naught apart merge after five two-hundred-fifty-sixths of a naught to the fourth, over mu M squared. That fourth power is brutal: halve the distance, and the time to merge drops sixteen-fold.

What a detector hears is the frequency. The gravitational-wave frequency is twice the orbital frequency, which Kepler's law ties to the separation. Differentiate it, substitute the shrinking rate, and eliminate a. Something remarkable happens: the two masses only appear in one combination, mu to the three fifths times M to the two fifths, called the chirp mass. Integrating gives the frequency as a function of the time left before coalescence. It rises without limit, a chirp. This is exactly the formula part one quoted, and laid over LIGO's data. Now we've derived it, five over two hundred fifty-six and all.

Here it is for two black holes with the masses of LIGO's first detection. Two tenths of a second before they merge, they're a thousand kilometers apart and the wave is at thirty hertz. As they spiral together, the frequency and the amplitude both climb, faster and faster: the chirp. In the last instants, a few hundred kilometers apart, our leading-order formula breaks down, and only a full numerical solution of Einstein's equation can follow the merger itself.

The first evidence that gravitational waves carry energy came from a pair of neutron stars. In 1974, Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor found a pulsar in a binary orbit: two neutron stars, each about one point four solar masses, orbiting every seven and three quarter hours, one of them a spinning beacon that ticks seventeen times a second, a superb clock. The orbit is eccentric, so the quadrupole formula needs a correction, worked out by Peters and Mathews: it radiates almost twelve times more than a circular orbit would. The prediction: the orbital period should shrink by two point four trillionths of a second, every second.

A shrinking period shows up as a drift in the time of closest approach, growing like the square of the elapsed time. Here's general relativity's prediction since 1974: by now, more than a hundred seconds. The measured decay, after correcting for the acceleration of the pulsar in our galaxy, agrees with the prediction to two parts in a thousand. The orbit's ellipse also turns, by four degrees a year: Mercury's effect, in a far stronger field. And a second system, the Double Pulsar, now agrees with the quadrupole formula to a few parts in a hundred thousand. In about three hundred million years, these two stars will merge, ending with a chirp like the ones LIGO hears.

## Ringdown and Hawking's area theorem

_Scene `s13_ringdown.py::Ringdown`_

When two black holes merge, the result is a single, distorted black hole, which settles down by ringing, like a struck bell. Small ripples of a black hole obey a wave equation in the tortoise coordinate, found by Tullio Regge and John Wheeler in 1957, with a potential barrier. The barrier peaks just outside the photon sphere, where light can orbit: waves near the hole are partly trapped there, leaking slowly inward to the horizon and outward to us.

Let's watch it happen. Send a pulse of gravitational waves toward the barrier, and solve the equation numerically. Part of the pulse crosses the barrier and falls into the hole. Part bounces back, and what comes back isn't a copy of the pulse: it's a ringing, at a definite frequency, dying away. On a logarithmic scale, the ringing's envelope is a straight line: an exponential decay, until a faint power-law tail takes over.

Fitting the ringing in our simulation gives a frequency and a damping rate. They match, to a fraction of a percent, the exact quasinormal frequency, computed by a completely different method, Edward Leaver's continued fraction from 1985. And there's a simple picture behind it: the ringing is waves trapped near the photon sphere, so its frequency is roughly the orbital frequency of light there, and it leaks away at the rate light orbits there are unstable. The crucial point: the frequencies depend only on the final mass and spin. Measure them, and you test whether the remnant really is a Kerr black hole with no hair. For the black hole left by LIGO's first detection, the main tone is at about two hundred and fifty hertz, dying away in four milliseconds.

Here are the last cycles of LIGO's first signal, the real data. After the peak, lay on a ringing with the frequency and damping time predicted for the final black hole, fitting only its amplitude and phase. The signal is faint and noisy here, but the tail end of the collision rings just as a Kerr black hole should.

In 1971, Stephen Hawking proved that this kind of collision can only make black holes bigger. The horizon is made of light rays, and Raychaudhuri's equation applies to them. If they ever started converging, they'd meet within a finite distance. But light rays that meet leave the horizon, and the horizon's light rays never leave it. So they never converge, and the horizon's area never decreases. That limits how much energy a merger can radiate: for two equal non-spinning holes, at most twenty-nine percent of their mass. LIGO's first event radiated about five percent, three solar masses' worth, in a fifth of a second.

In January 2025, LIGO caught the loudest black-hole merger yet, two holes of about thirty-three solar masses each. From the inspiral, you can infer the areas of the two horizons before the merger; from the ringdown, the area after. Before: two hundred and thirty-seven thousand square kilometers. After: three hundred and seventy-three thousand. The collaboration found the area increased, with a confidence of four point four sigma: Hawking's area theorem, tested on real black holes. And the ringdown was loud enough to hear the main tone and its first overtone, at the frequencies a Kerr black hole of that mass and spin should have.

An area that can only grow. That's a lot like another quantity in physics that can only grow: entropy. Is the resemblance a coincidence?

## IV. The laws of black-hole mechanics

_Scene `s14_thermo.py::Thermodynamics`_

In 1972, Jacob Bekenstein, a graduate student of John Wheeler's, asked a simple question. Take a box of hot gas, which has entropy, and drop it into a black hole. From the outside, the gas and its entropy are simply gone, so the total entropy of the universe outside has gone down, violating the second law of thermodynamics. Unless the black hole itself has entropy, which grows when things fall in. And there was an obvious candidate: the one property of a black hole that, like entropy, can only increase. Its area.

If area is like entropy, what's like temperature? Hover a rocket above a black hole. The acceleration it needs grows without bound as it nears the horizon. But measured from far away, redshifted by the same factor that slows its clocks, it tends to a finite limit: one over four M. This is the surface gravity, kappa. For a spinning hole it's this, and it turns out to be the same everywhere on the horizon of any stationary black hole, just as temperature is the same everywhere in a body in equilibrium.

The analogy goes deeper. The area of a Kerr horizon is a function of the hole's mass and angular momentum. Differentiate it: the partial derivatives are exactly the surface gravity over eight pi, and the horizon's angular velocity. So a small change in mass splits into kappa over eight pi times the change in area, plus Omega H times the change in angular momentum: the work done spinning it up. Compare the first law of thermodynamics: energy changes by temperature times entropy change, plus work. Temperature goes with kappa; entropy goes with area.

In 1973, James Bardeen, Brandon Carter and Stephen Hawking laid out the full parallel. The zeroth law: surface gravity is uniform on the horizon, like temperature in equilibrium. The first law we've just derived. The second law is the area theorem. And the third: you can't reduce kappa to zero, an extremal black hole, in a finite number of steps, just as you can't reach absolute zero. But there was a catch, and Hawking himself insisted on it. A body with a temperature radiates. A black hole, classically, absorbs everything and emits nothing, so its temperature must be zero. The analogy looked like just that: an analogy.

## Hawking radiation

_Scene `s15_hawking.py::Hawking`_

In 1974, Hawking applied quantum field theory to a collapsing star, expecting to confirm that black holes are perfectly black. He found the opposite. Here's the heart of it. Look at light rays leaving from just outside the horizon. A ray starting a tenth of the horizon radius out escapes quickly. One starting a hundred times closer takes four M times the log of ten longer, and so on: every factor of ten closer delays the escape by the same amount. Turn that around. The light reaching a distant observer late, at time u, left from an exponentially thin layer near the horizon. Rays arriving later and later trace back to a region squeezed by a factor of e every four M: the past position of a ray depends exponentially on when it arrives, with rate kappa, the surface gravity.

Why does that matter? In quantum field theory, what counts as a particle depends on what counts as a positive-frequency wave, and that depends on whose clock you use. A wave that oscillates smoothly in the past reaches the distant observer stretched exponentially: rapid at first, then frozen. Take its Fourier transform with respect to the observer's time. It isn't purely positive frequency any more: it contains negative frequencies too, which means particles have been created from the vacuum. And the ratio of negative to positive is exactly e to the minus two pi omega over kappa. We computed the transform numerically: the dots fall right on the line. That's a Boltzmann factor. The particles come out with a thermal, Planck spectrum, at temperature kappa over two pi: one over eight pi M.

There's a second, astonishingly short route to the same temperature. Measure distance from the horizon with rho. Near the horizon, the metric becomes flat spacetime as described by accelerated observers. Now do something strange: make time imaginary. The metric becomes rho squared times d tau over four M squared, plus d rho squared, which is the flat plane in polar coordinates, with tau over four M as the angle.

For the plane to be smooth at the origin, the angle has to go around exactly two pi, so imaginary time must repeat with period eight pi M. Otherwise the origin is the tip of a cone. And in quantum statistical mechanics, a system whose fields are periodic in imaginary time, with period h-bar over k T, is at temperature T. So a black hole has temperature one over eight pi M, the same answer, as Gibbons and Hawking showed in 1977. With the constants put back, that's this formula, which is carved on Hawking's memorial stone in Westminster Abbey.

Here's that geometry, the plane of r and imaginary time, embedded exactly as a surface. Far away, it's a cylinder: imaginary time going around. At the horizon, it closes off. With the right period, the tip is smooth, like the end of a cigar. With any other period, it ends in a sharp cone. The geometry itself picks out the temperature.

Put the numbers in. A black hole of one solar mass has a temperature of sixty billionths of a kelvin, and heavier ones are colder still: far colder than the cosmic microwave background, so every black hole we know of absorbs more than it emits. Only one lighter than about sixty percent of the Moon's mass would be hotter than the sky around it. The temperature fixes the entropy: one quarter of the horizon area, in Planck units. Bekenstein's guess was right, with the factor now fixed. For a solar-mass black hole, that's ten to the seventy-seven, about ten billion billion times the entropy of the Sun itself. And a body that radiates loses mass: left alone, a black hole evaporates, in a time proportional to its mass cubed, around ten to the sixty-seven years for a solar mass. Whether the information that fell in comes back out in the radiation, and how, is still one of the deepest open questions in physics.

## V. The expanding universe: Friedmann's equations

_Scene `s16_friedmann.py::Friedmann`_

Finally, the biggest solution of all: the universe. On the largest scales, the universe looks the same everywhere and in every direction. If that's exactly true, space must have the same curvature at every point, and there are only three possibilities. Positive curvature, like a sphere: a closed universe, where triangles have more than one hundred eighty degrees. Zero curvature: flat space. Or negative curvature, a hyperbolic space, drawn here in Poincaré's disk, where triangles have less than one hundred eighty degrees, and the circumference of a circle grows exponentially with its radius.

Space with constant curvature, multiplied by a scale factor a that can change with time: this is the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric, where k is plus one, zero, or minus one. Galaxies sit at fixed coordinates, and all distances between them grow in proportion to a of t. From any one galaxy, all the others recede, at speeds proportional to their distance: Hubble's law, with the Hubble rate a dot over a.

Now run the machine from part one. The Christoffel symbols: the Ricci tensor, computed and checked symbolically. Fill the universe with a perfect fluid, at rest relative to the galaxies, and take the time-time component of Einstein's equation. Divide by three: this is the Friedmann equation. The expansion rate squared is set by the density, the curvature, and the cosmological constant.

The space-space components give the acceleration equation, which we found in part one from Baez and Bunn's ball of coffee grounds. And energy conservation says how each ingredient thins out as space expands: matter as one over a cubed, radiation as one over a to the fourth, because each photon is also stretched, and vacuum energy not at all.

Here are the three ingredients of our universe, with their measured amounts, against the scale factor. Radiation dominated early on, until the universe was about a three-thousand-four-hundredth of its present size. Then matter took over, and only recently, at a redshift of about a third, did the cosmological constant overtake matter. The Friedmann equation also says how much density makes space flat: the critical density, about five protons per cubic meter today. The departure from it measures curvature, and our universe turns out to be flat to within a fraction of a percent.

Solve the Friedmann equation, and each mix of ingredients gives its own history. All of these universes expand at today's measured rate. A closed universe of matter expands, stops, and recollapses. A flat one of matter alone keeps slowing forever; an open one coasts. A universe of pure vacuum energy expands exponentially. Ours, matter plus a cosmological constant, decelerated for its first seven billion years, and now accelerates, heading toward the exponential. Einstein himself had wanted a static universe, and added the cosmological constant to hold it up; but that balance is unstable. Alexander Friedmann saw in 1922 that the equation demands a universe that changes, and in 1929 Edwin Hubble found that it expands.

## Light in an expanding universe: redshift and horizons

_Scene `s17_horizons.py::Horizons`_

How do we know the universe expands? Through light. A light ray moving through flat, expanding space covers a comoving distance d t over a in each moment. Two successive crests of a light wave, sent a moment apart, travel the same comoving distance. So the interval between them grows in proportion to the scale factor. The wavelength stretches by exactly the factor the universe expanded while the light was in flight. That's the cosmological redshift: not a Doppler shift through space, but space itself stretching the wave.

Expansion makes distances and horizons subtle, and one picture sorts them out. Time goes up, distance from us across, computed for our universe. Galaxies drift apart along these grey lines. Our past light cone, everything we can see now, is this yellow teardrop: light that reaches us from the most distant sources was emitted when those sources were close to us, and the expansion then carried the light away before it could make headway toward us. The teal curve is the Hubble sphere, where galaxies recede at the speed of light. Galaxies beyond it recede faster than light, and we still see many of them: nothing moves faster than light locally; space between us is expanding.

Now divide out the expansion, and use comoving distance. And finally, replace time by conformal time, the distance light could have traveled. In these coordinates, light moves at forty-five degrees again, like in a Penrose diagram. The particle horizon, the farthest anything we see could be today, is forty-six billion light-years away. And the event horizon: events beyond it today, sixteen and a half billion light-years away, will never be seen by us, because the expansion is accelerating.

The farthest light we can see is the cosmic microwave background, released when the universe became transparent, a few hundred thousand years after the Big Bang. The places that emitted it now lie on a sphere forty-five billion light-years away. But at the moment of emission, each of those places could only have received signals from a tiny region, drawn here to scale: about a degree across, as seen from Earth. So the sky contains some ten thousand patches that had never been in causal contact. Yet they all have the same temperature, to one part in a hundred thousand. How did they know? This is the horizon problem, and the leading proposed answer, inflation, adds an early epoch of exponential expansion, before the history we've drawn.

These equations are still being tested at the largest scales. The expansion rate inferred from the microwave background, assuming this model, is about sixty-seven kilometers per second per megaparsec. Measured directly from nearby supernovae, it's about seventy-three and a half. The disagreement, now at five to seven sigma, is called the Hubble tension. And maps of millions of galaxies from the DESI survey hint that dark energy may change over time, which a cosmological constant can't. The evidence isn't yet decisive, and DESI's full results are expected in 2027.

## Recap

_Scene `s18_outro.py::Outro`_

Let's put it together. Choosing coordinates that follow light showed us that the horizon is an ordinary place to cross, and that inside, r becomes time. Raychaudhuri's equation showed that gravity always focuses, so once a trapped surface forms, a singularity is unavoidable. Spinning black holes drag spacetime around them, and store energy that can be mined, while their area only grows.

Gravitational waves carry energy away, and from that one fact we derived the chirp of merging black holes and the slow death of the binary pulsar. Quantum theory turned the laws of black-hole mechanics into real thermodynamics, with a temperature and an entropy. And the same equation, applied to everything at once, describes an expanding universe, with horizons of its own.

All of it from one line, derived from a falling elevator, and reaching to the edge of the observable universe. Thanks for watching.
