# Economics, Built: Part 42, Social Insurance

Part 42 of a sixty-eight-part course in economics (see [the series plan](../README.md) and
[the syllabus](../SYLLABUS.md)), the last of Act VIII, *Work and the State*. It asks what the state should insure,
and how generously. Its answer: what private markets cannot, meaning risks that strike everyone at once, risks that
adverse selection unravels, and risks settled before anyone can buy cover; and generously until the last dollar is
worth a dollar, which turns on two measurable numbers, how far consumption falls when the risk strikes and how much
behaviour changes when it is insured.

It opens on Oregon's lottery of 2008, drawn as 900 dots of 100 people each: about 90,000 people on the list (91,675
names on the final list) for about 10,000 places in Medicaid, with about 30,000 names drawn month by month from March
to September, the first randomized study of insuring the uninsured in the United States. From there it covers:

- why markets underinsure: pooling on the Island (a 3% chance of losing one's job in a normal year, 9% in a recession
  one year in seven): the spread of the claim per member falls with the square root of the pool when risks are
  independent (5% of the mean at 10,000 members) but never below 55% when recessions strike everyone at once;
  adverse selection, moral hazard, common shocks and risks settled before birth; America's continued claims as a
  share of the unemployed, 35% on average since 1967, 28% in 2019, 72% in 2020 and 25% in 2026;
- a game: the Island's best unemployment benefit (a third of the wage: 84% find work, a tax of 6.5% pays for it,
  consumption falls 15% on losing the job), and the welfare curve behind it;
- Baily and Chetty's formula derived from the model and checked: at the welfare-maximizing benefit both sides equal
  0.397; its measurable form, risk aversion times the consumption drop against the elasticity;
- measuring both sides: the Island week by week (half the wage for 26 weeks, then a fifth: the weekly chance of a
  job rises from 6.3% to 13.4% as benefits run out; a mean spell of 12.8 weeks; a 10% higher benefit lengthens it
  3.8%, an elasticity of 0.41; 30 weeks instead of 20 cut job finding in the first 20 by 10%); Meyer (4-8% longer
  spells per 10%), Card, Chetty and Weber (5-9% and, for severance, 8-12% lower job finding), Schmieder, von Wachter
  and Bender; Gruber (consumption falls about 7%, 2.7% less per 10 points of replacement, over three times as much
  without benefits) and Ganong and Noel (6% at the start, 12% at exhaustion); the risk aversion that would justify
  benefits: 5.7 to 11.4 with a 7% drop, 3.3 to 6.7 with 12%;
- moral hazard or liquidity: Chetty's split on the Island (at the best benefit, liquidity is 28% of the response,
  matching (u'(c_u) - u'(c_e))/u'(c_u)), falling as benefits rise; Chetty's 60% for America, which makes the gain side
  1.5 and the optimal benefit above half the wage;
- health insurance: Arrow, Pauly and RAND (2,750 families, more than 7,700 people; the 95% coinsurance plan spent
  about 30% less than free care, the 25% plan about 20% less, with health on average not measurably worse); the
  Island's coinsurance trade-off (the patient's remaining risk against care worth less than it costs; the best plan
  pays 28% of each bill; no insurance costs a risk premium of 3.9% of income, free care overuse of 1.2%); the -0.2
  summary and why it is fragile; cost sharing cut highly effective care as much as care of little value;
- Oregon: the lottery as an instrument (on the Island 40% of those drawn are insured against 14% of the rest, a first
  stage of 26 points, close to Oregon's 25); the results (outpatient use +35%, prescriptions +15%, emergency visits
  +40%; catastrophic medical bills virtually eliminated; depression -9.15 points from 30%; no significant change in
  blood pressure, cholesterol or blood sugar); 200 Island lotteries with a planted one-point effect on blood pressure,
  detected in 14% of runs (the smallest effect reliably detected is 3.2 points), against 77% for depression; the
  value of Medicaid ($0.5 to $1.2 per dollar of cost, with about 60 cents of each government dollar a transfer to
  those who had paid for the uninsured's care);
- an intermission without narration: continued claims for unemployment insurance, 1967-2026, as a tune;
- pensions: pay-as-you-go arithmetic (a 12.4% payroll tax pays 63% of the average wage at 5.1 workers per
  beneficiary, 33% at today's 2.7 and 28% at 2035's projected 2.3); the return on pay-as-you-go against a fund
  (1.5% a year against 3%: a dollar becomes $1.57 rather than $2.43 over 30 years) and the implicit debt whose
  interest is exactly that gap; working longer, notional accounts and pre-funding;
- defaults: Madrian and Shea's firm (37% to 86% participation; 76% stayed at the default); the Island's inertia (37%
  opt-in against 87% automatic at 15 months, 56% still at the default); Chetty and coauthors' Danish passive savers
  (85%) and one cent of saving per dollar of subsidy; Save More Tomorrow;
- disability insurance: screening; the examiner design on the Island (naive gap -57 points of work, the leniency
  instrument -35, the truth for marginal applicants -32); Maestas, Mullen and Strand's range, from nothing for the
  most severe to 50 points for the least;
- help for the working poor: the negative income tax experiments (hours down about 5% for husbands, more for wives
  and single mothers); the 2025 EITC for three or more children (45% of earnings to $17,880, $8,046, withdrawn at
  21.06% from $23,350, gone at $61,555); Eissa and Liebman; the Island's EITC (a 10% larger credit draws in 1.2%
  more parents and 5 cents of each dollar comes back: an MVPF of 1.06); Akerlof's tagging (64% of each dollar reaches
  the needy, against 20% for a universal payment, while 30% of the needy are missed);
- a basic income: a 30% flat tax pays everyone on the Island $19,278 (64% gain, the Gini falls from 0.381 to
  0.267); with an elasticity of a quarter, $17,633, and at most $34,378 at 80%; the trials in Finland, the United
  States and Kenya, and what they cannot show;
- one yardstick: the MVPF; the Island's unemployment insurance (1.27 at a fifth of the wage, exactly one at the best
  benefit, 0.66 at half); Hendren and Sprung-Keyser's 133 policies;
- three dials (the duration elasticity of benefits, filled; the price elasticity of medical spending and the MVPF,
  new); an actuary's arithmetic (a tax of 14.6%, or a benefit of 28%, in 2035); *Called it?* on the 1983 reform's
  75 years of solvency (wrong: depletion is now projected for 2034); a frontier chapter on platform work, long-term
  care and insurance against automation.

## What is computed, and from what

Everything plotted is computed by `compute.py` from Agora's insurance module (`explainer.economics.agora.insurance`),
with the Island's Part 42 truths, or from the Data Room; the narration states the cached numbers.

- **Pooling.** Each member claims one with probability 3% (normal years) or 9% (a recession, probability 0.15): the
  variance of the claim per member is the average within-state variance over n plus the variance of the state's
  mean.
- **Claims.** Monthly averages of weekly continued claims (CCSA) over the number unemployed (UNEMPLOY), averaged by
  year (years with at least six months).
- **The static model.** A job loser chooses search e at cost 0.3856 e^12.81 / 12.81 (calibrated so that, at half the
  wage, 80% find work and the elasticity of staying unemployed, holding the tax fixed, is one half); consumption is
  3 + 1 - tau employed and 3 + b unemployed (3: savings and a partner's income, in wages); CRRA utility with risk
  aversion 2; the tax balances the budget, (1 - e) b = e tau. The formula's elasticity lets the tax adjust.
- **The weekly model.** No savings; weekly benefits of half the wage for 26 weeks, then a fifth; search cost 240 s^1.5
  / 1.5; a job lasts for ever; discount factor 0.999 a week; solved backwards in the weeks of benefit left.
- **Health.** Seven in ten fall ill; illness theta lognormal with median 5% of income and sigma 1.3, capped at 70%;
  a patient paying a share c chooses the share 1 - 0.316 c of the full course, so that the 95% plan spends 30% less
  than free care; untreated harm theta / (2 * 0.316); CRRA utility with risk aversion 3 over income less premium,
  out-of-pocket cost and unrepaired harm; expectations on 4,000 quantiles.
- **The lottery.** 75,000 on the list, 40% drawn; 30% of those drawn take up coverage, and 14% of everyone is covered
  from other sources; planted effects: emergency visits +0.41 (sd 2.4, 25,000 measured), depression -9 points from
  30%, catastrophic bills -4.5 points from 5.5%, systolic pressure -1 mmHg (sd 16) (12,000 measured); 200 runs; the
  run shown is the one with the median first stage.
- **Examiners.** 60,000 applicants with uniform severity, 200 examiners with leniency sd 0.12; without benefits an
  applicant works with probability 0.9 (1 - s)^1.5; two-stage least squares on the leave-one-out allowance rate.
- **Defaults.** 20,000 employees who act on their choice in any month with probability 4.5%; 75% then save.
- **The EITC.** The 2025 parameters for three or more children, single filers; the Island's parents have earnings
  lognormal around $24,000 (sigma 0.45), costs of working exponential with mean $16,000, a welfare benefit of $8,000
  and a payroll tax of 7.65%.
- **A basic income.** 20,000 incomes lognormal around $50,000 (sigma 0.7); incomes scale with (1 - t)^0.25.
- **Pensions.** Benefit over wage = 12.4% times workers per beneficiary; labour force growth 0.5% and wage growth 1%
  against a return of 3%, over a 30-year working life.

## Problems and solutions

**1.** *Pooling: each of 10,000 members claims with probability 5%, independently. What is the standard deviation of
the claim per member, relative to its mean? Now let the chance be 3% or 9% (a recession one year in seven): what is
the floor?*

*Solution.* Independent: sqrt(0.05 x 0.95 / 10,000) = 0.00218, which is 4.4% of the mean, 0.05. With recessions the
mean is 0.15 x 0.09 + 0.85 x 0.03 = 0.039, and however large the pool, the spread of the yearly mean remains:
sqrt(0.15 x 0.85) x 0.06 = 0.0214, or 55% of the mean. Pooling cannot remove it; borrowing across years can.

**2.** *Baily-Chetty: risk aversion 2, consumption falls 10% on losing a job, epsilon = 0.5, e = 0.8. Should benefits
rise or fall? Use the exact left side and the approximation.*

*Solution.* Exactly, (u'(c_u) - u'(c_e))/u'(c_e) = (1/0.9)^2 - 1 = 0.235; approximately, gamma x 0.10 = 0.20. The cost
side is 0.5/0.8 = 0.625. The gain is below the cost, so benefits should fall: a dollar moved to the unemployed is worth
less than the extra unemployment it buys.

**3.** *Liquidity: if 60% of the response of durations to benefits is liquidity, what is the gain side of the formula?
What if it were 20%?*

*Solution.* The gain side is L/(1 - L): 0.6/0.4 = 1.5, or 0.2/0.8 = 0.25. With 60% it exceeds any plausible cost and
benefits should rise; with 20% it is below the cost of problem 2, and they should fall.

**4.** *RAND: spending is $1,000 with 25% coinsurance and $700 with 95%. Find the arc elasticity.*

*Solution.* ((700 - 1,000)/(700 + 1,000)) / ((0.95 - 0.25)/(0.95 + 0.25)) = (-0.176)/(0.583) = -0.30.

**5.** *Oregon: the lottery raises coverage by 25 points and lowers the share screening positive for depression by
2.3 points. What is the effect of coverage? If the reduced form's standard error is 0.9 points, what is the IV
estimate's?*

*Solution.* The Wald estimate divides the reduced form by the first stage: -2.3/0.25 = -9.2 points. Ignoring the
noise in the first stage, the standard error scales the same way: 0.9/0.25 = 3.6 points, so the 95% interval runs
from about -16 to -2. A first stage of a quarter multiplies every uncertainty by four.

**6.** *Pensions: a 12.4% payroll tax, 2.3 workers per beneficiary. What benefit can it pay, as a share of the wage?
What tax keeps today's 33.5%?*

*Solution.* 0.124 x 2.3 = 28.5% of the wage. To pay 33.5%, the tax must be 0.335/2.3 = 14.6%.

**7.** *The EITC: with the 2025 schedule for three children, find the credit and the marginal rate at earnings of
$10,000 and $40,000.*

*Solution.* At $10,000 the credit is 45% of earnings, $4,500, and each extra dollar earned adds 45 cents: a marginal
rate of -45%. At $40,000 it is $8,046 - 0.2106 x ($40,000 - $23,350) = $8,046 - $3,506 = $4,540, and each extra
dollar removes 21.06 cents: a marginal rate of +21.06% (on top of other taxes).

**8.** *Derive Baily's formula: from W = e u(c_e) + (1 - e) u(c_u) - psi(e) and (1 - e) b = e tau, show that
dW/db = 0 gives (u'(c_u) - u'(c_e))/u'(c_e) = epsilon_{1-e,b}/e.*

*Solution.* The worker chooses e to maximize W given b and tau, so by the envelope theorem the change in e has no
first-order effect on W through the worker's own choice: dW/db = (1 - e) u'(c_u) - e u'(c_e) dtau/db. From the budget,
tau = (1 - e) b / e, so dtau/db = (1 - e)/e - (b/e^2) de/db. Substituting, dW/db = (1 - e)[u'(c_u) - u'(c_e)] +
u'(c_e) (b/e) de/db. With epsilon = dlog(1 - e)/dlog b = -(b/(1 - e)) de/db, the last term is -u'(c_e)(1 - e)
epsilon/e. Setting dW/db = 0 and dividing by (1 - e) u'(c_e) gives the formula. On the Island both sides equal 0.397
at the best benefit.
