## The music of the primes

_Scene `s01_hook.py::Hook`_

Here are the prime numbers. Two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen: the whole numbers that can't be split into smaller factors. They're the atoms of arithmetic, and yet they seem to appear almost at random.

Count them as you go, and you get a staircase that climbs by one at every prime. It's jagged and irregular in its details.

But zoom out, say to a hundred thousand, and the staircase follows a smooth curve almost perfectly. The primes are random in the small, but astonishingly regular in the large.

In 1859, Bernhard Riemann found the reason. He showed that the staircase is exactly equal to a smooth curve, plus a sum of waves, and that each wave comes from a single point where one particular function, the zeta function, equals zero.

Add the waves one at a time, and the smooth curve bends, step by step, into the exact staircase of the primes. The zeros of zeta are like the frequencies in the music of the primes.

Riemann guessed that all of these zeros lie on a single straight line. That guess, the Riemann hypothesis, is perhaps the most famous open problem in mathematics. It has been open for 167 years, and there's a million-dollar prize for a proof.

On October sixth, 2026, OpenAI released 722 mathematical manuscripts written by an unreleased AI model. Among them is a claimed proof of something weaker than the Riemann hypothesis, but long out of reach: the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. It says that no zero lies to the right of the line where the real part is seven eighths.

In this video, we'll build up what the Riemann hypothesis really says, and why its zeros control the primes. Then we'll see what the new result claims and how its proof works, and finally what has been checked, and what it does not prove.

## How many primes?

_Scene `s02_counting.py::CountingPrimes`_

Let's start with the most basic question. How many primes are there up to some number x? Call that count pi of x. The pi here has nothing to do with circles; it's just the letter p, for prime. There are four primes up to ten, and ten primes up to thirty.

Here are the real counts, going up by powers of ten. Up to a thousand, there are 168 primes. Up to a million, 78,498. Up to a billion, about fifty-one million.

Now look at the ratio, x over pi of x. That's the average gap between consecutive primes. Each time x grows by a factor of ten, the average gap grows by about 2.3, which is the natural logarithm of ten.

As a teenager, Gauss noticed this pattern, and guessed that near a number x, the primes have density about one over the logarithm of x. For example, a random number near a million is prime with probability about one in fourteen.

Add up that density, and you get the logarithmic integral, li of x: the area under the curve one over log t. That's the smooth curve we saw a moment ago.

The prime number theorem, proved in 1896 by Hadamard and by de la Vallée Poussin, says Gauss was right in the limit: the ratio of pi of x to li of x tends to one.

But how good is this approximation, really? Here is the error, li of x minus pi of x, all the way out to ten to the twenty-four. The error is tiny compared with the count. In fact, it has only about half as many digits.

Half as many digits means the error is around the square root of x, or even a bit less. Does that stay true forever? That question is exactly the Riemann hypothesis. And to see why, we need Riemann's function.

## Zeta: a bridge from primes to analysis

_Scene `s03_euler.py::EulerProduct`_

Riemann's tool was a function that Euler had studied more than a century earlier. Take every whole number, raise it to some power s, and add up the reciprocals. This is the zeta function.

At s equals two, laying the terms end to end, they add up to pi squared over six, as Euler famously showed.

At s equals one, it's the harmonic series. Its terms shrink too slowly, and the sum grows without bound, like the logarithm of the number of terms.

Euler's great discovery was that the same function can be written using only the primes. Here's how. Multiply the whole sum by one over two to the s. Since one over n to the s, times one over two to the s, is one over two n to the s, that gives exactly the even terms. Subtract, and every even term vanishes.

Now multiply what's left by one over three to the s, and subtract: every remaining multiple of three disappears. Then five, then seven. Each prime sieves out its multiples, exactly like the sieve of Eratosthenes.

Eleven, thirteen, and every other prime each take their turn, until only the one at the very beginning is left. So zeta of s, times all of these factors, equals exactly one.

Flip it around, and zeta becomes a product over the primes: Euler's product formula.

It's really the fact that every number factors into primes in exactly one way. Each factor on the right is a geometric series, and multiplying them out produces every term one over n to the s exactly once, built from n's prime factors. The left side is about all whole numbers, the kind of thing you can do analysis with. The right side is about the primes alone.

Euler put it to work right away. At s equals one, the sum on the left is the harmonic series, which diverges. So the product on the right must be infinite too. But a product of finitely many factors is finite. So there must be infinitely many primes: a new proof of Euclid's theorem, and the first hint that the primes are hidden inside zeta.

## Going complex: spirals and continuation

_Scene `s04_complex.py::ComplexZeta`_

Riemann's leap was to let s be a complex number: s equals sigma plus i t. But then what does n to the minus s even mean? Split it in two. n to the minus sigma is an ordinary positive number, a length. And n to the minus i t is e to the minus i t log n: a point on the unit circle, which means a rotation, by an angle of minus t times log n.

So each term of zeta becomes an arrow. Here's sigma equal to one and a half, with t equal to zero. Every arrow points to the right, and placed tip to tail, they add up along the real line.

Now turn up t. Each arrow rotates, and the n-th one turns at a rate proportional to log n, so the later arrows spin faster. The chain curls into a spiral, and its endpoint, right here, is zeta of s.

Now slide sigma down toward one. The arrows shrink more slowly, and the spiral takes longer and longer to settle. Below one, it never settles at all. Keep adding terms, and the spiral just keeps unwinding outward, forever. The series diverges.

And yet, look closely at that diverging spiral. It winds around a fixed point, and that center stays put no matter how many terms you add. You can pin it down by subtracting off the steady outward drift, which is N to the one minus s, over one minus s. When sigma is above one, this is just the ordinary sum. Below one, it still gives an answer, and it's the only way to extend zeta smoothly to these values of s. This is analytic continuation, and it defines zeta everywhere in the complex plane, except at s equals one.

Now let's go hunting for a zero. Keep sigma at one half and raise t. Watch the center. At t equal to 14.1347, the spiral winds around a center that sits exactly at zero. This is the first nontrivial zero of the zeta function.

## Where zeta vanishes: the Riemann hypothesis

_Scene `s05_landscape.py::ZeroLandscape`_

To see all of zeta at once, we'll color the complex plane. Each point s gets a color for the direction that zeta of s points in, and a brightness for its size. Wherever zeta is zero, every color meets at a single dark point.

Where the series converges, on the right, zeta is calm, close to one. Then analytic continuation fills in everything else.

The bright spot at s equals one is the pole, where zeta blows up: that's our harmonic series again.

On the negative real axis, there are zeros at minus two, minus four, minus six, and so on. These are called the trivial zeros. They're forced by a simple factor in zeta's symmetry, and they're completely understood.

All the other zeros, the nontrivial ones, live inside the critical strip, where the real part is between zero and one. And look where they are: at heights 14.13, 21.02, 25.01, 30.42, and 32.94. Every one of them sits on the line where the real part is exactly one half.

There's a deeper reason for this line. Riemann proved a functional equation: after multiplying zeta by a simple gamma-function factor, the result is unchanged when you swap s with one minus s. That's a reflection across the line where the real part is one half.

Zeta also takes mirror-image values above and below the real axis. So if there were ever a zero off the line, it would have to bring three partners with it: one reflected across the critical line, and two more below.

The Riemann hypothesis says that never happens: every nontrivial zero has real part exactly one half. In his 1859 paper, Riemann wrote that this was 'very probable', and that he had set aside the search for a proof, after a few fleeting attempts.

The evidence for it is overwhelming. Along the critical line, zeta can be rotated into a real-valued function, Hardy's Z function. Every zero on the line is a place where this curve crosses the axis. Here are the first ten.

In 1914, Hardy proved that infinitely many zeros lie on the line. Later work showed that a positive fraction do, more than forty percent by 1989. And computers have checked the zeros one by one: in 2021, Platt and Trudgian verified that the first twelve trillion zeros, every one up to a height of three trillion, lie exactly on the line. This year, a preprint whose argument is credited to an AI model, Anthropic's Claude, checked by Levent Alpöge and Ralph Furman, pushed the proven fraction past two thirds, and a second, independent proof followed.

Hilbert put the problem on his famous list in 1900, and in 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute offered a million dollars for a proof. But why should the primes care where these zeros are?

## How the zeros build the primes

_Scene `s06_explicit.py::ExplicitFormula`_

To see the connection, it's cleanest to count primes with weights. Instead of adding one at each prime, add the logarithm of p, and also count prime powers, like four, eight, and nine. This gives Chebyshev's function, psi of x, another staircase.

The weights make it simpler: the prime number theorem now just says that psi of x is close to x.

Riemann's insight, made rigorous by von Mangoldt, is an exact formula for this staircase. It's x, minus a sum over all the nontrivial zeros, rho, of x to the rho over rho, minus a couple of small correction terms. That's it. Exact, for every x.

Each zero, together with its mirror image below the axis, contributes one wave. Write rho as beta plus i gamma. Then x to the rho is x to the beta, times e to the i gamma log x, and the pair of conjugate zeros adds up to a cosine. The height gamma sets the frequency of the wave, and the real part beta sets how fast its amplitude grows.

Here's the wave from the first zero, at height 14.13. And here are the next two. On their own, they don't look like anything at all.

But start from the smooth line, x, and subtract the waves, one zero at a time. One. Two. Five. Twenty. A hundred. A thousand. The line bends, and bends, until it has become the staircase, jumping by log p at every prime power. Every prime is hiding in the zeros.

And it works in reverse. Take only the primes and their powers below a million, and add up cosine waves, one for each, with frequency log p. As a function of t, the total is mostly noise, except for sharp peaks. And the peaks fall exactly at the zeros of zeta: 14.13, 21.02, 25.01, and on and on.

The zeros are the frequencies of the primes, and the explicit formula is their Fourier series. The heights of the zeros decide where the steps fall. Their real parts decide how big the waves are. And that second fact is what the Riemann hypothesis is really about.

## Why the real part matters

_Scene `s07_error.py::ErrorTerm`_

Every wave grows like x to the beta. If a zero sits on the critical line, its wave grows like the square root of x. But if there were a zero with real part 0.9, its wave would grow like x to the 0.9, and in the long run it would dwarf every wave from the critical line.

So the size of the error in counting primes is set by the rightmost zero. Call Theta the largest real part of any nontrivial zero. Then the error in psi of x, and in pi of x, is about x to the Theta, and no smaller power of x will do.

The Riemann hypothesis says Theta is one half: the error is about the square root of x, the smallest it could possibly be. Von Koch showed in 1901 that these are exactly equivalent. That's the precise sense in which the primes are as regular as they can be.

So what do we actually know? Theta is at least a half, since there are zeros on the line. And Theta is at most one, because to the right of that line the Euler product converges, and a convergent product of nonzero factors can't be zero. The prime number theorem itself, in 1896, came down to showing that there are no zeros on the line real-part-one.

Then came zero-free regions. In 1899, de la Vallée Poussin showed there are no zeros in a thin region hugging that line. But here it is drawn to scale, with the height on a logarithmic axis. As you go up, the region gets thinner and thinner, like one over the logarithm of the height.

In 1958, Vinogradov and Korobov found a region that shrinks more slowly, though with constants this good it only wins at astronomically large heights. And it still pinches toward the line. For more than a century, every zero-free region known has had this shape.

And a region that pinches toward the line isn't enough to push Theta below one. Far enough up, a zero could still be hiding arbitrarily close to the line real-part-one, and the error term would be almost as big as x itself.

The statement that Theta is strictly less than one, that there's a zero-free strip of fixed width, the same at every height, is called the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. For the primes, it says the error is at most x to some fixed power less than one: a power saving. Before October 2026, no one could prove that there are no zeros past real part 0.999999.

## Coin flips: the Möbius function

_Scene `s08_mobius.py::MobiusRandomness`_

There's another way to state the Riemann hypothesis, and it's the one the new proof uses. Meet the Möbius function, mu of n. It's zero if n is divisible by a perfect square. Otherwise it's plus one if n has an even number of prime factors, and minus one if it has an odd number.

So six, which is two times three, gets plus one. Seven gets minus one. Twelve has a square factor, so it gets zero. And thirty, a product of three primes, gets minus one.

Why this odd function? Flip Euler's product upside down. One over zeta is a product of factors one minus p to the minus s. Multiply it out: each squarefree number appears once, with a sign for each prime in it. Those signs are exactly mu. One over zeta of s is the sum of mu of n over n to the s.

Now add up the signs. M of x is the running total of mu, here all the way out to ten million. It wanders up and down like a random walk, just like a running tally of coin flips.

A random walk of x steps typically drifts about the square root of x away from zero. And so does M. Up to ten million, it never gets more than about 0.57 times the square root of x away, once x is past a couple hundred.

Here's the remarkable fact. The Riemann hypothesis is true exactly when M of x stays below x to the one half plus epsilon, for every epsilon. In other words, the signs of mu are as random as coin flips, in the strongest sense. Square-root cancellation.

Why does cancellation in mu keep zeros away? Follow the chain. Suppose the partial sums of mu grow no faster than x to some power theta. Then the series for one over zeta converges whenever the real part of s is bigger than theta. A convergent series gives a finite number, so one over zeta is finite there. But at a zero of zeta, one over zeta would be infinite. So there are no zeros to the right of theta.

So to keep zeros out of a half-plane, it's enough to show the Möbius function cancels: that its sums beat the trivial bound, x, by some fixed power. A bound like x to the seven eighths would mean no zeros beyond seven eighths. That is precisely the shape of OpenAI's argument, applied to a smoothed and twisted version of this sum.

## Primes in progressions, and a ghost

_Scene `s09_characters.py::DirichletL`_

Riemann's zeta is just the first of an infinite family. Sort the odd primes by their remainder when divided by four. Some are one more than a multiple of four, others one less. In the long run, each class gets half of the primes. Up to ten million, it's 332,180 against 332,398.

But it's a race, and the three team leads almost all the time. It falls behind for the first time at 26,861. Chebyshev noticed this bias in 1853, and to understand races like this one, you need more than zeta.

To separate the two classes, Dirichlet attached a repeating pattern of signs to the remainders: plus one, zero, minus one, zero. This is a Dirichlet character, chi. Put chi into the zeta series and you get an L-function, which also has an Euler product over the primes.

There's one for every modulus q, and every pattern of this kind, and their zeros control how the primes split among the remainders mod q. The generalized Riemann hypothesis says that all of their nontrivial zeros lie on the critical line, too.

For these L-functions there's a famous ghost. The classical zero-free regions leave room for exactly one possible exception: a single real zero, extremely close to one, for an L-function whose character takes only the values plus and minus one. It's called a Landau–Siegel zero. If one existed, its wave in the explicit formula would be almost as big as x itself, and the primes would avoid half the remainders mod q for an enormously long time.

Nobody has been able to rule these ghosts out. Siegel proved in 1935 that they can't be too close to one, but his proof is ineffective: it can't tell you how large q has to be before the bound kicks in. Many theorems in number theory carry an asterisk because of this.

That's why the new claim is stated for every Dirichlet L-function at once, with a single line, seven eighths, that works for every modulus. That kills the ghost: a real zero near one would sit to the right of seven eighths. A separate nine-page manuscript in the release also excludes these zeros directly.

If it's correct, several old questions fall as consequences, as the manuscripts point out. The smallest number that isn't a perfect square mod a prime p would be at most a fixed power of log p, proving a conjecture of Vinogradov. Square roots mod a prime could be computed by a fast, deterministic algorithm, and Miller's primality test would become deterministic. Euler's list of 65 so-called idoneal numbers would be confirmed complete. And primes would be counted in every arithmetic progression with a power-saving error. Until now, all of these were known only by assuming some form of the generalized Riemann hypothesis.

Here is the main theorem, as claimed. Every Dirichlet L-function, including the Riemann zeta function, has no zeros with real part greater than seven eighths. The same holds for a family of L-functions built on a number system called the Eisenstein integers, which we'll meet in a moment.

Picture it on the critical strip. No zeros to the right of seven eighths, and by the mirror symmetry, none to the left of one eighth either. So every nontrivial zero is confined to a band around the critical line, the same band at every height. That's not the Riemann hypothesis, which demands the single line at one half. But no band of fixed width like this was ever known before.

The release also comes with a formal proof in Lean, a programming language in which a computer checks every logical step. Here is the formal statement for zeta: if the real part of s is greater than seven eighths, then zeta of s is not zero. We'll come back to what has and hasn't been checked.

So how could you possibly prove such a thing? The 49-page version comes with a readable outline, and its strategy turns out to be surprisingly concrete. Let's walk through it.

## A detour through the Eisenstein integers

_Scene `s11_eisenstein.py::EisensteinWorld`_

The proof doesn't work directly with ordinary whole numbers. It moves to the Eisenstein integers: numbers of the form a plus b omega, where omega is a cube root of one, one third of the way around the unit circle. Together they form a triangular lattice in the complex plane.

You can add and multiply them, just like integers. There are six units, numbers that divide one: the six sixth roots of one. And each has a size, its norm, a squared length that is always a whole number.

And, crucially, every Eisenstein integer factors into Eisenstein primes in an essentially unique way. Here are the Eisenstein primes, with their six-fold symmetry. Some ordinary primes, like seven, split into two of them. Others, like five, stay prime.

Why go there? First, nothing is lost. The Eisenstein world has its own zeta function, built by counting its numbers by norm, like these twelve of norm seven. And it factors: it's Riemann's zeta times a Dirichlet L-function. The same thing happens with every Dirichlet character. So if you can keep zeros out of a half-plane for the Eisenstein world's L-functions, you get it for zeta, and for every Dirichlet L-function, for free.

Second, the Eisenstein integers contain the sixth roots of one, and that unlocks a tool: the sixth-power residue symbol. For an Eisenstein prime pi, and any u, the symbol 'u over pi, sub six' is always one of the six sixth roots of one. Here it is for every u in view, with pi equal to four plus omega, drawn as a little arrow pointing at the answer. It's the analog of asking whether u is a perfect square modulo p, but with six possible answers instead of two.

Like the familiar symbol for squares, it repeats: shift u by any multiple of pi, and the answer doesn't change. It's also multiplicative. So the symbol of a perfect sixth power is always one: six times around the clock brings you back to the start. Keep this fact in mind. It's the key to the whole argument.

## Hiding one sum among many

_Scene `s12_amplify.py::Amplification`_

Here's the plan. By the Möbius argument, it's enough to show that one sum cancels: the sum of mu of n, over Eisenstein integers n whose norm is about D. Call it A one of D. There are about D terms, each plus one, minus one, or zero, so trivially, it's at most D in size. We need it to be at most D to some fixed power less than one.

Proving that a single sum cancels is hard. So instead of studying it alone, embed it in a whole family. For each Eisenstein integer u, twist the sum by the sixth-power symbol, u over n. That gives a family of sums, A u of D, one for each u up to some size H. Our sum is the member with u equal to one, since the symbol of one is always one.

The crucial estimate in the paper says that this family is small on average. Add up the squares of all the members, and you get at most about D times H. With about H members, that means a typical member has size only about the square root of D: perfect cancellation, on average, just like coin flips.

But averages can hide outliers. One member could still be large, and it might be ours. Now use the fact from before: the symbol of a sixth power is one. So when u is p to the sixth, for an Eisenstein prime p, the twist does nothing at all, except on the few n that are divisible by p. Our sum doesn't appear in the family just once. It appears over and over again.

You can see it in the toy version: every highlighted bar is, in effect, a copy of the first one.

Now count. Sixth powers of primes up to size H: there are roughly H to the one sixth of them. Each copy contributes about A one squared to the total, and the total is at most D times H.

So here's the squeeze. If our sum were large, its copies alone would eat up more and more of the budget, and past a certain size, they wouldn't fit. That size is D to the eleven twelfths.

Now just divide. A one squared is at most D times H to the five sixths. Take H about the same size as D, and A one squared is at most D to the eleven sixths. So A one is at most D to the eleven twelfths.

That's a power saving! And by the Möbius argument, it means there are no zeros to the right of eleven twelfths. The exponent comes straight from the sixth powers: one, minus half of one sixth. Everything now rests on the crucial estimate. Why should the whole family be small on average?

## Why the family is small

_Scene `s13_theta.py::ThetaReflection`_

Mean squares like this one are the territory of a tool called the large sieve. Here's the idea in a picture. If you have perpendicular directions, then the squared shadows of any vector on them can't add up to more than the vector's own length squared. Now think of each u as giving a direction, with one coordinate for every n. Different u's point in nearly perpendicular directions, so no single vector can line up with too many of them at once.

But for sixth-power symbols, the best general large sieve loses too much. And on its own, it knows nothing special about the Möbius function, which is exactly what we can't control.

The way out is a change of perspective, called Poisson summation. It trades a sum over u for a sum over a dual frequency. Here's the classic example. Sample a wide Gaussian bump at every integer, and add: many terms. Poisson summation says this equals a sum of narrow bumps, where essentially one term survives. Both sides give exactly the same number. Long sums turn into short ones, and short into long.

When you Poisson-sum a character like our sixth-power symbol, what comes out are Gauss sums. A Gauss sum adds up the character's values, each multiplied by a rotating unit arrow. Here's one for a sixth-order character mod 37. The walk wanders around, but it always ends at distance exactly the square root of p from where it started.

And now something special happens. An identity going back to Hasse, and used by Heath-Brown, says that mu of n, times the sextic Gauss sum that Poisson summation produced, equals a cubic Gauss sum, up to simple factors of size one. The Möbius function disappears. The randomness we couldn't control gets absorbed into Gauss sums. That's the reason for sixth powers: with ordinary quadratic symbols, the flip just hands you back another Möbius sum.

Cubic Gauss sums have a history. In 1846, Kummer computed their angles for every prime up to 500, and found them bunched toward one end, in proportions of roughly three to two to one. Here is the same computation: twenty-four, fourteen, seven.

Go further, out to sixty thousand, and the angles slowly spread out. Heath-Brown and Patterson proved in 1979 that in the limit they're perfectly even. But the slow drift is real too: it's a bias that Patterson predicted, proved assuming the generalized Riemann hypothesis by Dunn and Radziwiłł in 2024.

The reason behind all of this is deep. Kubota and Patterson showed that cubic Gauss sums are the Fourier coefficients of a special function, the cubic theta function, which lives on three-dimensional hyperbolic space and has a hidden symmetry. Its simpler cousin is Jacobi's theta function, whose symmetry is exactly the Gaussian identity we just saw.

Applying that symmetry turns the long sum of cubic Gauss sums into a short dual sum. And at each prime, the characters combine: one factor of chi to the minus one, from the Fourier side, two more from the theta side, and chi to the minus three is a sixth root of one cubed: just plus or minus one. The sixth-power family has turned into a quadratic one.

And for quadratic families, there's a sharp tool: Heath-Brown's quadratic large sieve, from 1995, extended to number fields by Goldmakher and Louvel. It bounds mean squares of quadratic symbols with essentially no loss. That gives the bound we need on the dual side, and with it, the crucial estimate for the family. Well, almost.

## Peeling off cubes; from 11/12 to 7/8

_Scene `s14_descent.py::Descent`_

There's one catch. The theta function's coefficients aren't only cubic Gauss sums at squarefree indices. It also has coefficients at n times a cube. So the symmetry controls a 'completed' sum, with all the cube layers included. To isolate the part we want, the proof subtracts the cube layers back off, using Möbius inversion, the same trick Euler's sieve used.

Small cubes are harmless, and are bounded directly. For the large ones, two more rounds of Poisson summation turn the leftover into a mean square of exactly the same kind, but with both of its size parameters shrunk by the same factor. So the problem reproduces itself at a smaller scale.

Each round shrinks the scales by a fixed power, so after finitely many rounds the sums are so short that you can simply count. Unwinding the recursion proves the crucial estimate, and with it, the zero-free half-plane at eleven twelfths.

That's the 49-page argument. The 199-page paper reaches eleven twelfths by a somewhat different route, using what it calls a zero detector, and then starts from that result and pushes further: with prime compensation, asymmetric scales, and two new moment estimates. Together, they move the line from eleven twelfths to seven eighths. The skeleton is the same: compare two exact ways of writing one sum, the theta reflection and Poisson summation, and win by a power.

None of these ingredients is alien. Gauss sums, the cubic theta function, large sieves, and recursive arguments like this one go back to Kummer, Kubota, Patterson, Heath-Brown, and more recently Goldmakher, Louvel, Blomer, Dunn and Radziwiłł. By the paper's own account, what's new is the way the pieces are fitted together, with savings that hold uniformly for every character.

## Is it right, and what does it mean?

_Scene `s15_status.py::Status`_

So, is it right? As of October seventh, one day after the release: the manuscripts have not been peer reviewed. They were posted straight to GitHub. Outside mathematicians have only just begun to work through the human-readable proof.

The strongest evidence is the formal proof. OpenAI's Lean library states the seven-eighths theorem for zeta, for every Dirichlet L-function, and for the Eisenstein family, plus the Landau–Siegel bound. Each is set up as a challenge for an independent checking tool, which allows only Lean's standard axioms, so no step can simply be assumed. The part of the library behind this result runs to about 137,000 lines. OpenAI itself cautions that results without a formalization could have issues.

What does a formal proof buy you? If it compiles as claimed, the theorem is true exactly as stated, for the standard library's definition of zeta. No gap can hide in the logic. What it doesn't give you is understanding: why it works, and what else the method can do. That's what the human reading is for.

The reactions came fast. The mathematician Levent Alpöge wrote: big, big, big, big props for quasi-Riemann and no Siegel zeroes. Others focused on the process. Andrew Sutherland of MIT said, of the release as a whole, 'We should ask for receipts.' Terence Tao criticized the breakneck pace at which AI is harvesting open problems, while Daniel Litt called it great for mathematics.

If it holds up, what does it mean? It's the first zero-free region of fixed width, 130 years after the prime number theorem. The error in counting primes, in any arithmetic progression, drops to at most about x to the seven eighths. The Landau–Siegel ghost is gone, and with it come all those consequences.

What it doesn't do is just as important. The Riemann hypothesis asks for the single line at one half.
