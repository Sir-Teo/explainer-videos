## One of the largest computations ever run

_Scene `s01_hook.py::Hook`_

You ask a question, and a moment later the answer starts streaming back, one token at a time. The model writing it is the result of one of the largest computations people have ever run.

Here is every notable AI model with a published estimate of its training compute, placed by release date and by how many arithmetic operations it took to train, on a logarithmic scale. GPT-2, in 2019: about ten to the twenty-one operations. GPT-3, a year later: more than a hundred times that. GPT-4: about seventy times more again.

The largest runs of 2025 and 2026 are estimated at ten to the twenty-six operations and beyond. At the frontier, training compute has been growing almost five times, every single year.

And so that none of this is a cartoon: every experiment in this video was actually run, in miniature, on the four-core computer that rendered it. All of our training runs together took less than ten to the fifteen operations, roughly a hundred billion times less than a frontier run. The scale is different. The ideas are the same.

This video follows one of those training runs from end to end. It starts with a crawl of the public web, which is filtered, deduplicated and mixed into a dataset of tens of trillions of tokens. Pretraining teaches a network to predict the next token of that text, with the work spread across tens of thousands of GPUs for months. Post-training then turns that raw text predictor into something that follows instructions and reasons, and the result is the assistant.

We'll go in four parts: the data, the training recipe, scaling it out across a cluster, and post-training. Along the way, we'll look at what the labs have actually published about how they do it, as of October 2026.

## Deduplication with MinHash

_Scene `s04_dedup.py::Dedup`_

The web is full of copies: mirrored sites, templates, boilerplate pages. Training on the same text over and over wastes compute and encourages memorization. Here's the biggest cluster of near-duplicates in our eight files: forty-one pages announcing that some domain name is for sale.

To measure how alike two pages are, cut each one into overlapping five-word shingles. The Jaccard similarity is the number of shingles they share, divided by the number of distinct shingles in either. For these two pages, it's 0.78.

But comparing every page with every other is hopeless. Billions of pages means billions of billions of pairs. MinHash gets around this with a beautiful trick. Run every shingle of a page through a random hash function, and keep only the smallest value. Two pages get the same minimum exactly when the smallest-hashed shingle of the two pages combined is one they share, which happens with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity. Repeat with 112 different hash functions, and each page gets a short fingerprint. For our two pages, 86 of the 112 minimums agree: an estimate of 0.77.

Then, instead of comparing fingerprints, hash them. Cut the 112 numbers into 14 bands of eight. Pages that match on all eight numbers of any band land in the same bucket, and become candidates. The chance of that, as a function of similarity, is this S-curve: pages that are eighty percent alike are caught ninety-two percent of the time; pages that are half alike, about five percent.

In our eight files, deduplication removed about three percent of the pages that survived filtering, in 114 clusters: domain-for-sale pages, access-denied pages, default web-hosting pages. Across a hundred crawls, the same pages turn up again and again, so the stakes are much higher, and FineWeb found a surprise. Deduplicating across all the crawls threw away ninety percent of the oldest ones, and what remained trained worse models. So FineWeb deduplicates each crawl separately.

## Quality, mixtures, and tokens

_Scene `s05_quality.py::Quality`_

Hand-written rules catch obvious junk, but they can't tell whether a page is worth learning from. For that, labs use models. FineWeb-Edu asked Llama 3 70B to grade half a million pages for educational value, from zero to five, trained a small, fast classifier to imitate those grades, and ran it over all fifteen trillion tokens. Then it kept the pages scoring three or more.

We ran that same classifier on four hundred random pages that survived our filters. Most score low: shops, services, local news. Keeping three and up, as FineWeb-Edu does, leaves just seven percent. The top scorer looks like this, and a typical low scorer like this.

At full scale, FineWeb-Edu kept a similar eight percent of FineWeb, 1.3 trillion tokens, and models trained on it matched older datasets on knowledge benchmarks with ten times fewer tokens. Since then, the trend is to rewrite rather than throw away. NVIDIA's Nemotron-CC used a language model to rephrase web pages, producing almost two trillion synthetic tokens. Moonshot rephrased Kimi K2's knowledge data, and a factual-recall score rose from 24 to 29 percent. And AI2's OLMo 3 repeats its best data up to seven times, instead of drawing a hard line.

Then the data is mixed. Llama 3's recipe was about half general knowledge, a quarter math and reasoning, seventeen percent code, and eight percent other languages. And in the last stretch of training, the mix shifts toward the very best data. Annealing Llama 3's 8-billion-parameter model this way raised its score on grade-school math problems by twenty-four percent.

The finished datasets are enormous: around fifteen trillion tokens for Llama 3, DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2; twenty-eight and a half trillion for GLM-5; thirty-three trillion for DeepSeek-V4, released in April 2026; thirty-six trillion for Qwen3. Last, the text becomes tokens. A tokenizer, trained with byte-pair encoding, breaks text into frequent pieces; frontier tokenizers have between 128 and 260 thousand of them. We trained a small one, with 2,048, and used it to encode 168 million tokens of FineWeb-Edu: the fuel for the training runs in the next part.

How does byte-pair encoding choose its pieces? Start from single bytes, and count every adjacent pair in the training text. In our 57 million words, the most common pair is a space followed by t: six million times. Merge it into one token, recount, and repeat: space-a, then h-e, i-n, r-e. At the sixth merge, space-t plus h-e makes space-the. Recounting the pairs ourselves gives exactly the merges our tokenizer learned. After two thousand merges, 'the cat sat on the mat', twenty-two bytes, becomes eight tokens.

## Part 2: How big? Scaling laws

_Scene `s06_scaling.py::ScalingLaws`_

Part two: the recipe.

Before training anything, a lab has to decide how big the model should be. The budget is compute, and there's a simple rule for it: training costs about six times the number of parameters, times the number of tokens. Two operations per parameter per token on the forward pass, a multiply and an add, and about four more on the backward pass. So a fixed budget is a fixed area: you can spend it on a big model trained on fewer tokens, or a small model trained on many. Which is better?

Check the rule on a real model. Llama 3.1's largest version has 405 billion parameters, so every token costs six times that: 2.4 trillion operations. It was trained on 15.6 trillion tokens. Multiply, and the whole run comes to 3.8 times ten to the twenty-five operations. Epoch AI's independent estimate of that run: the same, 3.8 times ten to the twenty-five.

The way to find out is an experiment, so we ran one. Pick a compute budget, and train models of several sizes, each on exactly the number of tokens the budget allows. Plot final loss against model size, and you get a valley: too small a model can't hold what it sees, too large a model doesn't get to see enough. Repeat at four budgets, twenty-five runs in all, and fit a parabola to each valley floor. The best size grows with the budget, as a power law, with an exponent of about three quarters. That's close to what OpenAI found in 2020 with small models. The much larger study we'll see next found about one half, and later work traced the gap to details of how very small models like ours are measured and tuned.

In 2022, DeepMind's Chinchilla paper did this with more than four hundred models, up to sixteen billion parameters, and fitted a formula for the loss as a function of model size and data. Here is the landscape it describes, with the coefficients as corrected by a 2024 replication; darker means lower loss. Each yellow line is a fixed compute budget. Along each one there's a best point, and together they trace this path: grow parameters and tokens together, about twenty tokens for every parameter. By that measure, GPT-3 had been trained on far too little data, and the 70-billion parameter Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, beat a model four times its size.

The formula also tells you how to split any budget. Since C is six N D, the number of tokens is C over six N, so the loss becomes a function of the model size alone. Set its derivative to zero, and the best size turns out to be a power of the budget: C to the beta over alpha plus beta. With the corrected coefficients, that exponent is 0.51, so model size and data should grow at almost the same rate. Now plug in Chinchilla's own budget, 5.76 times ten to the twenty-three operations. The formula asks for 72 billion parameters and 1.3 trillion tokens, about eighteen tokens per parameter. DeepMind trained Chinchilla with 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens.

But nobody actually stops at twenty. Llama 3's team used the same kind of IsoFLOP experiment, up to ten to the twenty-two operations, to choose the size of their 405-billion-parameter model, and then trained their small models far longer: the 8-billion-parameter Llama 3 saw fifteen trillion tokens, almost nineteen hundred per parameter, and it kept improving. The reason is cost after training: a smaller model trained for longer is much cheaper to run for millions of people. Frontier mixtures of experts land in between, at hundreds to over a thousand tokens per active parameter.

## Architecture and mixture of experts

_Scene `s07_architecture.py::Architecture`_

What gets trained? Still a transformer: the same basic blueprint as GPT-2, which our earlier video took apart piece by piece. But nearly every component has been refined: a simpler normalization, rotary position embeddings, gated MLPs, attention that shares or compresses its keys and values to save memory, and normalization of the queries and keys for stability. Our pocket models use the first three, and QK-norm gets an experiment of its own later on.

The biggest architectural change of the last two years is the mixture of experts. In a standard transformer, every token passes through the whole MLP in every layer. A mixture-of-experts layer replaces that one MLP with many smaller ones, the experts, and a small router that scores them for each token and sends the token to only the top few. So the number of parameters grows with the number of experts, while the computation per token grows only with the number chosen.

In symbols: the router turns the token's vector into one score per expert, with a softmax. Keep the top two, rescale their scores to sum to one, and add up those experts' outputs. With the scores we just saw, 0.38 and 0.31 become 0.55 and 0.45. And the saving is plain arithmetic. DeepSeek-V3 has 671 billion parameters, but each token passes through 37 billion of them, so training costs six times 37 billion operations per token, not six times 671 billion: eighteen times less.

Frontier open models push this hard. DeepSeek-V3 has 256 experts per layer and uses eight of them, plus one shared expert that every token visits: 671 billion parameters in total, but only 37 billion doing work for any one token. Kimi K2 has 384 experts and passes a trillion parameters. And Kimi K3, from July 2026, picks sixteen of 896, for 2.8 trillion parameters. Experts keep getting more numerous, and the fraction used per token keeps shrinking, now around three to five percent.

But routers have a failure mode. Experts that get more tokens improve faster and get chosen even more: the rich get richer. Here are three real runs of our pocket mixture of experts, eight experts, two per token. Watch the share of tokens each expert receives as training goes. With no balancing, the busiest expert ends up with three times its fair share, while another gets almost nothing. The classic fix is an extra loss term that penalizes uneven routing. DeepSeek-V3 used a gentler trick: a bias added to each expert's score, only for choosing experts, nudged up after every step if the expert was underused and down if it was overused, with no extra loss term competing with learning. Here, it balances almost perfectly. In our tiny runs, the auxiliary loss still finished with a slightly lower loss; in DeepSeek's experiments, at billions of parameters, the bias came out ahead.

Here's the arithmetic behind the two fixes. The auxiliary loss multiplies, for each expert, the share of tokens it gets, f, by the average probability the router gives it, P, and adds them up. If every expert gets an eighth, the total is exactly one, its minimum. Plug in our unbalanced run's real shares, taking P close to f, and it's 1.84; training with the loss brought it to 1.07. DeepSeek's bias never touches the loss. After every step, each expert's bias moves by 0.001 toward balance: up if it got fewer tokens than average, down if it got more. By the end of our run, the naturally popular experts carried biases near minus 0.3 and the neglected ones near plus 0.3: just enough to even out the choices.

A few other shifts are reshaping the blueprint, mostly to make long contexts and inference cheaper. Sparse attention lets each token look at only its most relevant earlier tokens. Hybrid models replace three of every four attention layers with cheaper linear-time layers. Multi-token prediction adds heads that guess several tokens ahead, both as extra training signal and to speed up generation. And context windows of a million tokens are now common, reached in stages near the end of training.

## The optimizer: AdamW and Muon

_Scene `s08_optimizer.py::Optimizer`_

Next, the optimizer: the rule that turns each gradient into an update. Plain gradient descent steps straight downhill, and in a long, narrow valley it zigzags across the steep walls while creeping along the floor. Adam fixes this one coordinate at a time: it keeps running averages of each parameter's gradient and squared gradient, and divides one by the square root of the other, so every coordinate moves at a similar speed. With decoupled weight decay, AdamW has been the default for most of a decade.

Here's Adam in equations. It keeps a running average of the gradient, m, and of the squared gradient, v. The step is m divided by the square root of v, after a correction for the averages starting at zero. Run it on two parameters: one with gradients around four, one with gradients a hundred times smaller. The averages differ by a factor of a hundred, but their ratio doesn't: after three steps both parameters move by 0.974 learning rates. AdamW adds one more term, shrinking every weight slightly, kept separate from the gradient step.

A newer optimizer, Muon, treats each weight matrix as a whole. A matrix's gradient can be broken into directions, its singular vectors, each with a size. Here are the sizes for a real gradient from one of our pocket models, on a log scale. A handful of directions dominate: the top five, out of 128, carry about two thirds of it, while a typical direction is some fifty times smaller than the largest. Muon keeps the directions but sets every size to one: each direction gets an equal step.

Computing that exactly would need a singular value decomposition, which is slow on GPUs. Muon's trick is a polynomial. Multiplying a matrix by itself in this pattern applies the same polynomial to every singular value at once, and the coefficients are chosen so that five rounds push every value toward one. Here are the real singular values of our gradient, scaled below one, going through five iterations: small ones are lifted fast, any that overshoot are pulled back, and all of them end up between about 0.6 and 1.2.

Here is that arithmetic for three of them. Take a singular value of 0.03. One application of the polynomial is mostly the first term: 3.4445 times 0.03, minus a tiny cubic correction, gives 0.103. Apply it again: 0.35, then 1.01. A value that starts at 0.9 overshoots and oscillates instead. But after five rounds, all three sit between 0.68 and 1.12. They aren't exactly one, and they don't need to be: roughly equal steps in every direction is the whole point.

Does it help? Here's our pocket model trained twice on the same data, once with AdamW and once with Muon, each with the better of the learning rates we tried in short test runs. Muon pulls ahead early and stays ahead. While the learning rate is held at its peak, Muon reaches any given loss with less than two thirds of the tokens AdamW needs. At the end, both learning rates are lowered and both losses drop, which is the subject of the next chapter. Muon finishes at 3.61, AdamW at 3.80.

Muon began in 2024 as a trick for a community speedrun of small GPT models. In 2025, Moonshot showed it scales, needing about half the training compute of AdamW, and trained the trillion-parameter Kimi K2 with it. By 2026, Zhipu's GLM models, DeepSeek-V4 and Kimi K3 all use Muon variants. One caveat: a careful 2025 benchmark found the speed-up shrinks as models grow, from 1.4 times at a hundred million parameters to 1.1 times at 1.2 billion. Our tiny model's 1.6 fits that trend, though our tuning was far lighter. Optimizer gains are real, but they're hard to measure at scale.

## Learning-rate schedules

_Scene `s09_schedule.py::Schedule`_

The learning rate sets how big each step is, and how it changes over the run matters a lot. Runs begin with a short warmup, ramping up from near zero, because big steps on a freshly initialized network can blow it up. Then the rate decays. The classic choice is half a cosine wave, falling from the peak to a tenth of it. Cosine has a catch: you must choose the length of the run in advance. Stop halfway, and the rate was never brought down: at t equals half of T, p is 0.474, the cosine is 0.083, and the rate is still 59 percent of its peak. The alternative is warmup, stable, decay: hold the peak for most of the run, and decay only in the last fifth.

Here are both schedules on one of our pocket models, with the same data. During the stable phase, WSD's loss lags slightly behind. Then its decay begins, and the loss drops off a cliff, finishing below cosine. Larger published comparisons usually find the two about level. And the stable phase can be reused: branch off and decay early, here at forty and sixty percent of the way, and each branch is a finished model for that budget. Our half-length branch even beat a separate half-length cosine run. One long run gives you a model at every budget.

Why does the loss fall so suddenly? One picture: late in training, the loss landscape looks like a river valley, with steep walls and a slowly descending floor. At a high learning rate, the optimizer bounces between the walls while drifting downstream. Lower the rate, and it settles onto the valley floor, revealing progress it had already made.

Frontier runs use variations of both. DeepSeek-V3 held its peak learning rate for ten trillion tokens, then decayed over the next four. Llama 3 used cosine, annealed to zero over its last forty million tokens, and averaged checkpoints. Kimi K3 fitted scaling laws for both schedules and chose cosine. And the batch size usually grows as training goes, from a few million tokens per step to tens of millions.

## Fewer bits: FP8 and FP4

_Scene `s10_precision.py::Precision`_

Every number in a training run is stored in a fixed number of bits, and that choice is one of the biggest levers on speed. Use half as many bits, and a chip can do roughly twice as many multiplications per second, while moving half as much memory. An H100 does about a thousand trillion operations per second in sixteen-bit bfloat, and twice that in eight-bit. A Blackwell Ultra chip does fifteen thousand trillion in four-bit.

A floating-point number has three parts: a sign, an exponent that sets its scale, and a mantissa that sets its precision within that scale. Ordinary thirty-two-bit floats have eight exponent bits and twenty-three mantissa bits. Bfloat16 keeps all eight exponent bits but only seven of mantissa: the same range, less precision. It's the workhorse of training today. The eight-bit format called E4M3 has four exponent bits and three of mantissa, and four-bit E2M1 has just two exponent bits and one mantissa bit.

Here is every positive number each format can represent, on a log scale. FP8 has a hundred and twenty-six of them, from about two thousandths up to 448. FP4 has seven: one half, one, one and a half, two, three, four and six. That's all.

Decoding one is simple arithmetic. In E4M3, the value is two to the exponent minus seven, times one plus the mantissa over eight. Take the bits 0, 0111, 011: the exponent is seven, so two to the zero; the mantissa is three, so the value is 1.375. The largest pattern, 0, 1111, 110, is two to the eighth times 1.75: 448. FP4 works the same way with a bias of one: its largest value, 0, 11, 1, is four times 1.5, which is six.

Because the range is so small, low-precision numbers are stored with a scale factor: divide by the scale, round, and multiply back. The question is how many numbers share one scale. Here is a real tensor: the residual stream of GPT-2 after six blocks, for one sentence. Forty-five tokens by 768 channels. A typical entry is about one. But a few channels are enormous: this one reaches almost 2,900 at the first token. Such massive activations show up in many transformers.

Now store it in FP4 with a single scale for the whole tensor. The outlier sets the scale, and every ordinary value rounds to zero: 99.99 percent of the tensor simply vanishes. Give every block of sixteen numbers its own scale, as NVIDIA's NVFP4 format does, and the picture comes back. Each value is still rounded coarsely, here with about twelve percent error, but the information survives.

Zooming in on one token, the word Paris: each block of sixteen values gets its own scale, stored in eight bits, so a large value only coarsens its own block, not the whole tensor.

Here's one block, step by step: sixteen real values from the Paris token. First, one scale for the whole tensor, so its largest value fits: 2,883, over 448 times 6, is 1.072. Then the block's own scale: its largest value is 8.84; divided by 6 and by 1.072 that's 1.374, rounded to the nearest FP8 number, the 1.375 we just decoded. Divide every value by the combined scale, round to the nearest FP4 value, and multiply back. The block's largest value, 8.84, becomes exactly 6 and comes back almost perfectly; 5.11 lands at 3.47, rounds to 3, and comes back as 4.42. Over the block, the error is 9.6 percent.

FP8, with its four exponent bits, is more forgiving: on this same tensor, even a single scale gives under three percent error. Labs still scale finely, for safety. DeepSeek-V3 was trained in FP8 with a separate scale for every 128 values, adding up partial sums in full precision every 128 products, and in tests against sixteen-bit training its loss stayed within a quarter of a percent. And in 2026, NVIDIA pretrained Nemotron 3 Ultra, a 550-billion-parameter model, on twenty trillion tokens in NVFP4, keeping the most sensitive layers in higher precision and using tricks like random rotations and stochastic rounding to tame the outliers.

## Loss spikes and stability

_Scene `s11_stability.py::Stability`_

Big runs are fragile. The loss can suddenly spike, and sometimes it never recovers. Google's PaLM saw about twenty spikes during training, even with gradient clipping; the fix was to restart from a checkpoint about a hundred steps earlier and skip a few hundred batches of data. Meta's OPT needed more than thirty-five manual restarts. And labs still hit this in 2026: NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Ultra report describes two divergences, one traced to gradients being summed in sixteen-bit instead of thirty-two-bit.

Many instabilities can be reproduced in miniature. Researchers at Google showed in 2023 that small models trained at high learning rates fail in the same ways big ones do. So we trained twelve pocket models: six learning rates, each with standard attention and with a fix called QK-norm. These runs are short, so nothing blows up outright. But look at the shape. From 0.003 to 0.03, the QK-norm model's loss barely moves, while the standard model's climbs. QK-norm makes training far less sensitive to the learning rate, for a small cost at the best setting.

What's going on inside? Here is the largest attention score each model produced, the dot product of a query and a key, against the learning rate. In the standard model it explodes: about nine at the gentlest rate, over a hundred at 0.01, and ten thousand at 0.1. A softmax over scores that large is one-hot, and in longer runs, this is what Wortsman and colleagues saw right before the loss diverged. QK-norm normalizes every query and key vector before the dot product, and the largest score stays below ten at every learning rate.

Why does normalizing cap the scores? A dot product can never exceed the product of the two lengths: that's the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. RMS normalization divides each 32-dimensional query and key by its root-mean-square, which sets its length to root 32. So the score, q dot k over root d, can be at most root 32, about 5.66, unless the learned gains grow. Our gentlest run peaked at 4.8, under the bound. Even at the highest learning rate, with grown gains, it reached only 9.1, while without normalization the score hit ten thousand. And a softmax over scores like that is brutal. Two scores 45 apart get weights in the ratio e to the minus 45, about three in ten to the twenty: the output is one-hot, and its gradient, p times one minus p, is zero.

QK-norm is now standard in many open models. Alongside it: a small z-loss that keeps the output logits from drifting, gradient clipping, warmup and careful initialization. Moonshot's MuonClip rescales the query and key weights whenever an attention score passes a hundred, and Kimi K2 trained on fifteen and a half trillion tokens with zero loss spikes. And new failure modes keep appearing: in 2026, DeepSeek-V4 traced spikes to outliers in its mixture of experts, and added activation clamping and an emergency re-routing mode that switches on after a spike.

## Part 3: Memory and data parallelism

_Scene `s12_memory.py::Memory`_

Part three: scaling out.

Let's count what a training run has to keep in memory. For every parameter: the weight itself, in sixteen-bit, two bytes. Its gradient: two more. Then the optimizer's state: a full-precision master copy of the weight, four bytes, and Adam's two running averages, four bytes each. Sixteen bytes per parameter.

For a 405-billion-parameter model like Llama 3.1, that's six and a half terabytes. An H100 has eighty gigabytes; even the newest Blackwell Ultra has 288. So just holding the training state takes eighty-one H100s, before counting the activations: the intermediate values saved during the forward pass for use in the backward pass, which for long sequences can be even larger. They're often thrown away and recomputed when needed, trading compute for memory.

So the work has to be split across many GPUs, and there are several ways to split it. The simplest is data parallelism: every GPU holds a full copy of the model and processes a different slice of each batch. Each computes its own gradient, and then they must all agree on the average before taking a step together.

That averaging is called an all-reduce, and the standard algorithm passes messages around a ring. Each GPU's gradient is cut into chunks; here, four GPUs with four numbers each. Every GPU needs to end up with the sum.

In each step, every GPU sends one chunk to its neighbor, which adds it to its own. After three steps, each GPU holds one chunk of the complete sum. Three more steps pass the finished chunks around the ring, and now every GPU has the whole sum. Each one sent only about twice the size of its gradient, no matter how many GPUs are in the ring.

But in plain data parallelism, every GPU still stores all sixteen bytes per parameter. ZeRO, and PyTorch's FSDP, shard that state instead. For a 7.5-billion-parameter model on sixty-four GPUs, replicated state is sixteen bytes times 7.5 billion: 120 gigabytes on every GPU. Split the twelve bytes of optimizer state sixty-four ways: four times 7.5, plus twelve times 7.5 over sixty-four, is 31.4. Shard the gradients too: 16.6. Shard the weights themselves, gathering each layer just before it's needed: sixteen times 7.5 over sixty-four, under two gigabytes. The price is more communication: about one and a half times that of plain data parallelism.

## Tensor, pipeline, expert parallelism

_Scene `s13_parallelism.py::Parallelism`_

Data parallelism still runs every layer in full on every GPU. When a single layer is too big, you split the layer itself. That's tensor parallelism. Take a matrix multiplication. Cut the weight matrix into columns, and each GPU computes its own slice of the output, with no communication at all. Feed those slices into the next matrix, cut into rows, and each GPU produces a partial sum. One all-reduce adds them up.

NVIDIA's Megatron-LM arranged a whole transformer layer this way, with just two all-reduces in the forward pass and two in the backward. But they happen in every layer, on the critical path, so tensor parallelism is kept inside one server, where eight GPUs are joined by NVLink at 450 gigabytes per second in each direction. Between servers, a network card manages about 50: nine times slower.

To spread a model across servers, cut it by depth instead: pipeline parallelism. The first GPU holds the first quarter of the layers, the next GPU the next quarter, and so on. Only activations pass between stages, so slower links are fine. But a naive pipeline is mostly idle. While one stage works, the others wait: three quarters of the time is bubble.

The fix is to cut each batch into micro-batches and keep the pipeline full. With eight micro-batches, the bubble shrinks to p minus one, over m plus p minus one: about a quarter. Cleverer schedules push it further. Zero-bubble pipelines split the backward pass in two and use one half to fill the gaps, and DeepSeek's DualPipe feeds micro-batches in from both ends at once, hiding communication behind computation.

Mixture-of-experts models add expert parallelism. Each GPU holds different experts, and every token must travel to the GPUs holding its chosen experts, and back again: an all-to-all exchange in every MoE layer. And for very long sequences, context parallelism splits the sequence itself across GPUs.

Real runs combine all of these. Llama 3.1, the 405-billion-parameter model, trained on 16,384 H100s arranged as a grid: tensor parallel across the eight GPUs inside each server, a sixteen-stage pipeline across sixteen servers, and a hundred and twenty-eight copies of that pipeline doing data parallelism. Each GPU sustained about 400 teraflops: roughly forty percent of its peak, which is typical. DeepSeek-V3 took a different shape, on 2,048 export-limited H800s: no tensor parallelism at all, a sixteen-stage DualPipe pipeline, and experts spread across 64 GPUs on eight nodes.

Now the arithmetic of a real run. An H100 peaks at 989 trillion operations per second; Llama 3.1's GPUs sustained about 400 trillion, forty percent, a figure called model FLOPs utilization. So training takes six N D divided by the cluster's speed: 3.8 times ten to the twenty-five, over sixteen thousand GPUs times 400 trillion. That's 5.8 million seconds: sixty-seven days, or 26 million GPU-hours. Meta reports 30.8 million, so this one line gets within fifteen percent. One step, sixteen million tokens, takes about six seconds. In that time each GPU must average its gradients with its 127 copies. It holds 3.2 billion parameters, 6.3 gigabytes of gradients; a ring sends about twice that, and at fifty gigabytes per second it takes a quarter of a second, hidden behind the backward pass.

## Keeping the run alive

_Scene `s14_operations.py::Operations`_

A run like this lasts weeks or months, and keeping it alive is a job in itself, because the more GPUs you use, the more often something breaks. Meta measured this across its research clusters: an eight-GPU job ran, on average, forty-eight days between failures. A thousand-GPU job: about eight hours. Failures scale with the number of parts, so at sixteen thousand GPUs you'd expect one every couple of hours, and at a hundred thousand, about every quarter of an hour.

And that's what Llama 3 saw. In one fifty-four-day stretch of training on 16,384 GPUs, the job was interrupted 466 times: once every two point eight hours. About four fifths of the unexpected stops traced to hardware, mostly the GPUs themselves and their memory.

So the training state is checkpointed regularly. When a GPU dies, the job restarts from the last checkpoint on healthy machines, and only the work since then is lost. Doing this well is the difference between a run that spends nine tenths of its time training and one that doesn't. Llama 3 stayed above ninety percent. Google kept redundant copies of Gemini's state in memory, so recovery took seconds, and raised the share of useful time from eighty-five to ninety-seven percent.

How often should you save? Saving takes time, delta, every tau minutes. But with a failure every M minutes, each failure throws away, on average, half an interval of work. Add the two, set the derivative to zero, and the best interval is the square root of two delta M. With Llama 3's failure every 2.8 hours and a checkpoint that takes one minute, save every eighteen minutes and lose eleven percent, split evenly between the two costs. Make checkpoints take ten seconds, from memory, and the best interval drops to seven and a half minutes, losing four and a half percent: one reason Google kept copies of Gemini's state in memory.

Some failures are silent: a chip that keeps running but computes slightly wrong numbers. At Gemini's scale, Google expected such silent data corruption every week or two, and caught it by replaying steps. Even the power grid notices: when sixteen thousand GPUs pause at the same moment, Meta saw the power draw swing by tens of megawatts.

## Part 4: Supervised fine-tuning

_Scene `s15_sft.py::SFT`_

Part four: post-training.

After pretraining, we have a base model: a superb document completer, but not an assistant. Ask the base version of Qwen3's smallest model where the Eiffel Tower is, and it decides it's reading a quiz. It writes the multiple-choice options, the answer key, and then the next question. The same model after post-training simply answers.

The first step is supervised fine-tuning. Conversations are written out as a single stream of tokens, with special tokens marking who is speaking. Here is Qwen3's actual format: a start marker, the speaker's role, the message, and an end marker. The assistant's turn even begins with a thinking block, empty here because thinking was switched off.

Training is the same next-token prediction as before, with one change: the loss is only counted on the assistant's tokens. The model isn't learning to predict what users say. It's learning how the assistant replies.

Here is that loss on real numbers. For each of the assistant's tokens, take minus the log of the probability the model gave it. Qwen3's base model, in grey, finds most of the answer easy, but pays heavily for the first word, at 7.3, for 'in', at 4.5, and for the end-of-turn marker it has never seen, at 12.9. Averaged over these twelve tokens, its loss is 2.12. The same model after post-training, in green, averages 0.52. Its biggest cost is 'in', at 3.6, because it would have said 'located in'. Fine-tuning is exactly the pressure that pushes these numbers down.

Where do the conversations come from? Early on, from people: OpenAI's InstructGPT used about thirteen thousand demonstrations written by contractors. The LIMA paper showed that a thousand carefully chosen examples go a long way, because the knowledge is already there from pretraining. Today most of the data is generated by stronger models and then filtered: Allen AI's Tülu 3 mix has 939 thousand prompts, and OLMo 3's reasoning set has 2.3 million, about forty-five billion tokens. This kind of imitation is powerful. DeepSeek distilled R1's reasoning into a 32-billion-parameter Qwen model with fine-tuning alone, and it scored 72.6 percent on the AIME 2024 math competition, far above the 47 percent that reinforcement learning reached directly on the same base.

## Learning from preferences

_Scene `s16_preferences.py::Preferences`_

Imitation only goes so far. For many qualities, it's far easier to judge an answer than to write a perfect one. So the next stage learns from comparisons: show a person two responses to the same prompt, and ask which one is better.

Those choices train a reward model: a copy of the language model whose output is a single number, a score. It's trained so that the probability a person prefers answer A over answer B is the sigmoid of the difference between their scores. That's the Bradley-Terry model, from 1952.

Here's one comparison with numbers. Say the reward model scores answer A at 1.3 and answer B at 0.4. Then it predicts that a person prefers A with probability sigma of 0.9: 71 percent. The loss is minus the log of that probability, 0.34. Its slope with respect to A's score is minus 0.29, and with respect to B's, plus 0.29: each step raises A's score and lowers B's by the same amount. Once a pair is ranked confidently, say with a gap of 3, the loss and the push both shrink to 0.05: the training effort goes to the comparisons the model still gets wrong. And only the gap matters: add ten to every score, and nothing changes.

Then comes reinforcement learning. The model writes answers, the reward model scores them, and the model is nudged toward the answers that score higher, with one constraint: a penalty for drifting too far from the fine-tuned model it started from, measured by the KL divergence. This is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback: the recipe behind InstructGPT and the first ChatGPT.

The leash matters, because the reward model is only a proxy for what people want. Here's a simulation. Each candidate answer has a true quality, and the reward model sees that quality plus some error. Pick the best of n answers by the reward model's score. At first, true quality climbs. But push harder, and you start selecting the answers whose errors were biggest: the proxy reward keeps rising while the true quality falls. That's Goodhart's law, and OpenAI measured exactly this shape with real reward models in 2022.

In 2023, Direct Preference Optimization showed you can skip the reward model and the RL loop altogether. A bit of algebra turns the same objective into a simple loss on each pair: raise the probability of the preferred answer, relative to the reference model, and lower the rejected one. DPO and its variants are now standard in open post-training pipelines. And the comparisons don't have to come from people: with constitutional AI, a model judges responses against a written list of principles.

Here's DPO on one pair. Each log-probability is a sum over the answer's tokens: the same per-token numbers as in fine-tuning. Compared with the reference model, the policy has raised the log-probability of the preferred answer by 2, and lowered the rejected one by 1. Times beta, 0.1, those are implicit rewards: 0.2, and minus 0.1. Their gap, 0.3, goes into the same Bradley-Terry loss as the reward model: minus log sigma of 0.3, which is 0.554. Every pair starts at log 2, 0.693, because at step zero the policy is the reference. And notice: the rejected answer is still the more likely one, in absolute terms. DPO doesn't care. Only the ratios to the reference count.

## Reinforcement learning and reasoning

_Scene `s17_rl.py::RL`_

The biggest change in post-training since 2024 is reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked automatically. A math problem has a final answer; code has tests. The reward is simply one if the answer is right, and zero if it isn't. There's no learned reward model for the policy to fool.

Here's the most popular algorithm for this, GRPO, run for real on a miniature. We pretrained a pocket model on three-digit additions, where four in ten of the examples were written by someone who always forgets to carry. So the model learned two habits: the right answer, and the carry-dropping mistake.

Give it a problem, 478 plus 356, and sample a group of eight answers. Only one is right: 834. Five drop both carries, 724, and two drop one of them. Each answer gets a reward: one or zero. GRPO's key idea is to grade each answer against its own group: subtract the group's average reward, and divide by the spread. Better than average gets a positive advantage; worse gets a negative one. Then every token of each answer is pushed up or down in probability according to its advantage. No critic network, no reward model: just a group of samples and a checker.

Here are those numbers, worked out. The rewards are a single one and seven zeros, so the mean is one eighth: 0.125. The spread, the sample standard deviation, is the square root of an eighth: 0.354. So the right answer gets 0.875 divided by 0.354: plus 2.47. Each wrong one gets minus 0.35. The advantages always sum to zero: probability pushed up in one place is pushed down somewhere else. And their size depends on how rare the outcome was. One success in eight earns plus 2.47; one failure in eight costs minus 2.47. And when all eight agree, the spread is zero, and so is every advantage.

GRPO's full objective adds one safeguard, borrowed from PPO. Rho is the ratio of a token's probability now to its probability when the answers were sampled, and it's clipped to within twenty percent of one. Take a token of the right answer, advantage plus 2.47, whose probability has already risen by thirty percent: rho is 1.3. Unclipped, the objective would be 3.21; clipped at 1.2 it's 2.96, and the minimum takes the clipped value. That value no longer depends on the weights, so its gradient is zero: this token has been pushed enough for this batch. Likewise a wrong answer's token already down to 0.6 of its old probability: the minimum is the clipped minus 0.28, and the push stops. In our toy, each batch is used for one step only, so rho is exactly one, and the gradient is simply the advantage times the gradient of the log-probability.

Repeat that over thousands of problems. Pass at one, the chance that a single sample is right, climbs from about sixty percent to nearly a hundred. But look at pass at eight, the chance that at least one of eight samples is right. It barely moves: it was already near the top. In this toy, reinforcement learning didn't teach the model anything new. It made it reliable at what it could already sometimes do. Whether that also holds at the frontier is debated: a 2025 study found the same pattern in large models, while others find that prolonged RL discovers genuinely new strategies.

And notice what happens late in training: most groups are all correct, like our 478 plus 356, which now gets eight out of eight. Every advantage is zero, and those groups teach nothing. Refinements like DAPO's dynamic sampling filter them out, alongside other tweaks to the clipping and to how the loss is averaged.

Back to pass at eight: why did it barely move? If each sample is right with probability p, all k samples fail with probability one minus p, to the k. At the start, the average sample was right 61 percent of the time. If every problem were average, pass at eight would be one minus 0.39 to the eighth: 99.95 percent. The measured 98.8 is a little lower, because failures cluster on the hard problems, like 478 plus 356, where one sample in eight was right. There, pass at eight is one minus seven eighths to the eighth: 66 percent. RL raised p on exactly those problems, and pass at one followed. Pass at eight was already near its ceiling.

At frontier scale, this is how reasoning models are made. DeepSeek-R1-Zero applied it directly to a base model, with just two rewards: is the answer correct, and is the format right? Its score on the AIME 2024 math competition rose from 15.6 percent to 71. Its answers grew from hundreds of tokens to thousands, as it learned on its own to re-check its work. In one transcript it stops and writes: wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here.

Today's frontier RL goes further: long, multi-step tasks with tools, in sandboxes. Fixing real software issues, driving a terminal, browsing the web. DeepSeek-V3.2 synthesized more than eighteen hundred environments, and spent over ten percent of its pretraining compute on post-training. Generating those long rollouts dominates the cost: in OLMo 3's runs, the learner spent three quarters of its time waiting for data. So generation and training run on separate GPUs, asynchronously, with fresh weights streamed to the generators as training goes.

And reinforcement learning finds loopholes. If a test can be gamed, a model will eventually game it, like a prime checker that simply memorizes the test cases. Anthropic reported in 2025 that models which learned to cheat in real coding environments generalized to broader bad behavior. Building environments that can't be gamed is now a central part of the job.

## The whole pipeline

_Scene `s18_outro.py::Outro`_

Before release, the model is evaluated: on hard benchmarks in science, math and coding, on long agentic tasks, and on safety tests, all written up in a system card. Benchmarks are a moving target: they saturate, and they leak into training data. In February 2026, OpenAI stopped reporting the popular SWE-bench Verified because of contamination.

So here is the whole pipeline. Billions of crawled pages, filtered and deduplicated down to tens of trillions of tokens. A recipe chosen with scaling laws, today usually a sparse mixture of experts, trained with Muon or AdamW in eight- or even four-bit arithmetic. Tens of thousands of GPUs, coordinated by four kinds of parallelism, and kept alive through failures every few hours. Then fine-tuning, preferences and reinforcement learning, which now takes a substantial share of the compute. And finally, the assistant.

And our pocket versions? The largest model we trained had about three million parameters, against up to 2.8 trillion at the frontier. All of our training runs together took less than ten to the fifteen operations; the largest frontier run is estimated at around ten to the twenty-seven. Four CPU cores, against a hundred thousand GPUs. Yet the curves have the same shapes.

Almost everything in this video comes from open-weight labs and open-source projects; the leading closed labs publish very little about how their models are trained. But the shape of the pipeline has been stable for years: get the data right, choose the size with scaling laws, keep the run stable across thousands of machines, and then teach the model with feedback. And more of it is open than ever. AI2's OLMo 3 releases every dataset and checkpoint; nanochat trains a GPT-2-grade chat model for under a hundred dollars; Hugging Face's playbooks write down the hard-won lessons. If you want to know how these models are made, you can run the whole thing yourself, at whatever scale you have.
