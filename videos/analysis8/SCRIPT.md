## A continuous function whose Fourier series diverges

_Scene `s01_hook.py::Hook`_

Seven rows of the ledger are filled. Each part found a complete space and asked what compactness costs there. Part 8 asks those questions about every complete normed space at once.

The n-th partial sum of a Fourier series at zero is the integral of f against the Dirichlet kernel. The kernel has a tall peak and many side lobes, some negative. A function bounded by one can line up its sign with the kernel's, and then its partial sum at zero is the total area under the absolute value of the kernel. Continuous functions can come within three percent of that. And that area grows without bound: like four over pi squared times the log of n, computed here out to a million. So for each n there's a bounded continuous function whose n-th partial sum at zero is large. Does a single continuous function have partial sums that grow without bound?

Du Bois-Reymond answered yes in 1873, with an explicit and delicate construction. Banach and Steinhaus answered it in three lines. The functionals that take f to its n-th partial sum at zero have norms that go to infinity. Their theorem then says the partial sums are unbounded, not just for one f, but for most continuous functions, in a precise sense due to Baire. A theorem about the existence of monsters, proved by counting. That's the spirit of this part.

Here's the plan, following chapter five of Real and Complex Analysis and the first five chapters of Rudin's Functional Analysis. First the spaces, and Baire's theorem with its three children: uniform boundedness, open mappings and closed graphs. Then Hahn-Banach and duality, weak convergence, and the compactness that comes back in the weak-star topology. Then extreme points, compact operators, and four applications.

## 1. Banach spaces and bounded operators

_Scene `s02_banach.py::Banach`_

Chapter one: complete normed spaces, named after Stefan Banach, and the maps between them.

A Banach space is a vector space with a norm in which every Cauchy sequence converges. We've met many. The sequence spaces, with the sum, square-sum and supremum norms, whose unit balls we know as the diamond, the circle and the square. The continuous functions with the sup norm, from Part 3; the sequences that tend to zero; and the L p spaces of Part 6. Every theorem in this part holds for all of them at once.

A linear map between normed spaces is continuous exactly when it's bounded: it stretches no vector by more than a fixed factor. And if it's continuous at one point, it's continuous everywhere, because linearity moves that point anywhere. The smallest such factor is the operator norm: the radius of the smallest ball containing the image of the unit ball. With this norm, the bounded operators themselves form a Banach space.

An operator from analysis: integrate from zero to x. On L two of the unit interval, how much can it stretch a function? The answer is two over pi. The function stretched most is a quarter of a cosine wave: integrating it gives a quarter sine wave, two over pi times as long. A discretization with two thousand points agrees to six digits. The other singular values follow the same pattern, two over three pi, two over five pi, falling to zero. That makes this operator compact, which chapter eleven explains.

## 2. Spaces without norms

_Scene `s03_tvs.py::TVS`_

Chapter two: why Rudin's Functional Analysis begins with spaces that have no norm at all.

Some important spaces have no single norm. Take the smooth functions on an interval. These two are within 0.08 of each other, but their derivatives differ by two, and their second derivatives by fifty. For smooth functions, close should mean close in every derivative. So the topology uses a whole sequence of seminorms, the sups of the k-th derivatives. Countably many seminorms still give a metric, and the space is complete: a Fréchet space. But no single norm does the job, and the test functions of Part 9 are worse still. That's why Grandpa Rudin starts with topological vector spaces.

Part 1 promised that closed and bounded doesn't mean compact beyond R n. Here's the honest end of that story. The unit vectors of l two slide off to the right, and every two of them are root two apart. A ball of radius 0.7 holds at most one of them, so no finite collection of such balls covers the unit ball. Riesz proved more: a topological vector space in which some neighborhood of zero is compact must be finite-dimensional. In infinite dimensions, compactness has to come from somewhere else.

One more warning. For p below one, L p is still a complete metric vector space, but its balls cave in, like this star. And it has no continuous linear functionals at all, except zero: the convex hull of any ball is the whole space. Hahn-Banach can't work there. So the theory keeps one assumption: local convexity, a base of convex neighborhoods of zero.

## 3. Baire's theorem

_Scene `s04_baire.py::Baire`_

Chapter three: Baire's category theorem, the root of three of the four pillars.

List the rationals between zero and one: q one, q two, and so on. Can the list be all the real numbers? Pick a closed third of the interval that misses q one. Inside it, a closed third that misses q two. And so on: the intervals shrink by a factor of three each time, each one dodging one more rational. Because the real line is complete, the nested intervals close in on a real number, and it's none of the q n. That's Baire's theorem in miniature: a complete metric space can't be a countable union of nowhere dense sets. The proof is always this: nested closed balls, each avoiding one more thin set, and completeness to finish.

Baire's theorem gives a notion of size. A set is meager if it's a countable union of nowhere dense sets, and residual if its complement is meager. In a complete space, residual sets are dense: in Baire's sense, they're almost everything. It's a different notion from measure. Put a tiny open interval around each rational, of total length epsilon. The union is open and dense, but its length is at most epsilon. Intersect these sets as epsilon goes to zero. The result is a dense G delta, residual, almost everything to Baire, and it has measure zero. Two meanings of most, and they disagree.

## 4. Uniform boundedness

_Scene `s05_ubp.py::UniformBoundedness`_

Chapter four: the uniform boundedness principle of Banach and Steinhaus.

Banach and Steinhaus: for a family of bounded operators on a Banach space, either their norms are bounded, or they blow up on a dense G delta. The proof is Baire. Let E k be the set of points whose orbit stays within k: a closed set. If every orbit is bounded, the E k cover the whole space, and Baire says one of them must contain a ball. A ball's worth of uniform bounds, shifted to the origin by linearity, bounds every operator's norm by the same constant. Pointwise bounds become uniform bounds.

Now the hook. On the continuous functions on the circle, the functional that takes f to its n-th partial sum at zero has norm equal to the integral of the absolute Dirichlet kernel. A continuous function shaped like the sign of the kernel nearly achieves it. Those norms grow like the log of n. So the family isn't uniformly bounded, and Banach and Steinhaus say the partial sums at zero are unbounded for a dense G delta of continuous functions. Most continuous functions have Fourier series that diverge at zero. For square-integrable functions, Carleson proved in 1966 that the series converge almost everywhere; for integrable ones, Kolmogorov had found a series diverging everywhere.

The same kernel answers another question. The Fourier coefficients of an integrable function tend to zero, by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, so the Fourier transform maps L one into the sequences converging to zero. Is every such sequence a Fourier series? If so, the open mapping theorem, coming next, would bound the L one norm by the largest coefficient. The Dirichlet kernels break that bound: their coefficients are all one, and their L one norms grow without limit. So the map isn't onto, and nobody had to find the missing sequence.

## 5. Open mappings and closed graphs

_Scene `s06_openmap.py::OpenMapping`_

Chapter five: Baire's second and third children, the open mapping and closed graph theorems.

The open mapping theorem: a bounded linear map of one Banach space onto another sends the unit ball to a set that contains a ball. In the plane, the image of the disk is an ellipse, and the largest disk inside it has radius one over the norm of the inverse. In infinite dimensions that's a theorem. Baire says some multiple of the image is dense in a ball; completeness, through an iteration, removes the word dense. Checked on sixty coordinates of l two: fifty random targets inside the predicted ball are all hit from inside the unit ball. The consequence: a bounded bijection between Banach spaces has a bounded inverse, for free.

The closed graph theorem: a linear map between Banach spaces whose graph is closed is automatically bounded. It's the open mapping theorem applied to the graph. A striking corollary, due to Hellinger and Toeplitz: a symmetric operator defined on all of a Hilbert space is bounded. Differentiation is symmetric on periodic functions, but sine n x over root pi has norm one while its derivative has norm n. So differentiation can't be defined on all of L two. It must live on a smaller domain, and that's why Part 10 needs unbounded operators.

## 6. Hahn-Banach

_Scene `s07_hahnbanach.py::HahnBanach`_

Chapter six: the Hahn-Banach theorem, the fourth pillar, and the only one not built on Baire.

Hahn-Banach extends a linear functional from a subspace to the whole space without breaking a bound. Here's the key step, in the plane: a functional on the x-axis, bounded by the length, extended to one more direction. The new value c must sit above every blue lower bound and below every pink upper bound. And it can: the highest blue point is below the lowest pink point, by the triangle inequality. Any c in the yellow band is a valid extension. That handles one dimension. Infinitely many need Hausdorff's maximality principle: pure logic, which no picture can show.

The geometric form: two disjoint convex sets, one compact and one closed, can be separated by a closed hyperplane. For two convex polygons, the hyperplane through the midpoint of their closest points, perpendicular to the gap, does it: the functional is below the yellow level on one set and above it on the other. In general the proof feeds the sandwich a special sublinear function: the gauge of a convex set, how far you must scale the set to reach a point.

The consequences are what make duality work. Every nonzero vector has a functional of norm one that measures its full length. So the norm of x is the supremum of the functionals over the dual unit ball, and functionals separate points. And a vector is outside a closed subspace exactly when some functional kills the subspace but not the vector. A normed space always has enough functionals to see everything, unlike L p for p below one.

## 7. Dual spaces

_Scene `s08_dual.py::Dual`_

Chapter seven: dual spaces, the spaces of functionals.

A functional on a normed space has its own norm: its largest value on the unit ball. So the functionals form a normed space too, the dual, with a unit ball of its own. For the one-norm, whose ball is the diamond, the dual ball is the square: the largest value of g dot x on the diamond is the largest coordinate of g. The circle is its own dual. And in general the dual of the p-norm is the q-norm, with conjugate exponents, checked here for sixty random functionals.

The duals we know. For p between one and infinity, the dual of l p is l q, and the dual of that is l p again: these spaces are reflexive. The sequences tending to zero have dual l one, and l one has dual l infinity, which is strictly bigger, so l one isn't reflexive. The continuous functions have the measures as their dual, as Part 6 proved. And l infinity has functionals that no sequence describes, like a limit for every bounded sequence, which only Hahn-Banach can produce. That's the promise from Part 6 kept.

## 8. Weak convergence

_Scene `s09_weak.py::Weak`_

Chapter eight: weak convergence, convergence as seen by every functional.

The unit vectors of l two never get close to anything: each is root two from every other, and one from zero. But look at them through a fixed functional, the inner product with a vector y. That reading is just the n-th coordinate of y, which goes to zero. For fifty random y's, the largest reading falls steadily. So e n converges to zero weakly: every functional sees the sequence converge, even though its norm never shrinks.

In L two there are three ways to converge weakly without converging. Oscillate: sine n x wiggles faster and faster, and against any fixed function its integral fades, by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. Concentrate: a tall thin spike of norm one sees less and less of any fixed function. Escape: a bump sliding off to infinity. All have norm one, all converge weakly to zero. These are the bump's evil twins from Part 3, back as the standard examples.

Weak limits aren't far from norm limits, though. Average the first N sine waves. The averages really do shrink, in norm, like the square root of pi over N. Mazur's theorem says this always happens: a weak limit is a norm limit of convex combinations. So closed convex sets are also weakly closed. And by Banach and Steinhaus, a weakly convergent sequence is always bounded.

## 9. Banach-Alaoglu

_Scene `s10_alaoglu.py::Alaoglu`_

Chapter nine: the compactness that infinite dimensions take away comes back, in the weak-star topology.

Banach-Alaoglu: the closed unit ball of a dual space is compact in the weak-star topology, where functionals converge when their values at each point converge. For the dual of l one, a functional of norm at most one is just a choice of a number between minus one and one for each coordinate: a point of an infinite cube. In general, each functional is a point in a product of intervals, one interval for each vector. Tychonoff's theorem says the product is compact, and linearity is a closed condition. For separable spaces there's a sequential version, proved by Cantor's diagonal argument, which is how it's used most often.

Here's the payoff for measures. Probability measures on the unit interval are functionals on the continuous functions, all in the dual unit ball. This sequence keeps changing shape: its mean wanders forever and never settles. But Alaoglu promises a weak-star convergent subsequence. Take n equal to 6, 44, 710, 103993, nearly multiples of two pi. Along it, the histograms settle onto a limit measure, and every moment converges: the error drops to four ten-millionths. This is how analysts produce limits of measures, from Haar measure to Prokhorov's theorem in probability.

## 10. Extreme points

_Scene `s11_kreinmilman.py::KreinMilman`_

Chapter ten: extreme points, and the Krein-Milman theorem.

A point of a convex set is extreme if it isn't the midpoint of two other points of the set. A point on an edge of the diamond splits along the edge, so only the four corners are extreme. On the circle, every boundary point is extreme. On the square, only the corners again. The same test in three dimensions, on random boundary points, gives none, all, and none. Krein and Milman: a compact convex set is the closed convex hull of its extreme points. The corners are enough to rebuild the whole set.

Now a surprise. In L one of the unit interval, take f equals two x, of norm one. Cut the interval where half the mass lies, at one over root two. Doubling f on each side gives two functions of norm one, and f is their average. Every unit vector splits like this, so the unit ball of L one has no extreme points at all. But a dual ball is weak-star compact by Alaoglu, so it has plenty of extreme points by Krein and Milman. Conclusion: L one is not the dual of any Banach space. Two compactness theorems, combined into a non-existence proof.

## 11. Compact operators

_Scene `s12_compact.py::Compact`_

Chapter eleven: compact operators, the ones for which linear algebra survives.

An operator is compact if it sends the unit ball into a set with compact closure. The Volterra operator from chapter one is an example: it maps the unit ball onto an infinite-dimensional ellipsoid whose axes are two over pi, two over three pi, two over five pi, falling to zero. Thin in every direction but finitely many: so in Hilbert space a compact operator is a limit of finite-rank ones, matrices in disguise.

The model compact operator is an integral operator with a continuous kernel. This one solves the equation of a string held at both ends: given a load f, it returns the displacement. Discretize it with two thousand points and diagonalize. The eigenvectors are the string's modes, sine n pi x, and the eigenvalues are one over n squared pi squared, to three digits, falling to zero. A compact operator's eigenvalues can only pile up at zero.

For compact operators, almost all of finite-dimensional linear algebra survives. The nonzero spectrum consists of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, accumulating only at zero. And Fredholm's alternative holds: T minus lambda is one-to-one exactly when it's onto, as for square matrices. The symmetric ones even have orthonormal eigenbases; that's the spectral theorem, which Part 10 proves in full.

## 12. Four applications

_Scene `s13_samples.py::Samples`_

Chapter twelve: four applications, one from each corner of the theory.

First, a limit for every bounded sequence. Hahn-Banach extends the ordinary limit to all of l infinity, keeping it linear, positive and unchanged by shifting the sequence. Shift invariance alone forces some values. Minus one to the n plus its shift is zero, so its limit is zero. The pattern one, zero, zero, plus two shifts, is the constant one, so its limit is a third: the average. For most sequences nothing forces the value, and no formula gives it. This is the functional on l infinity that's not in l one, the one Part 6 promised.

Second, a model use of the closed graph theorem, due to Grothendieck. A closed subspace of L p made entirely of bounded functions must be finite-dimensional. The closed graph theorem gives one constant C with the sup norm at most C times the L two norm, throughout the subspace. But n orthonormal functions force the constant to be at least root n. For trigonometric polynomials the ratio is exactly the square root of the dimension, so no single constant survives infinitely many dimensions.

Third, compactness builds a measure. Every compact group has exactly one probability measure unchanged by the group's motions: Haar measure. The proof finds it as a fixed point among all probability measures, which are weak-star compact by Alaoglu. For the rotations of space, it can be sampled with random unit quaternions. Apply fifteen hundred of them to the north pole, and the points spread evenly over the sphere. A test integral gives a third, and shifting the measure by a fixed rotation gives a third again.

Last, a warning about projections. Number the nodes of an infinite binary tree; each infinite branch is then a set of whole numbers. Two different branches part ways after finitely many steps, so they share only finitely many numbers, and there are uncountably many branches. A counting argument with this family shows that the sequences tending to zero have no bounded projection inside l infinity. In Hilbert space, every closed subspace has an orthogonal projection; in general Banach spaces, that's a luxury.

## The eighth row of the ledger

_Scene `s14_outro.py::Outro`_

Let's take stock. Banach spaces gathered the complete spaces of earlier parts into one family, where continuity means boundedness, and where the unit ball is never compact. Baire's theorem gave uniform boundedness, which proved that most continuous functions have divergent Fourier series; and it gave the open mapping and closed graph theorems. Hahn-Banach supplied enough functionals, and duality identified them. Weak convergence and the Banach-Alaoglu theorem brought compactness back, Krein-Milman found the corners, and compact operators kept linear algebra alive.

The ledger's eighth row. The spaces: Banach and Fréchet. Completeness pays through Baire's theorem. Compactness returns in the weak-star topology. The new interchange: pointwise bounds become uniform bounds. And the Fourier answer is now sharp: divergence for most continuous functions, and, by Carleson's theorem, convergence almost everywhere for square-integrable ones.

Next time, in Part 9: distributions. The absolute value has a corner, but its derivative is the sign function. The sign function jumps, and its derivative is a spike of total mass two, the Dirac delta, which no function can be. Distributions make that honest: define the derivative by moving it onto smooth test functions. Then everything can be differentiated, and the Fourier transform becomes a symmetry of the whole theory.
