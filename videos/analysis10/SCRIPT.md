## Two meanings of spectrum

_Scene `s01_hook.py::Hook`_

Nine rows of the ledger are filled. The last part is about operators, and what it means to diagonalize one.

Spectrum is the word for the lines of light a hot gas gives off. Hydrogen's visible lines sit at these wavelengths, computed from Rydberg's formula. And spectrum is the word for the set of complex numbers lambda where T minus lambda has no inverse. It's the same word for a reason: the energy levels of the atom are the spectrum of an operator, the Hamiltonian, and each line is a jump between two levels. The quantum videos assumed that such operators can be diagonalized. This part proves it.

Here's the first surprise. The shift on square-summable sequences moves every entry one slot to the right. It has no eigenvalues: S x equal to lambda x forces the first entry to vanish, then the next, then all of them. Its adjoint, the backward shift, drops the first entry, and for every lambda inside the unit disc the geometric sequence one, lambda, lambda squared is an eigenvector. Two hundred of them, checked. So the spectrum of the shift is the whole closed disc, though it has not a single eigenvalue.

A computer can only hold a finite piece of the shift: an N by N block whose only eigenvalue is zero. But measure how close T minus lambda is to failing to be invertible: the glow is the size of the inverse. Inside the disc it's astronomically large, like one over lambda to the N, and the contours spread outward as N grows. The eigenvalues lie, but the resolvent tells the truth. That's why the spectrum is defined by invertibility, not by eigenvectors.

## 2. The spectrum

_Scene `s03_spectrum.py::Spectrum`_

Chapter two: the spectrum, which is never empty, and how big it is.

For a six by six matrix, here's the trace of the resolvent, lambda minus A inverse, colored by phase. It's holomorphic except at the eigenvalues, where it has poles. In any Banach algebra the same is true. Outside the circle of radius the norm of x, the Neumann series inverts x minus lambda, and the invertibles are open, so the spectrum is closed and bounded. And it's never empty: if it were, every functional of the resolvent would be an entire function going to zero at infinity, so zero, by Liouville's theorem, and then Hahn-Banach makes the resolvent itself zero, which is absurd. A corollary: a Banach algebra where every nonzero element is invertible is just the complex numbers.

Expand the resolvent in powers of one over lambda. That's a Laurent series, Part 7's tool, and it converges exactly outside the smallest circle that contains the spectrum. By the root test from Part 2, its radius is the limit of the n-th root of the norm of x to the n. That's Gelfand's spectral radius formula: a purely algebraic, metric quantity equals the size of the spectrum. For ten random matrices, the n-th roots start well above the largest eigenvalue, and settle onto it, within a tenth of a percent by n equal to a thousand.

Part 8's Volterra operator integrates from zero. Its norm is two over pi. Its powers are repeated integrals, with kernel x minus t to the n minus one over n minus one factorial, and their norms collapse like one over n factorial: n factorial times the norm tends to one half. So the n-th roots go to zero, and by Gelfand's formula the spectrum is the single point zero. Yet zero isn't an eigenvalue, because if V f vanishes, so does its derivative, which is f. A nonzero operator, a one-point spectrum, and no eigenvector at all.

## 3. Functions of an operator

_Scene `s04_calculus.py::SymbolicCalculus`_

Chapter three: applying functions to operators, with Cauchy's formula.

How do you take the exponential of a matrix, or of any element of a Banach algebra? Part 7 already answered it. Cauchy's formula recovers a holomorphic function from its values on a circle, through the kernel one over lambda minus z. Put the operator in place of z: the kernel becomes the resolvent. Integrate around a contour enclosing the spectrum, and you get f of A. For a random matrix, with the exponential and a hundred twenty-eight points on the circle, the result matches the exponential computed the usual way, to rounding error.

And the spectrum goes along. The eigenvalues of A, on the left, are carried by the exponential to the eigenvalues of e to the A, on the right, while the grid around them is bent by the same map. That's the spectral mapping theorem: the spectrum of f of A is f of the spectrum of A, for every holomorphic f, in every Banach algebra.

Square roots need care, because the square root isn't holomorphic across its branch cut. Shift the matrix so its spectrum sits in the right half-plane, and take a contour that encloses the spectrum without touching the cut. The same formula, with the square root in it, gives a matrix whose square is B, to rounding error. Logarithms and fractional powers work the same way, and Runge's theorem from Part 7 explains why rational functions are enough. One famous consequence of this circle of ideas, Lomonosov's theorem, finds invariant subspaces with a fixed-point argument.

## 4. Gelfand theory

_Scene `s05_gelfand.py::Gelfand`_

Chapter four: Gelfand's theory, which turns every commutative Banach algebra into functions.

In a commutative Banach algebra, the maximal ideals are exactly the kernels of the homomorphisms to the complex numbers. Gelfand's idea: evaluate x at every homomorphism. That turns x into a continuous function on the space of homomorphisms, which is compact, by Alaoglu's theorem. In summable sequences under convolution, the homomorphisms are indexed by an angle theta: sum a n times e to the i n theta. So the Gelfand transform of a sequence is its Fourier series, a closed curve in the plane. And the spectrum of x is the range of its transform: here, the curve itself.

Here's what that buys. Our curve never passes through zero. Wiener asked: does one over it also have absolutely summable Fourier coefficients? Computed by fast Fourier transform: the coefficients of one over f decay like one over n squared, and their absolute sums settle at 2.65427. Gelfand's proof is three lines. The transform never vanishes, so the sequence lies in no maximal ideal. So it's invertible in the algebra, and the inverse transforms to one over f. Wiener's original proof took pages.

Add an adjoint, with the norm of x star x equal to the norm of x squared, the identity that holds for operators on Hilbert space. Then Gelfand-Naimark says: a commutative such algebra is exactly an algebra of continuous functions, with nothing lost. The dictionary: a normal element becomes the coordinate z on its spectrum, its adjoint becomes z bar, and any continuous f of it becomes f composed with z. For a normal matrix this is concrete. Its adjoint is a polynomial in the matrix, the one that interpolates z bar at the eigenvalues, and a square root computed two ways agrees to rounding error. And by the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction, every C-star algebra, commutative or not, is an algebra of operators.

## 5. Operators on Hilbert space

_Scene `s06_hilbert.py::HilbertOperators`_

Chapter five: operators on Hilbert space, where every operator has an adjoint.

On a Hilbert space every bounded operator has an adjoint, defined by moving it across the inner product. The matrix words all survive: self-adjoint, unitary, normal, projection. And one identity makes everything work: the norm of T star T is the square of the norm of T, because the size of T x squared is the inner product of T star T x with x. So the bounded operators form a C-star algebra, and Gelfand's theory applies to the algebra generated by any normal operator.

The simplest self-adjoint operators are the projections. Each point drops perpendicularly onto a closed subspace, its nearest point there, from Part 6. Projecting twice changes nothing, and the leftover is perpendicular, so Pythagoras splits the length. Projections will be the bricks of the spectral theorem: diagonalizing an operator will mean writing it as a sum, or an integral, of numbers times projections.

The numerical range collects the values of T x against x, for unit vectors x. For this non-normal matrix it's a convex region, traced by its supporting lines, and it contains the eigenvalues, but it's much bigger than they are. For a normal matrix, it's exactly the convex hull of the eigenvalues: T x against x is just an average of eigenvalues, weighted by the squared coordinates of x. And for a self-adjoint operator it's a real interval. Normal operators are the ones whose geometry is controlled by their spectrum.

## 7. A spectrum with no eigenvectors

_Scene `s08_noeigen.py::NoEigenvectors`_

Chapter seven: an operator with a whole interval of spectrum and not one eigenvector.

Multiply every function by x. That's a bounded self-adjoint operator on L two of zero to one, and it's quantum mechanics' position operator. Its resolvent is multiplication by one over x minus lambda, which is bounded exactly when lambda is outside the interval. So the spectrum is the whole interval from zero to one. But it has no eigenvectors: x minus lambda times f vanishing almost everywhere forces f itself to vanish almost everywhere. A full interval of spectrum, and nothing to diagonalize.

What makes 0.4 part of the spectrum, then? Take a bump centered at 0.4 and squeeze it. M minus 0.4 makes it small relative to its size, by a factor proportional to the width, so the ratio goes to zero. These are approximate eigenvectors: they show that M minus lambda can't have a bounded inverse, even though nothing is ever mapped exactly to lambda times itself.

So what can diagonalizing mean here? M is already diagonal, in a continuous sense: any function of M is just multiplication by that function. And each state psi spreads a unit of mass over the spectrum: the measure of a set omega is the integral of psi squared over omega. That's the Born rule: the probability that the particle's position lies in omega. The total is one, and the average of x is the expected position. The spectral theorem will say every self-adjoint operator looks like this.

## 8. The spectral theorem

_Scene `s09_spectral.py::SpectralTheorem`_

Chapter eight: the spectral theorem, the proof that uses every part of the series.

Diagonalizing a symmetric matrix means splitting space into eigenspaces. Here are the fifty eigenvalues of a random symmetric matrix, on its spectrum. Pick a vector x. Its squared coordinates in the eigenbasis put a mass on each eigenvalue, and the masses add up to the squared length of x. For any set omega, the projection onto the eigenvectors with eigenvalues in omega, E of omega, carries exactly the mass over omega. That assignment of a projection to every set is a projection-valued measure. And H is the integral of lambda against it. For the multiplication operator, E of omega multiplies by the indicator of omega: no eigenvectors needed.

Rudin's proof of the spectral theorem for a normal operator T uses the whole series. Take the algebra generated by T and its adjoint: commutative, and a C-star algebra. Its spectrum is compact and nonempty by Liouville, from Part 7, and its maximal ideal space is compact by Alaoglu, from Part 8. Gelfand-Naimark identifies it with the continuous functions on the spectrum, which needs Stone-Weierstrass, from Part 3. For two vectors x and y, f maps to f of T x against y: a bounded linear functional on continuous functions. By the Riesz representation theorem, from Part 5, it's integration against a measure. Those measures define the projections E of omega, in Hilbert space, from Part 6. And T is the integral of lambda d E.

One payoff. Average the powers of a unitary operator applied to a vector. In the spectral picture, that averages lambda to the k over the spectrum, which lies on the unit circle; the averages die everywhere except at lambda equal to one. So the averages converge to the projection onto the fixed vectors: von Neumann's ergodic theorem. For an eight by eight unitary with two fixed directions, the error falls like one over n.

## 9. Unbounded operators

_Scene `s10_unbounded.py::Unbounded`_

Chapter nine: unbounded operators, where the domain is part of the operator.

Momentum is a derivative, and derivatives are not bounded: sine n x has the same size for every n, but its derivative is n times bigger. And Part 8's Hellinger-Toeplitz theorem says a symmetric operator defined on all of Hilbert space is automatically bounded. So the operators of physics can't be defined everywhere. They come with a domain, a dense subspace, and the domain is part of the operator.

Here's the cautionary example. Take i times the derivative on the interval from zero to one, defined on smooth functions that vanish near both ends. Integration by parts gives a boundary term, which vanishes, so the operator is symmetric. But its adjoint has no boundary conditions at all, so it's much bigger. The equations D star f equals plus or minus i f have solutions, e to the minus x and e to the x: one dimension each. Symmetric is not self-adjoint, and only self-adjoint operators get a spectral theorem.

To make it self-adjoint, impose a boundary condition: f at one equals e to the i theta times f at zero, one condition for each point on a circle. Each gives a self-adjoint operator, with eigenfunctions e to the minus i lambda x, and the condition forces lambda into the ladder two pi n minus theta. Computed numerically for three values of theta, the eigenvalues match to many digits. Now turn theta around the circle. The whole ladder slides down, and after a full turn it lands on itself. The same differential expression, a circle of different operators, each with its own spectrum.

## 10. The Cayley transform

_Scene `s11_cayley.py::Cayley`_

Chapter ten: the Cayley transform, which turns unbounded self-adjoint operators into unitary ones.

A self-adjoint operator's spectrum lies on the real line, and for an unbounded one it runs off to infinity, like the ladder of the last chapter. The Cayley map, lambda minus i over lambda plus i, bends the real line onto the unit circle, because a real number is equally far from i and minus i. Zero goes to minus one, and both ends of the line go to one. The ladder lands on the circle, piling up at one from both sides: a bounded picture of an unbounded spectrum.

Von Neumann applied the same map to operators. If T is self-adjoint, T minus i times the inverse of T plus i is unitary, and bounded. Diagonalize that unitary with the bounded spectral theorem, then carry its spectral measure back along the inverse map, and you have T as the integral of lambda against a projection-valued measure on the real line. For merely symmetric operators the transform is only an isometry, and the dimensions it misses are the deficiency indices. Self-adjoint extensions correspond to unitary maps between those gaps: in our example, a circle of them, the circle of theta.

The momentum operator, minus i d d x on the whole line, is the leading example. Here's a packet with two components, and its Fourier transform, two bumps at the two momenta. Conjugating by the Fourier transform turns momentum into multiplication by xi, checked here to ten digits. So Plancherel's theorem from Part 9 is the spectral theorem for momentum: the Fourier transform is the change of basis that diagonalizes it. The dictionary's last card: eigenvalues become the spectrum, a whole measure's worth of them.

## 11. Stone's theorem

_Scene `s12_stone.py::Stone`_

Chapter eleven: Stone's theorem, and time evolution in quantum mechanics.

In the quantum videos, a state evolves by e to the minus i t H. Here's a packet in a harmonic well, sliding back and forth. Expand it in the eigenstates of H, with energies one half, three halves, and so on. Each coefficient is a clock hand that turns at its own rate, its energy. The sizes, the spectral measure of the state, never change: a Poisson distribution here. Computed both ways, by splitting the evolution into small steps and by the spectral decomposition, the states agree to eight digits, and the norm stays one.

Stone's theorem says this is the only way it can go. Any self-adjoint operator generates a continuous group of unitaries, e to the i t A, through its spectral measure. And conversely, every continuous one-parameter group of unitaries has a generator, the derivative at time zero, which is densely defined and self-adjoint. So if time evolution preserves probability, it's e to the minus i t H for a self-adjoint H: the assumption the quantum videos made is forced. Drop unitarity, keep contraction, and you get semigroups like the heat flow.

## 12. What this proves about quantum mechanics

_Scene `s13_quantum.py::QuantumClosure`_

Chapter twelve: what the series has proved about quantum mechanics.

Back to the hook. Hydrogen's Hamiltonian, for states without angular momentum, is this unbounded self-adjoint operator. Discretized and diagonalized, its lowest eigenvalues are minus one half over n squared, to six digits: the energy levels whose differences are the spectral lines. They pile up at zero. Above zero, the spectrum is continuous: the electron flies free. In a finite box that shows up as a dense ladder. A state's spectral measure has point masses at the bound states and a continuous part above.

The canonical commutation relation says x p minus p x is i times the identity. For matrices that's impossible: the trace of a commutator is zero, the trace of i I is not. Discretize x and p on sixty-four points and look at the eigenvalues of the commutator divided by i. Fifty-five of them sit near one, as the relation wants, and a few run off to plus and minus a hundred to make the trace zero. For bounded operators, Wielandt's argument rules it out too: commuting through powers gives n times the norm of B to the n minus one at most a fixed constant times it. So position and momentum must be unbounded: the honest reason the quantum videos needed this part.

So here's what's been proved. Observables are self-adjoint operators, so each has a spectral measure, and the Born rule is that measure. Position and momentum have continuous spectra and no eigenvectors, and their commutation relation forces them to be unbounded. Time evolution is e to the minus i t H, and by Stone's theorem it has to be. And hydrogen's spectrum is points below zero, continuum above.
